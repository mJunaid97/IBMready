    Alzheimer's disease

    Also known as: Alzheimer disease, dementia

    The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the death of neurons, beginning in the hippocampus.

    When to seek care

    Forgetting recent conversations, repeating questions, getting lost or struggling with familiar tasks over months should prompt a memory assessment; sudden confusion over hours or days is delirium and needs urgent review.

    Overview

    Alzheimer's starts years before symptoms, with abnormal proteins accumulating in the medial temporal lobes. Early disease affects the formation of new memories; over years it spreads to language, spatial skills, judgement and finally basic bodily functions. The risk rises steeply with age.

    Causes

    • Accumulation of amyloid-beta and tau proteins with loss of synapses and neurons
    • Rare inherited forms with early onset

    Risk factors

    • Age
    • APOE e4 gene
    • Family history
    • Cardiovascular risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, smoking, obesity
    • Low education, hearing loss, social isolation, depression, inactivity
    • Head injury

    Symptoms

    FatigueDizzinessWeakness

    What a clinician may find

    • Poor recall of recent events with preserved distant memory
    • Word-finding difficulty
    • Getting lost in familiar places
    • Impaired judgement and planning
    • Later: personality change, incontinence, immobility

    Complications

    • Falls
    • Infections, especially pneumonia and urinary infections
    • Malnutrition and dehydration
    • Carer strain

    Diagnosis

    History from the person and a relative, cognitive testing, blood tests to exclude thyroid, B12 and other reversible causes, and MRI showing hippocampal shrinkage. Amyloid PET or spinal fluid markers can confirm the diagnosis in specialist settings.

    Treatment

    Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil) and memantine give modest symptomatic benefit; anti-amyloid antibodies give a modest slowing of decline in a small group of people with early disease and can cause brain swelling or bleeding; whether they are available depends on national guidance. Structured routines, cognitive stimulation, treating hearing loss and depression, carer support and advance care planning matter as much.

    Prevention

    Up to 40% of dementia is attributable to modifiable factors: control blood pressure, stay physically, socially and mentally active, treat hearing loss, do not smoke, limit alcohol.

    How does Alzheimer's disease feel and progress?

    The NHS describes the symptoms of Alzheimer's disease as becoming increasingly severe as the condition progresses. The Alzheimer's Society says memory loss is usually the first and main symptom in the early stage. The NHS lists trouble finding the right word and poorer judgement as early symptoms, and changes in mood can occur too. Early symptoms also include becoming less flexible and more hesitant to try new things. In the middle stage, existing memory and thinking problems get worse, and many people find it harder to recognise family or close friends. Confusion about time and place becomes more severe, some people develop delusions or hallucinations, and help with daily tasks increases. In the later stage, the NHS lists difficulty eating and swallowing, weight loss and incontinence, which means passing urine or stools unintentionally. NHS 111 Wales says that in the severe stages people may need full-time care and help with eating, moving and using the toilet.

    What other types of dementia are there?

    Alzheimer's is the most common form of dementia, and other common types include vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies and frontotemporal dementia. The Alzheimer's Society says around 19 in 20 people with dementia have one of four main types. When someone has more than one type it is called mixed dementia, and the most common mix is Alzheimer's disease with vascular dementia. The Alzheimer's Society says mixed dementia affects at least 1 in 10 people diagnosed with dementia. Young-onset dementia is dementia that begins before the age of 65. Younger people are less likely to have memory loss as one of their first symptoms, and they are more likely to have a rarer form such as frontotemporal dementia. Mild cognitive impairment is not a type of dementia, but people with it have an increased risk of developing dementia.

    How is Alzheimer's disease assessed?

    For gradual memory problems, the GP is the usual first point of contact. A GP typically asks about symptoms, may examine the person and sets memory and thinking tasks. If the GP is unsure, the person may be referred to a specialist, such as an old age psychiatrist or a neurologist, who may be based in a memory clinic. Blood tests are mainly used to rule out other underlying causes rather than to make the diagnosis itself. According to NHS England, brain scans such as CT or MRI are not essential for a clinical diagnosis. A CT scan can check for signs of a stroke or a brain tumour, and shrinkage seen on a scan may support a diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease but cannot point to a specific diagnosis. PET scans are typically used when earlier scans are unclear. There is no single test that confirms dementia, so assessment combines the history, memory and thinking tasks and sometimes scans.

    What treatments and support are available?

    The NHS says there is no cure for Alzheimer's disease, but some medicines can temporarily ease symptoms or, for some people, slow progression. Donepezil, galantamine and rivastigmine can be prescribed for early to mid-stage disease, and memantine is used for moderate or severe disease. Non-drug options include cognitive stimulation therapy, which uses group activities and exercises to build memory and problem-solving skills. The NHS describes cognitive rehabilitation as using the parts of the brain that are working to help the parts that are not. Reminiscence and life story work involves talking about things and events from the person's past. The NHS says coping strategies are tried first for aggression, and if they fail a consultant psychiatrist can prescribe risperidone for persistent aggression or extreme distress, using it for the shortest time possible because of its serious side effects. The Alzheimer's Society says lecanemab and donanemab are approved for use in the UK but not on the NHS. NICE's final draft guidance said their benefits remain too small to justify the additional costs to the NHS, so the NICE website has the current position.

    Living with Alzheimer's disease and caring for someone

    NHS inform suggests keeping in touch with people and joining activities, and notes that dementia-friendly groups, communities, sport and cultural activities exist for people with dementia. It says a daily routine can encourage feelings of security, value and worth. Suggested memory aids around the home include pictures on cupboard doors showing what is inside, and technology such as a device that plays a recorded reminder when the front door is opened. Local councils provide information about aids and adaptations for the home, and memory services can offer memory aids and strategies. Depression is common in dementia, and NHS inform says a GP can talk through talking treatments that can help. A care needs assessment can be arranged through social services, and carers can ask the local council for a carer's assessment. NHS inform describes advance care planning as thinking ahead about which treatments a person would want, including whether they would want certain end-of-life treatments.

    What are the complications and the outlook?

    The Alzheimer's Society says the middle stage of dementia is often the longest. In the later stage many people have trouble eating or swallowing, which can lead to choking, chest infections and other problems. Delirium is a sudden change in mental state, where the person seems not themselves, and it is different from the slow worsening of dementia. The Alzheimer's Society says dementia can make a person much more likely to have delirium. Urinary tract infections can cause sudden and severe confusion in someone with memory problems or dementia, and confusion can also be a sign of a stroke or sepsis. NHS services such as South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust advise going to A&E or calling 999 if someone suddenly becomes confused. The Alzheimer's Society advises making an urgent GP appointment or calling NHS 111 for a sudden change in someone with dementia. Once the cause is treated, symptoms usually improve within a few days for many people, though some have memory and thinking problems for longer.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is Alzheimer's disease the same as dementia?

    Alzheimer's disease is the most common form of dementia, but it is not the only one. The Alzheimer's Society says around 19 in 20 people with dementia have one of four main types. Some people have more than one type, which is called mixed dementia, and the most common mix is Alzheimer's disease with vascular dementia. Young-onset dementia is dementia that begins before the age of 65.

    Is there a cure for Alzheimer's disease?

    The NHS says there is no cure, but some medicines can temporarily ease symptoms or, for some people, slow progression. Non-drug approaches such as cognitive stimulation therapy and reminiscence work are also used. A care needs assessment can be arranged through social services, and carers can ask the local council for a carer's assessment.

    Will I need a brain scan?

    According to NHS England, brain scans such as CT or MRI are not essential for a clinical diagnosis. A CT scan can check for signs of a stroke or a brain tumour, and shrinkage seen on a scan may support a diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease. PET scans are typically used when earlier scans are unclear.

    Why might someone with dementia suddenly become much more confused?

    A sudden change is different from the slow worsening of dementia and may be delirium. Urinary tract infections are one cause of sudden and severe confusion in someone with dementia. NHS services advise going to A&E or calling 999 for sudden confusion, and the Alzheimer's Society advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a sudden change in someone with dementia.

    Do memory problems always mean dementia?

    Memory problems are not always caused by dementia, so the GP may look for other causes. A GP typically asks about symptoms, may examine the person and sets memory and thinking tasks. Blood tests are mainly used to rule out other underlying causes rather than to make the diagnosis itself. If the GP is unsure, the person may be referred to a specialist, who may be based in a memory clinic.

    Conditions

    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Medications

    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

    Drug classes

    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
