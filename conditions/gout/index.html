    Gout

    Also known as: gouty arthritis, podagra

    An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.

    When to seek care

    Same-day assessment for a hot swollen joint with fever, since septic arthritis looks identical and is an emergency. Joint fluid is tested for infection as well as crystals because septic arthritis can mimic gout.

    Overview

    Uric acid is a waste product of purine metabolism. When its level exceeds saturation it crystallises in cool joints, classically the big toe, and the crystals provoke a sudden, exquisitely painful attack. Repeated attacks damage joints and form lumps (tophi). Lowering urate with medication dissolves the crystals and prevents attacks. Urate crystals can sit in a joint for a long time without causing symptoms; a flare is set off when something disturbs them, such as an infection, a joint injury, surgery, heavy drinking or certain foods. Flares typically bring intense pain, swelling and redness in one or more joints, last a week or two and then settle, and favour the joints furthest from the trunk: the toes, ankles, knees and fingers. Gout is several times commoner in men than in women. With early diagnosis, treatment and lifestyle changes, NIAMS describes it as one of the most controllable forms of arthritis.

    Causes

    • Reduced kidney excretion of urate (90%)
    • Overproduction: high purine diet, cell breakdown, rare enzyme defects

    Risk factors

    • Male sex, age
    • Obesity, metabolic syndrome
    • Alcohol (beer and spirits), sugary drinks, red meat and shellfish
    • Diuretics and low-dose aspirin
    • Chronic kidney disease
    • Family history
    • Acute illness, surgery or joint injury as triggers
    • Fructose-sweetened drinks

    Symptoms

    Joint painSwelling (oedema)Knee painFever

    What a clinician may find

    • A red, hot, shiny, extremely tender joint, often the first metatarsophalangeal joint
    • Tophi on the ears, fingers and elbows in chronic gout
    • A flare that settles over a week or two, leaving a normal-looking joint between attacks

    Complications

    • Joint damage and tophi
    • Kidney stones
    • Association with heart disease and kidney disease

    Diagnosis

    Crystals seen in joint fluid confirm it; a raised serum urate supports it (but may be normal during an attack); ultrasound or dual-energy CT shows crystal deposits. Infection must be excluded in a single hot joint. Gout is often diagnosed clinically from the history and examination. Aspirating fluid from the joint or bursa is the gold standard: the fluid is examined for urate crystals and tested for infection to exclude septic arthritis. Serum urate has limits: some people with gout have normal levels and many with high levels never develop gout, so clinicians may wait some weeks after a flare before measuring it.

    Treatment

    Attacks: NSAIDs, colchicine or steroids, started early; rest and ice. Prevention: allopurinol (or febuxostat) started after the attack settles and titrated to a target urate, with cover against flares for the first months; weight loss and diet changes. Treatment has two aims: easing the flare and preventing future flares and joint damage. Flares are treated with an NSAID such as ibuprofen, colchicine, or oral or injected corticosteroids, with paracetamol for additional pain relief. Where a medicine that raises urate is contributing, the prescriber may change it. Urate-lowering therapy is continued long term, because dissolving the crystals is what prevents further attacks and tophi; lifestyle changes such as losing weight, limiting alcohol and avoiding purine-rich foods support it.

    Healthy weight, limit alcohol and sugary drinks, moderate red meat and seafood, stay hydrated, review diuretics.

    What does a gout flare feel like?

    Gout is an inflammatory arthritis caused by urate crystals in and around joints, and it causes sudden flares of severe pain and swelling. The big toe is the most common site, and the ankles, knees, hands and fingers can also be affected. Left untreated, an attack usually lasts about one to two weeks. If gout is not treated, future attacks may last even longer.

    Further flares are common. StatPearls reports that around six in ten people have a second attack within a year, and the NHS notes that flares can come back every few months or after years. Without treatment, attacks tend to come more often and can affect more joints. Hard lumps of urate called tophi can also form under the skin, usually around the ears, fingers and elbows.

    How is gout diagnosed?

    A blood test for uric acid may be done, but the level in the blood often falls during a flare. Because of this, the test may be repeated some weeks after the flare has settled. A normal result during a flare does not rule gout out, and a raised urate level on its own, without joint symptoms, is not a diagnosis of gout.

    When the diagnosis is uncertain, fluid can be taken from the joint and looked at under a microscope for crystals, which is regarded as the reference standard. The fluid can also be tested for infection, because gout and septic arthritis can occur together. An ultrasound or a special CT scan can also check for crystal build-up in the joint if fluid cannot be taken or the diagnosis is still unclear.

    How is a gout flare treated?

    Treatment has two parts: easing a flare, and preventing future flares. NICE recommends an NSAID (an anti-inflammatory painkiller), colchicine or a short course of steroid tablets as first-line treatment for a flare, depending on the person's other conditions, medicines and preferences. NICE found no strong evidence that any one of these works better than the others when used first.

    A steroid injection into the joint or a muscle is considered if NSAIDs and colchicine cannot be used, are not tolerated or do not work. NIAMS notes that colchicine works best when started as soon as symptoms appear. NHS medicines information says it can take a day or two before pain and inflammation start to get better with colchicine. Ice packs can also ease symptoms, according to NHS inform.

    How are future flares prevented?

    NICE describes gout as a lifelong condition that benefits from long-term urate-lowering treatment, which aims to clear the urate crystals and prevent flares. NICE says this treatment can also shrink tophi. NIAMS says a main goal of treatment is to prevent and resolve tophi so that joint damage stops.

    Flares can increase when urate-lowering medicine is first started, so doctors may give another medicine alongside it for the first months. NICE recommends starting allopurinol low and increasing it slowly according to blood urate measurements. NIAMS says lifestyle changes alone are not enough when flares are frequent or tophi are present, so medicine is the main approach. The NHS says lifestyle changes can still help reduce future flares.

    What diet and lifestyle changes help?

    Versus Arthritis says heavy drinking, especially of beer and spirits, can raise urate levels and trigger attacks. It says beer is particularly bad because it contains a lot of purines, and that small amounts of wine do not appear to raise the risk. It advises losing weight gradually, and says extreme weight loss or starvation diets are not recommended because they can raise urate levels. The NHS also advises avoiding crash diets and lists dehydration as a possible trigger of an attack.

    An NHS dietetic leaflet says regularly drinking sugar-sweetened drinks can increase the risk of gout, while sugar-free fizzy drinks do not. Versus Arthritis lists a daily glass of skimmed milk among its management tips. NHS inform notes that vitamin C supplements are not suitable for everyone and recommends talking to a GP before using them.

    What are the complications and the outlook?

    Leaving gout untreated may cause lasting damage to the joints, and the NHS notes that repeated attacks can damage joints and cause kidney stones. Severe gout pain can lead to feelings of depression or anxiety, according to NHS inform.

    The NHS advises a GP appointment for a first episode of gout symptoms, or when usual treatments are not helping. For a painful swollen joint that is getting worse, especially with a very high temperature, feeling hot or shivery, or feeling sick or unable to eat, the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111. These can be signs of an infection inside the joint, called septic arthritis. Gout can look the same as septic arthritis, which is why fluid from the joint may be tested for infection.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long does a gout attack last?

    Left untreated, a gout attack usually lasts about one to two weeks, and later attacks may last even longer. The NHS notes that flares can come back every few months or after years. NHS medicines information says colchicine can take a day or two before pain and inflammation start to get better.

    Does gout go away on its own?

    Without treatment, a flare usually lasts about one to two weeks, according to the NHS, but further flares are common. StatPearls reports that around six in ten people have a second attack within a year. NICE describes gout as a lifelong condition that benefits from long-term urate-lowering treatment to prevent flares. The NHS advises seeing a GP when usual treatments are not helping.

    Should I have a uric acid blood test during a flare?

    The level of urate in the blood often falls during a flare, so a test then can be misleading. The test may be repeated some weeks after the flare has settled. A normal result during a flare does not rule gout out, and a raised level on its own, without joint symptoms, is not a diagnosis of gout.

    Is beer worse than wine for gout?

    Versus Arthritis says heavy drinking, especially of beer and spirits, can raise urate levels and trigger attacks. It says beer is particularly bad because it contains a lot of purines, while small amounts of wine do not appear to raise the risk. An Oxford Kidney Unit leaflet advises people prone to gout attacks to reduce alcohol to a minimum or avoid it.

    When should I get urgent help for a swollen joint?

    For a painful swollen joint that is getting worse, especially with a very high temperature or feeling sick or unable to eat, the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111. These can be signs of an infection inside the joint, called septic arthritis. The NHS advises a GP appointment for a first episode of gout symptoms.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Naproxen Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.

    Guides and news

    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Knee pain: causes and when to get help Knee pain can often be treated at home, and NHS 111 is the route for a badly swollen knee or a hot, red knee with a high temperature.
