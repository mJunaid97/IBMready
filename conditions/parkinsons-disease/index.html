    Parkinson's disease

    Also known as: Parkinson disease, PD

    A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.

    When to seek care

    See a doctor for a new tremor at rest, slowing of movement or a change in gait; urgent help for falls with injury, inability to swallow, or sudden confusion.

    Overview

    The basal ganglia need dopamine to release intended movements smoothly. As nigral neurons die, movements become slow and small, muscles stiff, and a resting tremor appears, typically starting on one side. Non-motor features such as loss of smell, constipation, sleep disturbance and later cognitive change are part of the disease. NINDS describes Parkinson's as a chronic, progressive neurological disorder characterised by resting tremor, muscle rigidity, slowness of movement, impaired balance and a shuffling gait. The root causes remain unknown; age, genetics and exposure to environmental hazards are the recognised risk factors. Under the microscope, unusual protein clumps called Lewy bodies form in brain cells. Sleep disorders, constipation and a reduced sense of smell can develop years or even decades before movement problems, and the classical motor features appear only once most of the dopamine neurons in the substantia nigra have already been lost, which is why the diagnosis is often made late.

    Causes

    • Accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein protein in neurons; the trigger is unknown in most cases
    • Genetic forms in a minority
    • Rare toxin exposure

    Risk factors

    • Age over 60
    • Male sex
    • Family history
    • Pesticide exposure
    • Head injury
    • REM sleep behaviour disorder
    • Constipation and a history of anxiety or depression as prodromal features
    • Rural living, farming and well-water drinking

    Symptoms

    WeaknessFatigueDizziness

    What a clinician may find

    • Resting tremor (pill-rolling)
    • Bradykinesia: slow, small movements
    • Rigidity (cogwheeling)
    • Stooped posture, shuffling gait, reduced arm swing
    • Reduced facial expression, soft voice, small handwriting

    Complications

    • Falls and fractures
    • Swallowing difficulty and pneumonia
    • Dementia and hallucinations
    • Depression
    • Motor fluctuations from long-term levodopa
    • Orthostatic hypotension and urinary incontinence
    • Psychosis as a side effect of levodopa in some patients

    Diagnosis

    Clinical, by a neurologist, on the combination of bradykinesia with tremor or rigidity and a response to levodopa. MRI excludes other causes; a DaT scan can confirm dopamine loss when uncertain. Early diagnosis is difficult because early symptoms are often misread as the effects of normal ageing. A large and sustained response to levodopa strongly supports Parkinson's disease over secondary or atypical parkinsonism.

    Treatment

    Levodopa remains the most effective drug; dopamine agonists, MAO-B and COMT inhibitors extend its effect. Physiotherapy, exercise, speech and occupational therapy maintain function. Deep brain stimulation helps selected patients with fluctuations. Levodopa, taken as a tablet or liquid, is combined with benserazide or carbidopa so that it is not broken down in the bloodstream before it reaches the brain. Long-term use is linked to involuntary jerky movements (dyskinesias), 'on-off' effects and wearing-off before the next dose is due, and in some people to psychiatric effects including psychosis. In milder disease clinicians may start with dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors or amantadine to delay levodopa; COMT inhibitors extend its effect. Deep brain stimulation is introduced when medication no longer controls symptoms.

    Prevention

    No proven prevention; regular exercise and coffee are linked to lower risk and exercise slows decline. Caffeine and physical activity appear protective in observational studies, although reverse causation is possible because early disease itself reduces how much people exercise. Smoking is not recommended: the lower rate seen in smokers in these studies does not mean that smoking prevents Parkinson's disease.

    What do the symptoms feel like, and how does Parkinson's progress?

    Parkinson's disease usually develops gradually, and the symptoms are mild at first. The NHS lists three main movement symptoms: tremor, slowed movement and muscle stiffness. The tremor is shaking that typically starts in a hand or arm and is most noticeable when the limb is at rest. Slowed movement is called bradykinesia, and it can lead to a small-stepped, shuffling walk. Slowed movement can make everyday tasks difficult. Stiffness, also called rigidity, can make movement and facial expression harder and sometimes causes painful cramps. The order in which symptoms develop and how severe they are differs for each person. Symptoms generally worsen over time, and the speed of progression varies widely between people.

    What other symptoms can happen?

    Parkinson's also brings symptoms that are not about movement. The NHS and NHS inform list balance problems, a reduced sense of smell, nerve pain, bladder problems, constipation, sweating, swallowing difficulties, and changes in mood or memory. Balance problems can make a fall more likely, and a fall can lead to injury. A loss of the sense of smell sometimes occurs several years before other symptoms develop. The NHS also lists problems sleeping (insomnia) among the symptoms that are not about movement. NHS 111 Wales lists depression, anxiety and dementia among the cognitive and psychiatric symptoms. A review of advanced Parkinson's disease says that late-stage patients develop motor and non-motor complications that can greatly affect quality of life.

    How is Parkinson's disease diagnosed?

    The NHS says that no test can conclusively show that someone has Parkinson's disease. A doctor bases the diagnosis on the symptoms, the medical history and a detailed physical examination. If Parkinson's is suspected, the GP refers the person to a specialist, such as a neurologist, for a formal diagnosis. Blood tests and brain scans may be used to rule out other conditions. A DaT scan is a brain scan, done in a nuclear medicine department, that looks at dopamine transporters in the brain. It can help tell Parkinson's apart from conditions such as essential tremor, and NICE recommends DaT scans only in selected cases. For the scan, a tracer is injected into a vein and the person lies still, and the report goes to the referring doctor, who contacts the person to discuss the results.

    What treatments are used?

    There is currently no cure for Parkinson's disease, but treatments are available to help reduce the main symptoms and maintain quality of life. Treatment can include medicine, therapies such as physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy, and sometimes surgery. Most people with Parkinson's eventually need a medicine called levodopa. The NHS says levodopa usually improves movement problems. MAO-B inhibitors are another option in early Parkinson's disease and give less improvement in movement symptoms than levodopa. When tablets no longer give enough control, some people are considered for levodopa gel delivered through a tube into the small intestine. Another device-aided option is a continuous infusion of apomorphine under the skin. Deep brain stimulation is a type of surgery that does not cure Parkinson's disease but can ease symptoms for some people. It involves implanting a device, sometimes called a brain pacemaker, with leads placed in the brain by stereotactic surgery. It is considered for people whose symptoms are no longer well controlled by oral medicines.

    Living with Parkinson's disease

    Regular exercise can help relieve muscle stiffness, lift mood and reduce stress. Many people with Parkinson's have problems with speech and swallowing. A speech and language therapist can often help by teaching speaking and swallowing exercises. An occupational therapist can pinpoint everyday tasks that are hard, such as dressing or getting to the shops. Occupational therapists can also provide equipment and strategies that help people stay independent and safe at home. A physiotherapist can work on muscle stiffness and joint pain through movement and exercise. Parkinson's nurse specialists can help arrange referrals to physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy. As the condition advances, it can become increasingly difficult to carry out everyday activities without help.

    What is the outlook?

    NHS 111 Wales says that Parkinson's disease does not directly cause people to die, but it can leave them more vulnerable to serious infections. NHS 111 Wales says that most people with Parkinson's now have a normal or near-normal life expectancy. One single-centre study of hospital admissions found that respiratory infection was the most common cause, at 23% of admissions. A review of palliative care in advanced Parkinson's disease lists aspiration pneumonia, urinary tract infections, complications from falls and fractures, and sepsis from pressure ulcers as the main risks. A separate Rotterdam Study analysis found reduced life expectancy in people diagnosed with parkinsonism, mainly when diagnosis came before age 70. The outlook varies from person to person.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is there a cure for Parkinson's disease?

    There is currently no cure for Parkinson's disease. Treatments are available to help reduce the main symptoms and maintain quality of life, and they include medicine, physiotherapy and occupational therapy. For some people surgery called deep brain stimulation can ease symptoms, although it does not cure the condition.

    Can a test confirm Parkinson's disease?

    The NHS says that no test can conclusively show that someone has Parkinson's disease. A doctor bases the diagnosis on symptoms, medical history and a detailed examination. Blood tests and brain scans may be used to rule out other conditions, and a DaT scan, which looks at dopamine transporters in the brain, may be used when the diagnosis is unclear.

    Does Parkinson's disease shorten life?

    NHS 111 Wales says that most people with Parkinson's now have a normal or near-normal life expectancy. NHS 111 Wales says the condition does not directly cause death, but it can leave people more vulnerable to serious infections. A Rotterdam Study analysis found reduced life expectancy in people diagnosed with parkinsonism, mainly when diagnosis came before age 70.

    Does Parkinson's disease only affect movement?

    No, because people can also have balance problems, a reduced sense of smell, nerve pain, bladder problems, constipation, sweating, swallowing difficulties, and changes in mood or memory. The order in which symptoms develop and how severe they are differs for each person. A reduced sense of smell sometimes appears several years before other symptoms.

    Who can help with everyday difficulties?

    A physiotherapist can work on muscle stiffness and joint pain through movement and exercise. An occupational therapist can pinpoint everyday tasks that are hard, such as dressing or getting to the shops. A speech and language therapist can often help by teaching speaking and swallowing exercises. Parkinson's nurse specialists can help arrange referrals to these therapies.
