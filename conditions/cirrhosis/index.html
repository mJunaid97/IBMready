    Cirrhosis

    Also known as: liver cirrhosis

    Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and impair function.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for vomiting blood, black stools, confusion or drowsiness, a rapidly swelling abdomen with fever, or deepening jaundice. MedlinePlus advises emergency help for sudden abdominal or chest pain in someone with cirrhosis, and prompt contact with a clinician for any new symptom suggesting the disease.

    Overview

    Years of injury from alcohol, fatty liver disease or viral hepatitis replace liver tissue with scar. The result is failure of the liver's jobs (jaundice, bleeding, confusion) and high pressure in the portal vein (varices, fluid in the abdomen, an enlarged spleen). Stopping or treating the cause can slow or halt further scarring and may allow some recovery, but complications and the risk of liver cancer can persist, so follow-up continues. Cirrhosis is the final stage of chronic liver disease: scar tissue replaces healthy tissue and stops the liver working normally. Early on it often causes nothing noticeable, and it is sometimes found by chance on an abdominal ultrasound or on blood tests taken for another reason. As it progresses people report fatigue, itchy skin, poor appetite, unintended weight loss and swelling of the legs and ankles, then fluid in the abdomen (ascites) and yellowing of the eyes and skin (jaundice). Hepatic encephalopathy, confusion caused by the failing liver no longer clearing toxins, can progress to unconsciousness. Some people have more than one cause at the same time, for example alcohol and fatty liver disease.

    Causes

    • Alcohol
    • Metabolic dysfunction-associated (fatty) liver disease
    • Hepatitis B and C
    • Autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis
    • Haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
    • Some medicines (drug-induced liver injury)
    • More than one cause acting together

    Risk factors

    • Heavy drinking
    • Obesity and type 2 diabetes
    • Unprotected sex or injected drug use (viral hepatitis)
    • Family history of liver disease

    Symptoms

    FatigueSwelling (oedema)NauseaAbdominal pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Jaundice, spider naevi, red palms
    • Enlarged spleen, ascites
    • Muscle wasting, bruising
    • Confusion and a flapping tremor (encephalopathy)
    • Oedema of the legs and ankles
    • Itching and scratch marks

    Complications

    • Bleeding oesophageal varices
    • Ascites and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis
    • Hepatic encephalopathy
    • Liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma)
    • Kidney failure (hepatorenal syndrome)
    • Malnutrition, weight loss and muscle wasting

    Diagnosis

    Liver function tests, clotting and platelet count; ultrasound or elastography (FibroScan) measuring stiffness; tests for the cause (hepatitis viruses, iron, autoantibodies); endoscopy for varices; six-monthly ultrasound for cancer surveillance. The diagnosis rests on history, examination and test results together: blood tests show liver damage, reduced synthetic function and infection; imaging shows a nodular liver, an enlarged spleen and ascites; elastography measures stiffness without a needle, so liver biopsy is reserved for cases where the cause or stage remains uncertain.

    Treatment

    Remove the cause: complete abstinence from alcohol, weight loss, antivirals for hepatitis B and C. Diuretics for ascites, beta blockers or banding for varices, lactulose for encephalopathy, nutrition, vaccination. Liver transplantation for decompensated disease. No treatment reverses established cirrhosis, but treating the disease that caused it may stop it getting worse and prevent liver failure: antiviral medicines for hepatitis B and C, steroid medicines for autoimmune hepatitis, abstinence for alcohol-related disease and weight loss for fatty liver disease. Complications are managed as they arise with medicines for abdominal and leg swelling, for encephalopathy and for dilated oesophageal veins, and with surveillance imaging for liver cancer. Vaccination protects against hepatitis B, one of the leading causes. Liver transplantation is considered for advanced, decompensated disease.

    Prevention

    Drink within recommended limits or not at all, keep a healthy weight, vaccinate against hepatitis B, never share needles, treat hepatitis C. Hepatitis B vaccination prevents one of the leading causes outright.

    Conditions

    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Fatty liver disease (MASLD) A common condition in which fat builds up in the liver, often linked to being overweight.
    • Hepatitis C Hepatitis C is an infection with a virus that causes inflammation of the liver, which can damage the liver over time.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Hepatitis B tests A set of blood tests that shows whether someone has hepatitis B now, had it in the past, is immune from vaccination, or carries the virus long term.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) A membrane enzyme of bile-duct and liver cells that rises with cholestasis, alcohol and many medicines.
    • INR The prothrombin time expressed as a standardised ratio, the number used to dose warfarin and to gauge the liver's production of clotting factors.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Lactulose Lactulose is a laxative used to treat constipation, and it is also used for hepatic encephalopathy, a condition caused by severe liver disease.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.

    Drug classes

    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
    • Osmotic laxatives Osmotic laxatives draw water into the bowel to soften poo; lactulose is the one covered on this site.

    Guides and news

    • Alcohol units: the UK low-risk drinking advice The UK Chief Medical Officers advise adults not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.
    • Liver blood tests: what the results mean Liver blood tests can point towards a possible problem but do not name the cause, and results that are not normal are very common.
    • Swollen ankles and legs: causes and red flags Swollen ankles and legs often come from sitting or standing for long periods, but can signal a clot or a heart, kidney or liver problem.
