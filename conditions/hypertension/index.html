    High blood pressure (hypertension)

    Also known as: hypertension, raised blood pressure

    Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).

    When to seek care

    A reading of 180/120 or higher with headache, chest pain, breathlessness, vision change, confusion, weakness or difficulty speaking needs emergency care: call 999 or go to A&E. A reading this high without symptoms needs urgent medical advice from a GP or NHS 111.

    Overview

    High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms but silently damages arteries, heart, brain, kidneys and eyes over years. It affects around one adult in three and is the single biggest contributor to stroke and heart disease worldwide. Treatment is cheap and effective, yet half of those affected do not know they have it.

    Causes

    • Primary (essential) hypertension in over 90%: genes, salt, weight, alcohol, inactivity, age
    • Secondary causes in the rest: kidney disease, adrenal tumours, narrowed renal arteries, sleep apnoea, some drugs (NSAIDs, steroids, the pill), pregnancy

    Risk factors

    • Family history
    • High salt intake
    • Obesity
    • Alcohol
    • Inactivity
    • Age
    • Black African or Caribbean ancestry

    Symptoms

    HeadacheDizziness

    What a clinician may find

    • Usually none; very high pressure can show retinal changes, an enlarged heart or kidney damage

    Complications

    • Stroke
    • Heart attack and heart failure
    • Chronic kidney disease
    • Aortic aneurysm and dissection
    • Retinopathy and vision loss
    • Vascular dementia

    Diagnosis

    Repeated readings in clinic confirmed by home or 24-hour ambulatory monitoring. Blood and urine tests, an ECG and eye examination look for organ damage and secondary causes.

    Kidney function testsElectrolytesUrinalysisUrine proteinBlood glucoseLipid profileElectrocardiogram
    Ultrasound

    Treatment

    Lifestyle first: less salt, weight loss, exercise, less alcohol. Drugs when readings stay high or risk is raised: ACE inhibitors or ARBs, calcium-channel blockers, thiazide-like diuretics, added stepwise; most people need two or more.

    RamiprilAmlodipineFurosemideMetoprolol and beta blockers

    Prevention

    A diet rich in fruit, vegetables and low in salt, regular activity, healthy weight, moderate alcohol and no smoking.

    Does high blood pressure cause symptoms?

    The NHS says high blood pressure usually causes no symptoms, so the only way to find out is to have it measured. Rarely, high blood pressure can cause headaches, blurred vision or chest pain, the NHS says. NHS inform lists headaches, blurred or double vision, regular nosebleeds and shortness of breath as symptoms of very high blood pressure and says to contact a GP practice if they occur.

    For urgent advice, the NHS names NHS 111 for frequent headaches, blurred vision, or chest pain that comes and goes. It says to call 999 for chest pain that does not go away, or chest pain with sweating, sickness, light-headedness or breathlessness, as this could be a heart attack.

    What are the types and causes of high blood pressure?

    MedlinePlus describes primary, or essential, high blood pressure as the most common type, and says it usually develops gradually with age. Secondary high blood pressure is caused by another medical condition or by the use of certain medicines.

    MedlinePlus lists chronic kidney disease, disorders of the adrenal gland and pregnancy among the causes of secondary high blood pressure. An NHLBI news item from the US names antidepressants, decongestants, hormonal birth control pills and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen as medicines linked to high blood pressure.

    White coat hypertension is the name for readings that run high in the clinic but stay normal outside it. Home or 24-hour monitoring can show whether this is happening.

    How is high blood pressure checked and diagnosed?

    A blood pressure test usually takes a couple of minutes, according to the NHS. A cuff on the upper arm inflates and then releases. The test may be done a couple of times to make sure the measurement is accurate. The result has two numbers. The top, or systolic, figure is the pressure as the heart pumps. The bottom, or diastolic, figure is the pressure as it rests between beats.

    When a clinic reading is high, NICE recommends 24-hour monitoring to confirm the diagnosis, or home monitoring if 24-hour monitoring is declined or not tolerated. The NHS says a doctor or nurse may give a home monitor for use at home.

    NICE says investigations for target organ damage should be started while the diagnosis is being confirmed, and that drug treatment may be considered straight away if damage is found.

    The NHS says tests are available at pharmacies, GP surgeries and some workplaces.

    How is high blood pressure treated?

    The NHS says lifestyle changes come first. If those are not enough, or if the risk of other problems is high, medicines are added. Medicine groups named by the NHS and the British Heart Foundation include ACE inhibitors, angiotensin-2 receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics and beta-blockers. The NHS says more than one medicine may be needed to keep blood pressure under control.

    Which medicine is offered first depends on age, ethnic background and whether the person has type 2 diabetes. The NHS says beta-blockers are now mainly used when other treatments have not worked.

    Blood Pressure UK says dizziness and tiredness are the most commonly reported side effects of blood pressure tablets, and that they often improve or go away as the body adjusts. NHS inform says some people have to take medicine to treat high blood pressure for the rest of their life. The NHS says not to stop taking the medicine if side effects occur, and Blood Pressure UK says that stopping it on its own lets blood pressure go back up.

    What can help lower blood pressure day to day?

    The NHS describes a healthy, balanced diet, regular activity, weight loss for people who are overweight, cutting back on alcohol and caffeine, and not smoking. Several NHS leaflets say that losing weight helps to lower blood pressure. NHS trust leaflets say that cutting down on salt, ready meals and takeaways helps. One NHS trust leaflet says salt substitutes made from potassium chloride are not suitable for everyone, for example people with kidney problems or heart failure, or those who take certain blood pressure medicines.

    The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) advises continuing the treatment plan, having regular follow-up, and learning to monitor blood pressure at home. It adds that the clinician may change or add medicines over time.

    What are the complications of high blood pressure?

    MedlinePlus describes high blood pressure as raising the risk of heart disease and stroke. NHS inform names stroke, heart attack, kidney disease and vascular dementia among the problems that untreated high blood pressure can cause. The British Heart Foundation says high blood pressure can lead to complications such as kidney failure, heart failure and vascular dementia.

    MedlinePlus says hypertensive heart disease develops because the heart must work harder against the pressure, which over time thickens the heart muscle. A thickened muscle may not get enough oxygen, which can cause chest pain, and without good blood pressure control the heart muscle can weaken and heart failure may develop.

    MedlinePlus says the aim of treatment is to lower blood pressure to reduce the risk of related health problems, and that medicines and lifestyle changes can often control it.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is high blood pressure for life?

    NHS inform says some people have to take medicine to treat high blood pressure for the rest of their life. The NHS describes lifestyle changes as the first step, with medicines added if these are not enough or if the risk of other problems is high.

    Can blood pressure tablets be stopped if a person feels well?

    Blood Pressure UK says that stopping medicine on its own lets blood pressure go back up, and the US CDC says to talk to the health care team or a pharmacist before stopping blood pressure medicine. The NHS says not to stop taking the medicine if side effects occur.

    What is white coat hypertension?

    White coat hypertension is the name for readings that run high in the clinic but stay normal outside it. Home or 24-hour monitoring can show whether this is happening. An NHS service specification describes 24-hour monitoring as the most accurate method for confirming a diagnosis of high blood pressure, and says it can reduce unnecessary treatment.

    Can blood pressure medicines affect pregnancy?

    The NHS says some blood pressure medicines may not be suitable in pregnancy, because they can reduce blood flow through the placenta to the baby or affect the baby in other ways. The US NHLBI says women who have high blood pressure during pregnancy are more likely to have high blood pressure later in life.

    When does high blood pressure need urgent help?

    The NHS says to contact NHS 111 for frequent headaches or blurred vision, or chest pain that comes and goes. It says to call 999 for chest pain that does not go away, or chest pain with sweating, sickness, light-headedness or breathlessness.

    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    • Abdominal aortic aneurysm An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the aorta, the body's main blood vessel, where it runs through the tummy.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Erectile dysfunction Erectile dysfunction, also called impotence, is being unable to get or keep an erection.

    • Peripheral arterial disease A long-term condition in which the arteries that supply blood to the legs become narrowed or blocked.

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Androgens Steroid hormones that act on the androgen receptor.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Calcium channel blockers Medicines that relax the muscle in artery walls (and, for some members.
    • Combined hormonal contraceptives Combined hormonal contraceptives contain both an oestrogen and a progestogen, and include the combined pill, a skin patch and a vaginal ring.
    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
