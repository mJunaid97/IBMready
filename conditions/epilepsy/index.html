    Epilepsy

    Also known as: seizure disorder, fits

    A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    When to seek care

    Call emergency services for a seizure lasting more than five minutes, repeated seizures without recovery, a first seizure, injury, or difficulty breathing afterwards.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Overview

    Seizures take many forms depending on where they start: a focal seizure may cause twitching of one hand, an odd smell or a period of unresponsive staring; a generalised tonic-clonic seizure involves loss of consciousness, stiffening and jerking of the whole body. Most people with epilepsy become seizure-free on medication.

    Causes

    • Often unknown or genetic
    • Scarring from stroke, head injury, infection or birth injury
    • Brain tumours and malformations
    • Hippocampal sclerosis
    • Dementia in older people

    Risk factors

    • Family history
    • Previous brain injury or infection
    • Febrile seizures in childhood
    • Stroke

    Symptoms

    DizzinessWeaknessHeadacheFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Usually normal between seizures
    • Post-seizure confusion, bitten tongue, incontinence

    Complications

    • Injury during seizures
    • Status epilepticus (a seizure over five minutes: an emergency)
    • Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP)
    • Driving and employment restrictions
    • Depression

    Diagnosis

    A witness account or video is the most useful evidence. An EEG may show epileptic discharges; MRI looks for a structural cause; blood tests exclude metabolic causes and an ECG excludes a cardiac cause of collapse.

    Treatment

    Anti-seizure medication chosen by seizure type (for example levetiracetam and lamotrigine; valproate is avoided in girls and women who could become pregnant because of the risk to an unborn baby), taken consistently. Surgery, laser ablation or nerve stimulation for drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Seizure first aid education for family.

    Prevention

    Take medication regularly, sleep well, limit alcohol, avoid known triggers; helmets and safe driving reduce head injuries that cause acquired epilepsy.

    What are the main types of seizure and what do they feel like?

    Epilepsy seizures are grouped by where they start in the brain and how much of it is involved. Focal seizures start in one part of the brain, and some people stay aware during them while for others awareness is affected. Generalised seizures start in, or very quickly affect, both sides of the brain.

    Some people notice a warning before a seizure, called an aura. Epilepsy Action says an aura is a type of focal seizure in its own right. Examples include a rising feeling in the tummy, unusual smells or tastes, and tingling in the arms and legs.

    In a tonic-clonic seizure the person loses consciousness and the muscles go stiff, so someone who is standing may fall. This is followed by repeated jerking of the arms or legs. A seizure that begins on one side of the brain can also spread to both sides, which is called a focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.

    How is epilepsy diagnosed?

    Epilepsy Action advises seeing a GP as soon as possible after a suspected seizure, even if the person did not go to hospital at the time. NICE says people with a suspected first seizure should be referred urgently so that a specialist is involved early in diagnosing epilepsy, and its quality standard aims for them to be seen within 2 weeks. Epilepsy Action says anyone with a suspected seizure should have an ECG, a heart tracing, to rule out a heart problem.

    No single test can confirm epilepsy. An EEG may be requested, and if it is done after a first seizure it should be done as soon as possible. A normal EEG does not rule epilepsy out, because many people with epilepsy do not show abnormal activity between seizures. NHS inform says a diagnosis can still be made from the person's description of their seizures even when tests show nothing. NICE recommends an MRI scan for most people who are diagnosed with epilepsy. An MRI scan cannot decide on its own whether someone has epilepsy and is useful alongside other information.

    Epilepsy Action advises keeping a seizure diary and taking it to appointments.

    How is epilepsy treated?

    The NHS says medicine to prevent seizures, called anti-seizure medicine, is the main treatment for epilepsy. NHS 111 Wales says these medicines help control seizures in about 7 out of 10 people. They do not cure epilepsy, but they can stop seizures happening. NHS inform says some anti-seizure medicines can reduce how well some types of contraception work. The NHS says it is very important to discuss treatment with a specialist if there is a chance of becoming pregnant while taking epilepsy medicine.

    Other options are considered when medicines do not control the seizures. The NHS describes surgery as an option for rare cases. NICE's evidence review describes vagus nerve stimulation as used for people whose seizures have not responded to anti-seizure medicines and who are not suitable for surgery. The ketogenic diet, which is high in fat and low in carbohydrate and protein, is mainly used in children, usually those who have not responded to medicines. NHS inform says it should only be used under the supervision of an epilepsy specialist with the help of a dietitian.

    What should happen if someone has a seizure?

    NHS advice for a convulsive seizure is to cushion or gently hold the person's head to protect it from injury. Epilepsy Action adds that anything potentially harmful nearby should be cleared. The NHS also advises not restraining the person and not putting anything in their mouth. Once the jerking stops, the advice is to turn the person onto their side in the recovery position.

    The NHS says to call 999 for an ambulance if it is the person's first seizure, if the seizure lasts longer than is usual for them, or if it lasts more than 5 minutes and their usual pattern is not known. A 999 call is also advised if the person does not recover as usual, has another seizure before recovering, is seriously injured or has trouble breathing. Status epilepticus, a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes or seizures back to back with no recovery in between, needs emergency treatment, and NHS inform says to phone 999 straight away if it is suspected.

    Living with epilepsy and the outlook

    NHS 111 Wales suggests keeping a seizure diary, noting when seizures happen and what the person was doing beforehand, to help work out triggers. Epilepsy charities and NHS guidance list lack of sleep, missing anti-seizure medicine, stress and alcohol among possible triggers, and the CDC lists hormonal changes with a period. Flashing lights trigger seizures in a small number of people.

    GOV.UK says a person must stop driving straight away after any epileptic seizure and tell the DVLA. When driving can start again depends on the type of seizure and how long the person has been seizure-free. Epilepsy can also affect decisions about contraception and planning a pregnancy.

    The NHS says an epileptic seizure usually stops by itself and does not cause any lasting problems. Rarely, a seizure can last a long time, or several can happen without recovery in between, which is called status epilepticus and is a medical emergency that needs a 999 call. There is also a small risk of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). The NHS describes SUDEP as rare and says epilepsy treatment can reduce the risk. The CDC lists frequent or uncontrolled seizures among the factors linked to a higher risk. Epilepsy Action says there is not enough research to show that anti-suffocation pillows reduce the risk.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can epilepsy be cured?

    The NHS says anti-seizure medicines do not cure epilepsy, but they can stop seizures happening. NHS 111 Wales says they help control seizures in about 7 out of 10 people. When medicines do not work well enough, other options such as surgery, nerve stimulation or a special diet may be discussed with an epilepsy specialist.

    Does a normal EEG mean it is not epilepsy?

    No. A normal EEG does not rule epilepsy out, because many people with epilepsy do not show abnormal activity between seizures. No single test can confirm epilepsy. Doctors weigh the EEG together with the story of what happened, any scan results and other information.

    Can I drive after a seizure?

    GOV.UK says a person must stop driving straight away after any epileptic seizure and tell the DVLA. When they can start again depends on the type of seizure and how long they have been seizure-free. The exception is for people who have already been given permission to drive with their type of seizures.

    What is an aura?

    An aura is a warning that some people have before a seizure. Epilepsy Action says it is a type of focal seizure in its own right. Examples include a rising feeling in the tummy, unusual smells or tastes, and tingling in the arms and legs.

    When should I call 999 for a seizure?

    The NHS says to call 999 if it is the person's first seizure, if the seizure lasts longer than usual for them, or if it lasts more than 5 minutes and their usual pattern is not known. It also advises calling if the person does not recover as usual, has another seizure before recovering, is seriously injured or has trouble breathing.

    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Medications

    • Diazepam Diazepam is a benzodiazepine, a type of medicine used to treat anxiety, muscle spasms and seizures.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Gabapentin A prescription medicine used to treat epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • Pregabalin Pregabalin is a prescription medicine used for nerve pain, epilepsy and generalised anxiety disorder.

    Drug classes

    • Benzodiazepines Benzodiazepines are depressant medicines that slow the central nervous system, and diazepam is the member covered on this site.
