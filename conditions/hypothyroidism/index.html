    Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism)

    Also known as: hypothyroidism, Hashimoto's thyroiditis, myxoedema

    Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.

    When to seek care

    See a doctor for persistent tiredness with weight gain, cold intolerance or low mood; emergency care for drowsiness, very low temperature or confusion in someone with known hypothyroidism. Myxoedema coma, an extreme form in which the body's functions slow to a life-threatening point, needs immediate hospital treatment.

    Overview

    Thyroxine sets the pace of nearly every cell, so its lack slows everything: energy, thought, bowel, heart rate and heat production. Symptoms creep in over months and are easily mistaken for ageing or depression. A simple blood test makes the diagnosis and a daily tablet of levothyroxine restores normal life. Symptoms are non-specific and gradual: difficulty concentrating, low mood, hair loss, a hoarse voice and heavier or irregular periods are common additions to tiredness, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation and dry skin. No single symptom reliably identifies the condition, and many people with mild (subclinical) hypothyroidism have no symptoms at all, so attributing a given complaint to the thyroid can be difficult. Hashimoto's disease, in which the immune system attacks the thyroid, is the most common cause where iodine intake is adequate; iodine deficiency remains the commonest cause worldwide.

    Causes

    • Autoimmune (Hashimoto's) thyroiditis
    • Treatment of an overactive thyroid (radioiodine, surgery)
    • Iodine deficiency worldwide
    • Drugs: amiodarone, lithium, immunotherapy
    • Pituitary failure (rare)
    • Congenital
    • Thyroiditis (inflammation of the gland)
    • Radiotherapy to the neck

    Risk factors

    • Female sex
    • Age over 60
    • Family history
    • Other autoimmune disease
    • Pregnancy and the year after

    Symptoms

    FatigueWeaknessSwelling (oedema)Joint painTingling (pins and needles)

    What a clinician may find

    • Weight gain, dry skin and hair, puffy face
    • Slow pulse, cold intolerance
    • Slow-relaxing reflexes
    • Goitre
    • Hoarse voice, constipation, heavy periods
    • Enlarged thyroid felt on examination of the neck
    • Muscle weakness, aches and cramps

    Complications

    • High cholesterol and heart disease
    • Depression and cognitive slowing
    • Infertility and pregnancy complications
    • Myxoedema coma (rare, life-threatening)
    • Premature birth and other pregnancy complications when untreated

    Diagnosis

    A raised TSH with a low free T4 confirms primary hypothyroidism; thyroid peroxidase antibodies indicate the autoimmune cause. Cholesterol is often raised. A GP typically asks about symptoms, examines the neck for an enlarged thyroid and arranges blood tests. Because hypothyroidism contributes to high cholesterol, people found to have raised cholesterol are tested for it.

    Treatment

    Levothyroxine once daily on an empty stomach, dose adjusted by TSH every six to eight weeks until stable, then checked yearly; higher doses are needed in pregnancy. Dosing starts low and rises gradually, with regular blood tests until the right dose is found; some people feel better within weeks, others take months, and treatment is usually lifelong. Side effects such as sweating, chest pain, headaches and sleep problems mostly occur when the dose is too high. Extra care is needed in people with heart conditions, diabetes or adrenal problems. People who are mildly affected with few or no symptoms may not need treatment and are simply monitored. An unusually high dose requirement prompts a check for absorption problems, missed doses or coeliac disease.

    Levothyroxine

    Prevention

    Adequate iodine intake; screening in pregnancy and after thyroid treatment. Pregnant women taking levothyroxine usually need more frequent testing, and the dose often changes.

    What does an underactive thyroid feel like?

    An underactive thyroid develops slowly, and symptoms may not show for months or even years. Because the changes build up gradually, some people go months or even years without noticing symptoms. The NHS lists common symptoms such as feeling very tired, putting on weight, feeling colder than usual and constipation. A croaky or hoarse voice is also described, and some women have heavy or irregular periods. Symptoms often resemble those of other conditions, which can delay recognition.

    Many of these problems have other explanations, so a blood test that measures hormone levels is the only accurate way to confirm a thyroid problem. The NHS describes an underactive thyroid as usually lifelong, and says it can be managed with medicine.

    What causes an underactive thyroid?

    The main cause in the UK is Hashimoto's disease, which is an autoimmune condition. Autoimmune means the body's defence system mistakenly attacks its own tissue, in this case the thyroid. Inflammation of the thyroid, called thyroiditis, is another cause. Not getting enough iodine in the diet can also be a cause, but the NHS says this is rare in the UK.

    Surgery or radiotherapy around the neck or thyroid can also lead to it. Treatment for an overactive thyroid can lead to it as well. The NIDDK says people who receive radioactive iodine treatment will probably eventually develop an underactive thyroid. Certain medicines, including lithium, amiodarone and interferon alpha, have been linked to the condition. Some babies are born with an underactive thyroid, which is called congenital hypothyroidism.

    What happens at an appointment, and which tests are used?

    A GP may ask about symptoms and may feel the neck to check whether the thyroid is enlarged. The TSH blood test needs no special preparation, according to MedlinePlus. MedlinePlus also says biotin (vitamin B7) can distort TSH results. TSH is a hormone made by the pituitary gland that tells the thyroid how much to produce. A high level of TSH together with a low level of thyroid hormone (T4) can point to an underactive thyroid.

    When Hashimoto's disease is suspected, a blood test for thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies can be used, and the NIDDK says it is positive in most people with the condition. An ultrasound scan may be used if antibodies are not found but Hashimoto's disease is still suspected. The NIDDK says ultrasound can also help rule out other causes of an enlarged thyroid, such as nodules. The NIDDK adds that further testing is usually unnecessary to confirm the diagnosis.

    How is an underactive thyroid treated?

    The usual treatment is levothyroxine tablets. Levothyroxine replaces the thyroid hormone the body is missing. NHS inform says people usually need to take it for the rest of their lives. Some people with very mild symptoms, or none, may not need any treatment.

    Regular blood tests are used at the start until the right dose is found. A follow-up blood test is usually arranged after a dose is started or changed. The NIDDK says the condition can most likely be controlled with thyroid hormone medicine as long as the recommended dose is taken as instructed. The NIDDK also warns that taking too much thyroid hormone medicine can cause serious problems, such as atrial fibrillation (an irregular heart rhythm) or osteoporosis (weak bones). NHS guidance says the wrong dose can cause side effects such as feeling sick, diarrhoea, headaches or problems sleeping.

    Pregnancy, babies and children

    The NHS advises telling the GP when pregnant or trying to conceive with an underactive thyroid. Further tests are needed, and the levothyroxine dose will probably change during pregnancy. Untreated, an underactive thyroid in pregnancy can raise the chance of problems such as pre-eclampsia, birth defects, premature birth or miscarriage.

    Congenital hypothyroidism, an underactive thyroid present from birth, often has subtle signs or none at birth. This is why newborn screening matters. A clinical review reports that growth and development return to normal with early and adequate treatment. Some babies have a temporary form of the condition. Another review says some children treated early still have mild hearing deficits, so hearing should be checked periodically.

    What are the complications and the outlook?

    NHS 111 Wales says an underactive thyroid is not usually serious if the right treatment is being given. Untreated, it raises the risk of cardiovascular disease, according to the NHS, and NHS 111 Wales lists bone problems including osteoporosis. The NHS also lists goitre, which is an enlarged thyroid gland, and problems in pregnancy as possible complications.

    A cardiology review describes the heart effects as typically reversible with hormone replacement. Myxoedema coma is a very rare but life-threatening complication that needs emergency hospital treatment. The route to care for myxoedema coma is to call 999. The NHS says treatment usually involves thyroid hormone replacement medicine given directly into a vein.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is an underactive thyroid permanent?

    NHS inform says people usually need to take levothyroxine for the rest of their lives. Regular blood tests are used at the start to find the right dose. Some people with very mild or no symptoms may not need treatment. The NIDDK says it can most likely be controlled with thyroid hormone medicine taken as instructed.

    What is Hashimoto's disease?

    Hashimoto's disease is an autoimmune condition in which the body's defence system attacks the thyroid. It is the main cause of an underactive thyroid in the UK, according to the NHS. The NHS says it is most common in women and usually starts between the ages of 30 and 50. A blood test for TPO antibodies can be part of the check, and the NIDDK says it is positive in most people with the condition.

    How do doctors confirm an underactive thyroid?

    A GP may ask about symptoms and may feel the neck to check whether the thyroid is enlarged. A blood test measuring hormone levels is the only accurate way to confirm a thyroid problem. A high TSH with a low T4 can point to an underactive thyroid.

    Can an underactive thyroid affect pregnancy?

    Untreated, it can raise the chance of problems such as pre-eclampsia, premature birth or miscarriage. The NHS advises telling the GP when pregnant or trying to conceive. Extra tests are then needed, and the levothyroxine dose will probably change during pregnancy.

    Can an underactive thyroid be dangerous?

    It is not usually serious with the right treatment, according to the NHS. Untreated, it can raise the risk of cardiovascular disease and of bone problems such as osteoporosis. Myxoedema coma is very rare but life-threatening and needs emergency hospital treatment, and the route to care is 999.

    Conditions

    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Depression Depression is a low mood that lasts for weeks or months and affects daily life.
    • High cholesterol (hypercholesterolaemia) High cholesterol, also called hypercholesterolaemia, means there is too much cholesterol in the blood.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.
    • Thyroid cancer Thyroid cancer is a cancer that starts in the thyroid gland, a gland in the neck that makes hormones.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Medications

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.

    Drug classes

    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.

    • Levothyroxine: timing and other medicines The NHS says levothyroxine replaces a missing thyroid hormone and is taken at the same time each day on an empty stomach.

    • Tiredness and fatigue: causes and next steps Tiredness is common and often has an ordinary cause such as poor sleep, stress or illness.
