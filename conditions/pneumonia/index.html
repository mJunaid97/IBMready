    Pneumonia

    Also known as: chest infection, lung infection

    Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for breathlessness at rest, confusion, blue lips, chest pain, coughing blood, or fever with fast breathing in a child or older adult.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Overview

    Bacteria (most often Streptococcus pneumoniae), viruses (influenza, COVID-19, RSV) and occasionally fungi cause pneumonia. It ranges from a mild illness treated at home to a life-threatening one in the elderly, the very young and those with chronic disease, and it remains the leading infectious cause of death in children worldwide.

    Causes

    • Bacteria: pneumococcus, Haemophilus, Mycoplasma, Legionella, Staphylococcus
    • Viruses: influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV
    • Aspiration of stomach contents
    • Hospital-acquired organisms in ventilated or frail patients

    Risk factors

    • Age over 65 or under 2
    • Smoking and COPD
    • Weak immune system, diabetes, heart, kidney or liver disease
    • Swallowing problems after stroke
    • Recent flu

    Symptoms

    CoughFeverShortness of breathChest painFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Fast breathing, fever, crackles and dull percussion over the affected lobe
    • Low oxygen saturation
    • Confusion in the elderly

    Complications

    • Pleural effusion and empyema
    • Sepsis
    • Respiratory failure
    • Lung abscess

    Diagnosis

    Clinical findings plus a chest X-ray showing consolidation. Blood tests (white cells, CRP, kidney function), oxygen saturation, sputum and blood cultures, and a severity score (CURB-65) guide where and how to treat.

    Full blood countCRP testPulse oximetry and blood gasesMicrobiology testsKidney function tests
    X-rayCT scan

    Treatment

    Antibiotics chosen by likely organism and severity (amoxicillin for mild community-acquired pneumonia; combinations in hospital), antivirals for influenza, oxygen, fluids, and support of breathing in severe cases. Rest and hydration at home for mild cases.

    AmoxicillinParacetamol

    Prevention

    Pneumococcal, influenza and COVID-19 vaccination, stopping smoking, good hand hygiene, and managing chronic diseases.

    What are the symptoms of pneumonia?

    The NHS says pneumonia symptoms can come on suddenly or build up over a few days. A cough, breathlessness and a fever are typical, and chest pain and confusion can also occur, with confusion being common in older people. Some people cough up yellow or green phlegm. Where the lining around the lung is also inflamed (pleurisy), the chest pain can be sharp and get worse when breathing.

    The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111 for a cough that lasts about three weeks or more, yellow or green phlegm or breathlessness. It advises calling 999 for severe difficulty breathing, pale, blue or blotchy skin, lips or tongue, sudden confusion or coughing up more than streaks of blood, and says not to drive yourself to A&E. Pneumonia is more common, and can be more serious, in young children.

    What causes pneumonia, and what are the complications?

    Pneumonia is an infection of one or both lungs. Bacteria, viruses or fungi can cause it. The pneumococcus bacterium can cause pneumonia, and the vaccine against it is described below.

    Aspiration, which means food or liquid going down into the lungs by mistake, can also cause pneumonia.

    Pneumonia can affect people of any age. Pneumonia can be more serious in young children, in people over 65 and in people with other serious health problems, who are more likely to need hospital care.

    Complications of pneumonia can include pleurisy, fluid around the lungs, a lung abscess, sepsis and respiratory failure.

    How is pneumonia diagnosed?

    NHS hospital leaflets describe a doctor first asking about symptoms and examining the chest. A chest X-ray may be arranged to confirm the diagnosis, and blood tests and tests on a sample of phlegm (sputum) may also be done.

    Doctors often use a scoring system to gauge how serious the pneumonia is. NHS inform describes a scoring system called CRB-65 that estimates the risk of complications and whether urgent hospital treatment is needed. Most people recover at home, while people who are very unwell or at higher risk may be admitted.

    In hospital the team can give antibiotics through a drip, and sometimes steroids, and may add oxygen to help breathing. The NHS says a follow-up chest X-ray is not routine for everyone. It may be arranged about six weeks later for some people, for example those who smoke or are over 50.

    How is pneumonia treated, and how long does recovery take?

    The NHS says antibiotics are the usual treatment when bacteria are the likely cause. Mild cases can often be treated at home with antibiotics, plenty of rest and plenty of fluids. Pneumonia caused by a virus does not need antibiotics.

    People who are very unwell, or at higher risk, may be treated in hospital. There the usual treatment is antibiotics, often given into a vein, and sometimes steroids. Oxygen can be added to help with breathing.

    Recovery varies from person to person. NHS guidance sets out a rough timeline: the high temperature should have gone after about a week, chest pain and mucus should have reduced substantially after about four weeks, and cough and breathlessness should have eased substantially after about six weeks. Most symptoms should have resolved by three months, although tiredness can linger, and most people feel back to normal by about six months.

    Recovery can take longer in older people and in people with other health problems. The cough may last for two to three weeks after the antibiotics are finished, and tiredness can last longer as the body recovers. People who have had pneumonia are also more prone to other chest infections for a while.

    Can pneumonia be prevented?

    The pneumococcal vaccine helps protect against serious illnesses such as pneumonia, but it does not protect against illnesses caused by other bacteria or viruses. The pneumococcal vaccine helps protect against serious pneumococcal infections, which can lead to pneumonia, septicaemia and meningitis.

    In the UK the pneumococcal vaccine is offered to babies as part of the routine childhood programme. The pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine is given to people aged 65 and over and to people at high risk because of long-term health conditions. The NHS also offers flu, RSV and COVID-19 vaccines to some groups, such as older people and people with certain long-term health conditions.

    Frequently asked questions

    Do all cases of pneumonia need antibiotics?

    No. The NHS says antibiotics are the usual treatment when bacteria are the likely cause, but pneumonia caused by a virus does not need antibiotics.

    Can pneumonia be treated at home?

    Mild cases can often be treated at home with antibiotics, rest and plenty of fluids. People who are very unwell, or at higher risk, may be treated in hospital, where antibiotics are often given into a vein and oxygen can be added.

    Why does the cough last so long after pneumonia?

    The NHS and hospital leaflets say a cough can carry on for two to three weeks after antibiotics are finished, and tiredness can last longer. NHS guidance sets out a rough timeline in which cough and breathlessness should have eased substantially after about six weeks.

    When is pneumonia an emergency?

    The NHS says to call 999 for severe difficulty breathing, pale, blue or blotchy skin, lips or tongue, sudden confusion or coughing up more than streaks of blood, and not to drive yourself to A&E. For a cough that lasts about three weeks or more, yellow or green phlegm or breathlessness it advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.

    Is there a vaccine against pneumonia?

    There are vaccines against some causes. The pneumococcal vaccine is offered to babies and to people aged 65 and over or at higher risk. Flu, RSV and COVID-19 vaccines are also advised for specific groups, but vaccines do not cover every cause of pneumonia.

    Conditions

    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.
    • COVID-19 COVID-19 is an illness caused by a virus called coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2), with symptoms that overlap with colds and flu.
    • Flu (influenza) Flu is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.

    Symptoms

    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • HIV test A blood test that looks for the human immunodeficiency virus.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Lymphocytes The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells).
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…

    Drug classes

    • Bronchodilators Inhaled medicines that relax the muscle around the airways within minutes (salbutamol, terbutaline) or for about 12 hours (salmeterol.
    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.
    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.
