    Herniated (slipped) disc

    Also known as: slipped disc, prolapsed disc, sciatica

    Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels, which can press on a nerve root.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for numbness around the saddle area, difficulty passing urine or controlling bowels, or weakness in both legs; urgent review for progressive weakness or unbearable pain.

    Overview

    Discs weaken with age and can bulge or rupture under load. Most herniations cause back pain alone; when the extruded material compresses a nerve root the result is sciatica: sharp pain, tingling and numbness shooting down the leg in that root's territory, sometimes with weakness. Nine in ten settle within three months without surgery. The lower back is by far the commonest site, followed by the neck; herniations in the upper and mid back are rare. Injury or strain can push the disc out of place or break it open. Without pressure on a nerve the result may be a low backache or no pain at all; with nerve pressure, pain, numbness or weakness follow the path of the affected nerve, typically down one side. Sciatica is the name for this pattern of leg symptoms, not a diagnosis in itself: a herniated disc is its leading cause, but spinal stenosis, piriformis syndrome, pelvic injury or fracture, tumours and spondylolisthesis can produce the same picture.

    Causes

    • Age-related disc degeneration
    • Lifting, twisting or a sudden strain
    • Repetitive loading

    Risk factors

    • Age 30 to 50
    • Heavy manual work, driving
    • Smoking
    • Obesity
    • Family history

    Symptoms

    Back painNumbnessTingling (pins and needles)Weakness

    What a clinician may find

    • Pain reproduced by straight-leg raising
    • Reduced ankle or knee reflex
    • Weak ankle dorsiflexion or big toe extension
    • Sensory loss in a dermatome
    • Pain, numbness or weakness following a single nerve's path down one leg

    Complications

    • Cauda equina syndrome (emergency)
    • Persistent nerve damage
    • Chronic pain

    Diagnosis

    Clinical, with MRI reserved for red flags, progressive neurological deficit or when surgery or injection is being considered, because disc bulges are common in people without symptoms. A plain spine X-ray cannot show the disc or confirm a herniation; MRI shows the disc, the nerve root and the degree of compression. Because other conditions, including nerve tumours, can mimic sciatica, imaging that does not match the clinical signs prompts a search for a non-disc cause.

    Treatment

    Stay active, pain relief (NSAIDs, short courses of stronger analgesia), physiotherapy, and time. Epidural steroid injections for severe radicular pain. Microdiscectomy for persistent sciatica beyond six to twelve weeks or for progressive weakness; emergency decompression for cauda equina syndrome. The first step is to stay active within comfort, with simple pain relief, followed by physiotherapy if symptoms persist; most people treated this way recover and return to normal activity. Anti-inflammatory medicines are used for mild to moderate pain, muscle relaxants for spasm, and short courses of stronger analgesia only when pain is severe and not responding. An epidural steroid injection is given under X-ray guidance so that the medicine reaches the exact level of the herniation. Surgery is considered when conservative treatment has not reduced the pain or when weakness progresses; only a minority of people with a symptomatic herniation need it.

    Prevention

    Lift with the legs, keep the back and core strong, healthy weight, do not smoke.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Sciatica Pain, and sometimes tingling, numbness or weakness, in one leg when the sciatic nerve is irritated or compressed.

    Symptoms

    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.

    Drug classes

    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
