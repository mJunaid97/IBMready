    Colorectal (bowel) cancer

    Also known as: bowel cancer, colon cancer, rectal cancer

    A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.

    When to seek care

    See a doctor within days for blood in the stool, a change in bowel habit lasting three weeks, unexplained weight loss or anaemia; emergency care for severe pain with vomiting and a swollen abdomen.

    Overview

    Bowel cancer is the third commonest cancer worldwide. Because it grows slowly from polyps, screening by stool testing and colonoscopy can find and remove growths before they turn malignant. Early cancers are cured by surgery; symptoms usually appear late, so screening participation matters. Early cancers may produce no symptoms, which is why screening is offered to people who feel well: it finds cancers at the stage when treatment is most effective and removes precancerous polyps before they turn malignant. Where symptoms occur they depend on where the tumour sits and whether it has spread, and all of them, including bleeding and a change in bowel habit, also occur in benign conditions, so they are not diagnostic on their own. Treatment is planned by stage, from stage 0 (confined to the lining) to stage IV (spread to distant organs), and the cancer can recur after treatment, so follow-up continues for years.

    Causes

    • Accumulated genetic mutations in the lining cells, usually sporadic
    • Inherited syndromes: Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis

    Risk factors

    • Age over 50
    • Family history
    • Long-standing inflammatory bowel disease
    • Processed and red meat, low fibre, obesity, inactivity
    • Smoking and alcohol
    • Type 2 diabetes
    • A personal history of adenomas (polyps) or a previous bowel cancer

    Symptoms

    Abdominal painFatigueSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • Change in bowel habit lasting weeks
    • Blood in or on the stool
    • Iron-deficiency anaemia
    • A palpable mass
    • Weight loss
    • Narrower stools, diarrhoea or constipation persisting for weeks

    Complications

    • Bowel obstruction or perforation
    • Spread to liver, lungs and peritoneum
    • Anaemia

    Diagnosis

    Faecal immunochemical test for screening or triage, then colonoscopy with biopsy; CT of chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging; MRI of the rectum; blood CEA marker. Screening uses a faecal immunochemical test (FIT) that detects hidden blood in the stool; a positive test or new symptoms lead to colonoscopy, which allows the whole colon to be inspected, polyps removed and the tumour biopsied. Once cancer is confirmed, staging determines treatment: CT of the chest, abdomen and pelvis looks for spread to the liver and lungs, MRI of the pelvis defines how far a rectal cancer has grown through the wall and towards nearby structures, and the blood marker CEA gives a baseline for follow-up.

    Treatment

    Surgery to remove the tumour and lymph nodes is the mainstay; chemotherapy after surgery for higher-risk disease; radiotherapy and chemotherapy before surgery for rectal cancer; chemotherapy, targeted and immune drugs for spread; liver metastases are sometimes resectable. The National Cancer Institute's PDQ summary describes surgery as the most common treatment at every stage. For stage 0 a polypectomy or local excision with clear margins may be enough, with colon resection for larger lesions; for stages I and II the standard operation is wide resection of the affected segment with its lymph nodes and rejoining of the bowel (anastomosis). Chemotherapy after surgery is the established approach for stage III disease and is still under evaluation for stage II. Rectal cancer is often treated with chemotherapy and radiotherapy before surgery to shrink the tumour. For stage IV and recurrent disease the options include surgery, systemic chemotherapy, targeted and immune therapies and clinical trials; liver metastases may be treated by resection, chemotherapy before or after surgery, local ablation or chemotherapy delivered into the hepatic artery.

    Prevention

    Take part in screening from the age offered, eat fibre and less processed meat, keep active and a healthy weight, do not smoke, limit alcohol; aspirin lowers risk in Lynch syndrome. Diet, body weight and physical activity have some of the strongest links to risk of any cancer type, so these are the modifiable factors with the most to gain.

    What are the symptoms of bowel cancer?

    The NHS lists a change in bowel habit, blood in the poo, bleeding from the bottom, ongoing tummy pain, unexplained weight loss and ongoing tiredness among the possible symptoms of bowel cancer. The NHS says having these symptoms does not definitely mean bowel cancer, but it is important to get checked by a GP. Several UK sources advise seeing a GP when a change in bowel habit lasts three weeks or more.

    Blood in the poo can have harmless causes such as haemorrhoids (piles), but it should always be checked. NHS England advises an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111 for black or dark red poo or bloody diarrhoea. It advises calling 999 or going to A&E for bleeding from the bottom that will not stop, or a large amount of blood or clots. A GP appointment is the usual route for other symptoms.

    How is bowel cancer staged?

    Bowel cancer is the term for colon cancer and rectal cancer, and treatment differs between the two. Doctors usually describe how far the cancer has spread using the TNM system. T describes the main tumour, N whether nearby lymph nodes are involved, and M whether the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body.

    Macmillan describes number stages from 0 to 4, although some providers list only stages 1 to 4. Stage 0 means the cancer is confined to the mucosa, the innermost layer of the bowel wall. Stage 3 means the cancer has reached nearby lymph nodes but has not spread elsewhere. Stage 4 means it has spread to distant organs such as the liver or lungs, or to the lining of the abdomen called the peritoneum.

    What happens at the GP and in hospital?

    The NHS and Bowel Cancer UK describe a GP asking when symptoms started, whether they come and go, and about general health, medical history and any family history of bowel cancer. The doctor may feel the tummy and ask to do a rectal examination, where a gloved finger is placed inside the back passage to feel for lumps. The NHS says this can be uncomfortable but should not be painful, and a person can ask for someone else to be in the room. The GP may give a home test kit called a FIT, which checks for hidden blood in the poo, and may arrange a blood test.

    Cancer Research UK says an urgent referral means a hospital appointment or more tests within two weeks, and that it does not definitely mean cancer. Macmillan calls colonoscopy the main test used to diagnose bowel cancer. Polyps found during it can be removed, and biopsies (small samples) can be taken. CT colonography is used if a colonoscopy did not give clear enough pictures.

    How is bowel cancer treated?

    The NHS says people may be offered a combination of surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and targeted medicines, depending on the details of the cancer. NHS hospital trust pages add that the size and location of the cancer matter, along with general health, age, and the grade, stage and type of cancer.

    Removing the affected section of bowel is the core treatment. NHS 111 Wales says surgery is usually able to completely remove cancer that is confined to the bowel, and that a local resection may be enough for very small, early tumours. Chemotherapy can be given before surgery to shrink a tumour, after surgery to lower the risk of the cancer coming back, or to treat advanced cancer. Targeted medicines and immunotherapy are sometimes used for advanced bowel cancer.

    Radiotherapy is used mostly for rectal cancer. It is often combined with chemotherapy, called chemoradiotherapy, and is usually given before surgery.

    What is a stoma?

    A stoma is an opening made on the tummy so that waste can leave the body and collect in a bag. A colostomy is made from the colon, and an ileostomy from the small bowel. A surgeon may make a temporary stoma to let the bowel heal after surgery. Reversal usually takes place after treatment, and further treatment such as chemotherapy may delay it.

    Macmillan says only a small number of people with colon cancer need a permanent stoma. If a rectal cancer is very low and close to the anus, a permanent stoma is more likely. Colostomy UK says people with a colostomy should not generally have a high stoma output. NHS trust leaflets on ileostomies list dry mouth with constant thirst, dizzy spells and passing less urine as signs of dehydration.

    What can happen after treatment?

    Some people develop low anterior resection syndrome after an operation that removes all or part of the rectum. Macmillan says bowel changes can often be managed by changing the diet. A review describes symptoms such as urgency, frequent bowel movements, clustering of stools and difficulty emptying.

    Macmillan says tiredness can continue for weeks or months after treatment finishes. It describes late effects as effects that begin months or even years later. Bowel Cancer UK lists bowel and bladder changes among them and says healthcare teams are used to talking about effects on sex life. The NICE guideline says follow-up after surgery for non-metastatic disease should include a blood test for carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and a CT scan of the chest, abdomen and pelvis.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does a bowel cancer symptom always mean cancer?

    No. The NHS says having these symptoms does not definitely mean bowel cancer, but it is important to get checked by a GP. Many symptoms have other causes, such as piles, infections or inflammatory bowel disease. An urgent referral to a specialist also does not definitely mean cancer.

    Is a colonoscopy done under sedation?

    NHS trust leaflets describe sedation, or gas and air (Entonox) as an alternative. Entonox is a gas the person breathes in themselves, and it wears off faster. Trusts give the test as taking about 20 minutes to an hour, longer if a polyp is removed.

    Will I need a stoma?

    Not necessarily. Macmillan says only a small number of people with colon cancer need a permanent stoma. A surgeon may make a temporary stoma to let the bowel heal after surgery, and reversal usually takes place after treatment. A permanent stoma is more likely when a rectal cancer is very low and close to the anus.

    Which bleeding needs urgent help?

    NHS England advises an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111 for black or dark red poo or bloody diarrhoea. It advises calling 999 or going to A&E for bleeding from the bottom that will not stop, or a large amount of blood or clots.

    Conditions

    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.
    • Ovarian cancer A malignant tumour of the ovary or, as is now recognised for many cases of the commonest type, of the adjacent fallopian tube.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.

    Biomarkers

    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.

    Drug classes

    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
