    Fibroids

    Also known as: uterine fibroids, leiomyoma, myoma, fibromyoma, uterine leiomyoma

    Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus, ranging from the size of a pea to a mass that fills the abdomen.

    When to seek care

    See a GP for periods heavy enough to disrupt life, bleeding between periods, tiredness or breathlessness that could mean anaemia, pressure symptoms in the bladder or bowel, a swelling in the lower abdomen, or difficulty conceiving. Seek urgent care for sudden severe abdominal pain, bleeding that soaks a pad every hour for several hours, or faintness. Any bleeding after the menopause, or a fibroid that grows after the menopause, needs prompt assessment.

    Overview

    Fibroids are the commonest benign (non-cancerous) growth of the uterus: the NHS estimates that around 1 in 3 women develop them at some point, most often between the ages of 30 and 50, and they are more common in Black women. They occur in anyone with a uterus, including trans men and non-binary people. Fibroids depend on oestrogen and progesterone, so they grow during the reproductive years and in pregnancy and usually shrink after the menopause. They are described by position: intramural fibroids sit within the myometrium, subserosal fibroids bulge from the outer surface towards the abdomen, submucosal fibroids push into the cavity and distort the lining, and pedunculated fibroids hang on a stalk inside or outside the uterus. Many cause no symptoms and are found by chance on a scan. When they do cause trouble it is usually heavy periods, pressure on the bladder or bowel, pain or, with submucosal fibroids, difficulty conceiving.

    Causes

    • The exact cause is unknown; each fibroid arises from a single smooth muscle cell of the myometrium that multiplies abnormally
    • Growth is driven by oestrogen and progesterone, which is why fibroids appear after puberty, enlarge in pregnancy and shrink after the menopause
    • Genetic changes within the fibroid cells and local growth factors sustain the growth
    • Fibroids are not caused by infection and do not develop from the endometrium

    Risk factors

    • Age between 30 and 50
    • Black or African-Caribbean ancestry
    • A mother or sister with fibroids
    • Being overweight, which raises oestrogen levels
    • Early first period
    • Never having given birth; pregnancy appears to lower the risk

    What a clinician may find

    • An enlarged, firm, irregular uterus felt on abdominal or pelvic examination
    • A mass in the lower abdomen that can be large enough to resemble pregnancy
    • Pallor and other signs of iron deficiency anaemia from heavy bleeding
    • A normal examination when the fibroids are small

    Complications

    • Iron deficiency anaemia from heavy or prolonged periods
    • Pressure effects: frequent urination or difficulty emptying the bladder, constipation, and rarely blockage of a ureter
    • Subfertility and miscarriage, particularly with submucosal fibroids that distort the cavity
    • Problems in pregnancy: pain from degeneration of a fibroid, an awkward position of the baby, obstructed labour, caesarean birth and heavier bleeding after delivery
    • Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, causing sudden severe pain
    • Uterine sarcoma (leiomyosarcoma) is a rare cancer that can look like a fibroid on a scan; fibroids themselves are not thought to turn cancerous, but a fibroid that grows quickly, or any growth or bleeding after the menopause, needs prompt assessment

    Diagnosis

    Fibroids are often discovered on a scan done for another reason. When symptoms prompt investigation, the history covers the heaviness and pattern of bleeding, pressure symptoms and plans for pregnancy, and the examination feels for an enlarged or irregular uterus. Ultrasound, transvaginal or through the abdomen, is the first imaging and shows the number, size and position of the fibroids; NICE recommends it when the uterus can be felt through the abdomen or a pelvic mass is suspected. MRI maps fibroids more precisely before myomectomy or uterine artery embolisation and helps to distinguish them from adenomyosis. Hysteroscopy is used when submucosal fibroids are suspected and allows small ones to be removed at the same time. A full blood count is offered to anyone with heavy bleeding to check for anaemia; NICE does not recommend testing ferritin routinely, and iron treatment is guided by the blood count and symptoms. A pregnancy test is done in anyone of reproductive age with bleeding or a pelvic mass.

    Treatment

    Fibroids that cause no symptoms need no treatment, only review if they are large. For heavy bleeding the options are those for heavy menstrual bleeding generally. For fibroids smaller than about 3 cm, NICE advises the levonorgestrel intrauterine system as the first option where the cavity is not distorted, with tranexamic acid, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen, the combined pill or cyclical progestogens if it is unsuitable, and iron to correct anaemia. Fibroids of 3 cm or more are referred for specialist assessment. GnRH analogues shrink fibroids and stop bleeding by inducing a temporary menopause; they are used for a few months before surgery, with add-back hormone therapy to protect bone. Procedures that keep the uterus are myomectomy (removal of the fibroids through the abdomen, laparoscopically or hysteroscopically), uterine artery embolisation, in which a radiologist blocks the blood supply so that the fibroids shrink, endometrial ablation for small fibroids with heavy bleeding, and MRI-guided focused ultrasound where it is available. The effect of uterine artery embolisation and myomectomy on future pregnancy is uncertain and is discussed before choosing between them; endometrial ablation is not suitable for anyone who may want a pregnancy. Hysterectomy is the definitive treatment for those who have completed their family and whose symptoms are severe.

    Prevention

    There is no proven way to prevent fibroids. Keeping to a healthy weight may lower the risk. Treating heavy periods early prevents iron deficiency anaemia, and regular review of known fibroids catches growth or new symptoms before they cause complications.

    Conditions

    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Pelvic organ prolapse Descent of one or more of the pelvic organs (the bladder, uterus, rectum, small bowel or, after hysterectomy.

    Symptoms

    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.

    Biomarkers

    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.

    Guides and news

    • Bleeding after menopause or between periods Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be checked by a GP.
    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
