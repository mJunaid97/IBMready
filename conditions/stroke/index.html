    Stroke

    Also known as: cerebrovascular accident, CVA, brain attack, TIA

    Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic, 85%) or a bleed (haemorrhagic).

    When to seek care

    Call emergency services immediately at any sign of Face drooping, Arm weakness or Speech difficulty: Time matters. Call 999 even if the symptoms go away: a TIA is a warning that a stroke may follow and needs urgent specialist assessment.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Overview

    Brain tissue dies within minutes of losing its blood supply, and the lost functions map the artery: weakness and numbness of the opposite side, loss of speech, loss of half the visual field, or unsteadiness and double vision from the brainstem. Treatment to reopen the artery works only in the first hours, so stroke is a medical emergency recognised by the FAST test.

    Causes

    • Atherosclerosis of the carotid and cerebral arteries
    • Emboli from the heart in atrial fibrillation
    • Small vessel disease from high blood pressure and diabetes
    • Bleeding from high blood pressure, aneurysms or vascular malformations
    • Arterial dissection in younger people

    Risk factors

    • High blood pressure
    • Atrial fibrillation
    • Smoking
    • Diabetes
    • High cholesterol
    • Age
    • Previous TIA or stroke
    • Excess alcohol

    Symptoms

    WeaknessNumbnessDizzinessHeadacheTingling (pins and needles)

    What a clinician may find

    • Facial droop
    • Arm drift
    • Slurred or absent speech
    • Loss of a visual field
    • Unsteady gait

    Complications

    • Permanent disability
    • Swallowing problems and aspiration pneumonia
    • Depression
    • Seizures
    • Recurrent stroke

    Diagnosis

    Immediate CT to distinguish a bleed from a blockage, then CT or MR angiography to find the blocked vessel. Blood glucose is checked at once to exclude hypoglycaemia. ECG, echocardiography and carotid ultrasound search for the source.

    Blood glucoseElectrocardiogramFull blood countLipid profileEchocardiogramAmbulatory ECG monitoring
    CT scanMRI scanUltrasound

    Treatment

    For ischaemic stroke, clot-dissolving thrombolysis within 4.5 hours and mechanical thrombectomy for a large-vessel blockage, as soon as possible (usually within 6 hours of onset, and up to 24 hours in selected people whose scans show brain tissue that can still be saved), then antiplatelet drugs, statins, blood pressure control and anticoagulation if AF is found. Bleeds need blood pressure control, reversal of anticoagulants and sometimes surgery. Rehabilitation by a stroke team drives recovery.

    Prevention

    Treat blood pressure and atrial fibrillation, stop smoking, control diabetes and cholesterol, exercise, and manage weight and alcohol.

    What are the symptoms of a stroke?

    The NHS uses the FAST test to describe the main signs of a stroke. FAST stands for face, arms, speech and time to call 999. The main signs are facial droop, arm weakness and slurred speech. Other signs include weakness or numbness down one side of the body, blurred vision, and confusion and memory loss.

    The NHS also lists difficulty speaking or finding words, dizziness, a severe headache, and nausea or vomiting among the symptoms. The NHS describes a stroke as a medical emergency and says to call 999 if a stroke is suspected. It says to call 999 even if the symptoms stop, because an assessment in hospital is still needed. A transient ischaemic attack, or TIA, causes symptoms like those of a stroke, and the Stroke Association says they usually last from a few minutes to a few hours.

    What are the types of stroke?

    Ischaemic strokes happen when the blood supply is stopped by a blood clot, and NHS inform says this is the cause in most cases. The NHLBI says the blockage is usually caused by a piece of plaque that builds up in the arteries during some cardiovascular diseases. Haemorrhagic strokes happen when a weakened blood vessel supplying the brain bursts. The NHS says bleeding can occur inside the brain tissue or in the space around it.

    A TIA is also called a mini-stroke because the symptoms are temporary. The WHO says a TIA usually lasts only a few minutes and does not cause long-term damage. The Stroke Association describes a TIA as a warning sign, and the CDC calls it a sign of a serious condition that will not go away without medical help. The NHS lists high blood pressure, diabetes, atrial fibrillation and high cholesterol among the risk factors for stroke.

    How is a stroke diagnosed?

    The NHS says a stroke is diagnosed mainly through blood tests, scans and other checks. It lists CT scans, MRI scans and ultrasound scans to check in and around the brain, and an electrocardiogram (ECG) to check the heart. NHS inform says that even when the physical signs of a stroke are obvious, a brain scan is needed.

    The scan shows whether a clot or a burst blood vessel caused the stroke, which part of the brain is affected and how severe it is. The Stroke Association says a brain scan should ideally happen soon after arrival at hospital. The CDC says there is no way to know at the start whether symptoms are from a TIA or from a major type of stroke.

    How is a stroke treated?

    The NHS says treatment depends on the type of stroke. Early treatment may include clot-dissolving medicine, surgery to remove a clot or drain fluid, and a procedure to ease pressure inside the skull. The Stroke Association says thrombolysis uses a clot-busting medicine to break up clots and restore blood flow.

    Thrombectomy removes the blood clot to help reduce brain damage by restoring blood flow. NHS inform says it is effective for ischaemic strokes caused by a clot in a large artery in the brain. The NHS says clot-dissolving treatment is most effective when started as soon as possible after the stroke.

    The Stroke Association says most people who have an ischaemic stroke are given blood-thinning medication to help prevent clots, and that most will need it for life. NHS inform says a combination of treatments is usually recommended to reduce the risk of another stroke, and long-term prevention can involve anticoagulants or antiplatelets and statins to lower cholesterol.

    How long does recovery take, and what does rehabilitation involve?

    The NHS says it can take days, weeks, months or years to recover from a stroke. Some people recover within days or weeks, while for others recovery can take months or years and may mean making life changes.

    NHS inform describes several kinds of rehabilitation. Physiotherapy often involves exercises to improve muscle strength to overcome walking difficulties. Occupational therapists assess a person’s ability to carry out everyday tasks and can help adapt the home or suggest equipment to make tasks easier. Speech and language therapists help with communication and swallowing problems. NHS inform notes that problems with language can affect speaking, understanding, reading and writing. Rehabilitation usually starts in hospital and can continue at home or in the community.

    What problems can follow a stroke, and how do people cope?

    The WHO lists trouble swallowing (dysphagia) and aspiration pneumonia, a lung infection, among the complications of stroke. The Stroke Association says being immobile raises the risk of blood clots forming in the leg veins. NHS inform says tiredness can be extreme and can last months or even years, and that bewilderment, anger and frustration are also common.

    The Stroke Association says depression is common after a stroke and that at least one third of survivors have some form of it within the first year. NHS inform says medicines and psychological therapies, such as counselling or cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), can help some people. The Stroke Association lists its Stroke Helpline as 0303 3033 100.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does FAST stand for?

    FAST stands for face, arms, speech and time to call 999, the NHS says. The main signs are facial droop, arm weakness and slurred speech. The NHS says to call 999 even if the symptoms stop, because an assessment in hospital is still needed.

    Is a TIA the same as a stroke?

    A TIA, or mini-stroke, has symptoms like those of a stroke, but the Stroke Association says they usually last from a few minutes to a few hours. The CDC says there is no way to know at the start whether symptoms are from a TIA or a major type of stroke. The Stroke Association describes a TIA as a warning sign.

    Which scans are used to diagnose a stroke?

    The NHS lists CT scans, MRI scans and ultrasound scans to check in and around the brain, along with blood tests and an electrocardiogram (ECG) to check the heart. NHS inform says the scan shows whether a clot or a burst blood vessel caused the stroke, which part of the brain is affected and how severe it is.

    How long does it take to recover from a stroke?

    The NHS says it can take days, weeks, months or years to recover from a stroke. Some people recover within days or weeks, while for others recovery can take months or years and may mean making life changes. NHS inform describes rehabilitation from physiotherapists, occupational therapists and speech and language therapists.

    Can a stroke affect mood?

    The Stroke Association says depression is common after a stroke, with at least one third of survivors having some form of it within the first year, and that it can appear at any point or return. NHS inform says anger, frustration and bewilderment are also common, and that medicines and psychological therapies can help some people.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • High cholesterol (hypercholesterolaemia) High cholesterol, also called hypercholesterolaemia, means there is too much cholesterol in the blood.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Peripheral arterial disease A long-term condition in which the arteries that supply blood to the legs become narrowed or blocked.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.

    Biomarkers

    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
