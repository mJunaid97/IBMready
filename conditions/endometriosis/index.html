    Endometriosis

    Also known as: endometrioma, chocolate cyst, deep infiltrating endometriosis, pelvic endometriosis

    A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries, the peritoneum and the ligaments of the pelvis, where it responds to the hormones of the menstrual cycle and causes inflammation, pain and scarring.

    When to seek care

    See a GP for period pain that stops normal activities, pain during or after sex, pain when passing urine or opening the bowels during a period, blood in the urine or stool during periods, or difficulty getting pregnant. Seek urgent care for sudden severe pelvic pain, pain with fever or vomiting, fainting, or pain with a positive pregnancy test, which can signal a ruptured or twisted cyst or an ectopic pregnancy.

    Overview

    Endometriosis affects roughly 1 in 10 women of reproductive age worldwide, and anyone with a uterus and ovaries can develop it, including trans men and non-binary people. The misplaced tissue bleeds a little with each period but has no way out, so it provokes inflammation, adhesions and cysts: a deposit on the ovary can grow into a blood-filled endometrioma, often called a chocolate cyst, and deep infiltrating disease can involve the uterosacral ligaments, the space between the rectum and the vagina, the bowel and the bladder. The result is period pain that stops normal life, pain with sex, bowel and bladder symptoms and, for some, difficulty conceiving. The condition depends on oestrogen, so it begins after puberty and symptoms usually improve after the menopause, although they do not always settle, and oestrogen-containing HRT can keep deposits active. Its symptoms overlap with other pelvic conditions and examination and scans are often normal in early disease, and the NHS notes that getting a diagnosis can take a long time.

    Causes

    • The cause is not fully understood, and endometriosis probably arises in more than one way
    • Retrograde menstruation, in which menstrual blood carrying endometrial cells flows back along the fallopian tubes into the pelvis, is the most widely accepted explanation
    • Transformation of peritoneal cells into endometrial-like tissue, and spread of cells through lymph or blood vessels, may explain deposits at distant sites
    • Oestrogen drives the deposits to grow and bleed, and an altered immune response fails to clear them
    • Genetic factors: the condition runs in families

    Risk factors

    • A mother or sister with endometriosis
    • Early first period, short cycles, and heavy or prolonged periods
    • Never having given birth, or a late first pregnancy
    • Anything that obstructs the outflow of menstrual blood, such as a congenital abnormality of the uterus or vagina
    • Reproductive age: the condition is rare before puberty, and symptoms usually improve after the menopause, although they do not always settle

    What a clinician may find

    • Tenderness on pelvic examination, particularly behind the uterus and over the uterosacral ligaments
    • Nodules felt in the posterior vaginal fornix in deep disease
    • A fixed, retroverted uterus, or an enlarged ovary where an endometrioma has formed
    • A normal examination, which does not rule endometriosis out

    Complications

    • Subfertility and infertility, from adhesions that distort the tubes and ovaries and from inflammation that impairs fertilisation and implantation
    • Chronic pelvic pain with a large effect on work, education, relationships and mental health
    • Ovarian endometriomas, which can rupture or twist the ovary (torsion)
    • Adhesions binding the bowel, bladder and uterus together; rarely bowel obstruction or blockage of a ureter
    • A small increase in the risk of some types of ovarian cancer, although the overall risk remains low

    Diagnosis

    Diagnosis starts with the pattern of symptoms: pain that follows the menstrual cycle, pain during or after sex, and cyclical bowel or urinary symptoms, together with an abdominal and pelvic examination. Transvaginal ultrasound is the first investigation and can show endometriomas and deep deposits in the bowel or bladder, but it often misses superficial disease on the peritoneum. MRI helps to map deep infiltrating disease before surgery. Laparoscopy, with a biopsy of the deposits seen, is the definitive test, although NICE advises that a normal examination, ultrasound or MRI should not be used to rule the condition out or to withhold treatment. There is no diagnostic blood test. A pregnancy test, urinalysis and a full blood count help to exclude pregnancy, urine infection and anaemia while the cause of pelvic pain is being worked out.

    Treatment

    Treatment depends on symptoms, age and whether pregnancy is wanted. Pain is managed first with paracetamol and a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen. Hormonal treatment aims to stop the deposits responding to the cycle: the combined hormonal contraceptive or a progestogen is offered first, and GnRH analogues, which produce a temporary menopause, are reserved for specialist use and are normally given with add-back hormone therapy to protect bone and limit menopausal symptoms. Some of these treatments, such as the combined hormonal contraceptive and contraceptive progestogens, also prevent pregnancy; others, including GnRH analogues, are not contraceptives, so contraception is discussed separately. Hormonal treatment does not have a lasting effect on fertility once it is stopped. Surgery is laparoscopic excision or ablation of the visible deposits, removal of endometriomas and division of adhesions; deep disease involving the bowel, bladder or ureter is managed by a specialist endometriosis centre. Hysterectomy, with or without removal of the ovaries, is a last resort for those who have completed their family; it is normally combined with excision of the deposits, it does not guarantee that pain will end, and hormone replacement therapy is discussed if the ovaries are removed before the menopause. Fertility treatment, pain management services and support groups such as Endometriosis UK are part of long-term care.

    Prevention

    There is no known way to prevent endometriosis. Hormonal contraception that makes periods lighter or stops them may reduce symptoms and slow the growth of deposits. Recognising the symptoms early, and taking period pain that disrupts life seriously rather than accepting it as normal, shortens the delay to diagnosis and treatment.

    Conditions

    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.
    • Ovarian cancer A malignant tumour of the ovary or, as is now recognised for many cases of the commonest type, of the adjacent fallopian tube.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.

    Symptoms

    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.

    Drug classes

    • Combined hormonal contraceptives Combined hormonal contraceptives contain both an oestrogen and a progestogen, and include the combined pill, a skin patch and a vaginal ring.

    Guides and news

    • Bleeding after menopause or between periods Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be checked by a GP.
    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • NICE: endometriosis tests in primary care NICE draft guidance proposes that two non-invasive tests for endometriosis could be used in NHS primary care while more evidence is gathered.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
    • Periods: what is normal and when to see a GP The NHS gives an average cycle of about 28 days.
