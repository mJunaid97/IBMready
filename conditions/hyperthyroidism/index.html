    Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism)

    Also known as: hyperthyroidism, thyrotoxicosis, Graves' disease

    Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for palpitations with breathlessness or chest pain, fever with confusion and agitation, or sudden vision change or eye pain. NHS and NIDDK advice is to see a GP for unexplained weight loss, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or persistent nervousness and tiredness, since untreated hyperthyroidism can cause serious heart problems.

    Overview

    Too much thyroxine speeds the body up: weight loss despite appetite, tremor, palpitations, sweating, anxiety and diarrhoea. Graves' disease can also inflame the eye muscles, pushing the eyes forward. Treatment blocks hormone production, destroys thyroid tissue with radioiodine, or removes the gland. Thyroid hormones regulate how the body uses energy, so an excess touches nearly every organ, including the rhythm of the heart. Older adults may present differently, with loss of appetite or withdrawal from people rather than restlessness, and the condition is sometimes mistaken for depression or dementia. Thyroid eye disease (Graves' ophthalmopathy), with dryness, redness, protrusion or double vision, affects a minority of people whose overactive thyroid is due to Graves' disease. The course depends on the cause: thyroiditis may settle without treatment as stored hormone is used up, whereas Graves' disease usually worsens over time if left untreated.

    Causes

    • Graves' disease (autoimmune, 75%)
    • Toxic multinodular goitre and toxic adenoma
    • Thyroiditis releasing stored hormone
    • Excess thyroxine tablets, amiodarone, iodine loads
    • Thyroiditis following a viral infection, some medicines or pregnancy
    • Rarely, a non-cancerous pituitary tumour; iodine contrast dye mainly matters when the thyroid already has a problem

    Risk factors

    • Female sex, age 20 to 50
    • Family history
    • Smoking (Graves' eye disease)
    • Other autoimmune disease
    • Recent pregnancy
    • Type 1 or type 2 diabetes
    • Primary adrenal insufficiency
    • Use of nicotine products

    Symptoms

    FatigueWeaknessShortness of breathDizzinessSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • Fast pulse, atrial fibrillation, tremor, warm moist skin
    • Goitre, sometimes with a bruit
    • Eye signs: lid retraction, protrusion, double vision
    • Weight loss, brisk reflexes
    • In older adults: apathy, loss of appetite and withdrawal rather than overactivity

    Complications

    • Atrial fibrillation and heart failure
    • Osteoporosis
    • Thyroid eye disease with vision loss
    • Thyroid storm (rare, life-threatening)
    • Underactive thyroid after radioiodine or surgery, needing lifelong levothyroxine

    Diagnosis

    Suppressed TSH with raised free T4 or T3; TSH-receptor antibodies confirm Graves'; a radioiodine uptake scan distinguishes causes when antibodies are negative. Assessment starts with the history and a blood test of thyroid function. If the blood test confirms overactivity, further tests identify the cause, because the cause determines treatment. The diagnosis is usually prompted by unexplained weight loss, a racing or irregular heartbeat, or persistent nervousness and tiredness.

    Treatment

    Beta blockers for symptoms; antithyroid drugs (carbimazole, propylthiouracil) for 12 to 18 months; radioiodine or thyroidectomy for relapse or large goitres, followed by levothyroxine replacement. Eye disease needs specialist care and stopping smoking. Antithyroid medicines reduce hormone production; radioactive iodine destroys thyroid cells; surgery removes part or all of the gland; and beta blockers such as propranolol ease a rapid heartbeat while definitive treatment takes effect. Radioactive iodine often leads to an underactive thyroid later, which then needs lifelong hormone replacement.

    Metoprolol and beta blockersLevothyroxine

    Prevention

    Not smoking reduces eye disease; no prevention for the autoimmune process itself.

    What does an overactive thyroid feel like?

    The symptoms of an overactive thyroid can appear gradually or suddenly, and they vary in how severe they are. People often notice nervousness, irritability, mood swings, poor sleep and persistent tiredness. Other changes include sensitivity to heat and a tremor. Some people also have swelling in the neck from an enlarged thyroid gland, called a goitre. Because many of these symptoms can have other causes, symptoms alone cannot confirm the diagnosis.

    In older adults the picture can look different, with loss of appetite and apathy rather than restlessness. For this reason the condition can be mistaken for depression or dementia in older people. The NHS advises seeing a GP for symptoms or signs of an overactive thyroid. A blood test of thyroid function is the usual first step.

    What causes an overactive thyroid?

    The most common cause is Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition. The NHS says about 4 in every 5 people with an overactive thyroid have Graves' disease. Other causes include thyroid nodules and inflammation of the gland called thyroiditis. The NHS says too much iodine, for example from medicines that contain it such as amiodarone, can also trigger an overactive thyroid. The NHS describes thyroiditis as swelling of the thyroid, which can cause extra thyroid hormones to be produced. A recent iodine load, such as from amiodarone or contrast dye, can make an uptake scan unreliable, so an antibody test may be used instead.

    Further tests can help to tell the causes apart. An antibody blood test can help identify Graves' disease. A radioactive iodine uptake scan can also help, with high uptake typical of Graves' disease and low uptake typical of thyroiditis.

    How is an overactive thyroid diagnosed?

    The starting point is a visit to a GP, who may arrange a thyroid function blood test. A diagnosis is based on the symptoms and the results of blood tests that assess how well the thyroid is working. These tests measure thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) and the thyroid hormones T3 and T4. A low TSH with a high T3 or T4 usually points to an overactive thyroid. A mild form with a low TSH but normal T3 and T4 is called subclinical hyperthyroidism. Thyroid UK says many cases settle by themselves, so regular TSH checks are needed. Thyroid UK also says an undetectable TSH carries a higher risk of progressing to overt hyperthyroidism. Other blood tests and a thyroid uptake scan may be used to find the cause.

    What are the treatments for an overactive thyroid?

    The NHS describes three main treatments: medicine, radioactive iodine treatment and surgery. Antithyroid medicines such as carbimazole and propylthiouracil are a common first choice. They are usually taken for more than a year, and the medicine is then stopped to see whether the condition returns. Some people need to continue the medicine for several years or possibly for life. Beta-blockers, such as propranolol or atenolol, can relieve symptoms such as tremor and a rapid heartbeat, but they are not suitable for people with asthma.

    Radioactive iodine is a highly effective treatment, and often a single dose is enough. It is not suitable for women who are pregnant or breastfeeding, or for people with severe eye problems caused by the condition. Surgery is reserved for cases such as a large goitre, severe eye problems, when other treatments cannot be used, or when symptoms return. Radioactive iodine often leads to an underactive thyroid, which is sometimes temporary but often permanent and needs long-term thyroid hormone medicine.

    Can an overactive thyroid affect the eyes?

    Eye problems, known as thyroid eye disease or Graves' ophthalmopathy, can occur in people whose overactive thyroid is caused by Graves' disease. NHS pages give different figures, from around 1 in 20 to around 1 in 4 people with Graves' disease. Symptoms can include dry and gritty eyes, red eyes, sensitivity to light, watering, blurred or double vision, and bulging eyes. Severe disease can cause vision loss, and treatments named by NHS inform include eye drops, sunglasses, corticosteroids, radiotherapy and surgery. Smoking increases the risk of thyroid eye disease, and quitting helps. Thyroid UK says eye problems can continue in people who keep smoking after their thyroid is treated.

    An NHS hospital leaflet advises getting urgent advice from a doctor if the eyes become more prominent and swollen, for example from the GP the same day or NHS 111.

    What about pregnancy?

    NIDDK says a woman with mild hyperthyroidism in pregnancy probably will not need treatment. Uncontrolled hyperthyroidism in pregnancy can lead to serious problems for the mother and the baby, according to NIDDK. NIDDK says more severe cases are typically treated with antithyroid medicines, most often propylthiouracil early in pregnancy. Radioactive iodine is not suitable during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and NIDDK says it can damage the baby's thyroid gland. NHS inform says becoming pregnant can lead to a relapse of symptoms, especially in someone with a history of Graves' disease. A UK hospital trust guideline suggests ultrasound scans in later pregnancy to watch the baby's growth when the mother has hyperthyroidism. Antibodies from a mother with Graves' disease can cross the placenta and affect the baby's thyroid function, according to a systematic review.

    What are the complications and outlook?

    An overactive thyroid is usually treatable, according to the NHS. The NHS says an overactive thyroid can raise the risk of atrial fibrillation, heart failure and osteoporosis, which weakens the bones. Atrial fibrillation is an irregular and often abnormally fast heart rhythm. Rarely, an undiagnosed or poorly controlled overactive thyroid can lead to thyroid storm, a serious, life-threatening condition. The NHS lists triggers such as infection, pregnancy, missed doses of medicine and physical damage to the thyroid. Warning signs include a rapid heartbeat, high temperature, confusion and loss of consciousness. Severe thyroid eye disease can lead to vision loss, and radioactive iodine can leave the thyroid underactive. Thyroid storm is an emergency, and anyone with these signs needs 999.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can an overactive thyroid get better without treatment?

    A mild form with a low TSH but normal hormone levels, called subclinical hyperthyroidism, is often monitored with regular TSH checks, and Thyroid UK says many cases settle by themselves. Other people are treated with medicine, radioactive iodine or surgery. Some need medicine for several years or possibly for life. A GP can arrange the thyroid blood test that starts the process.

    Is an overactive thyroid the same as an underactive thyroid?

    No, an overactive thyroid makes too much thyroid hormone, while an underactive thyroid means hormone levels are too low. Radioactive iodine treatment for an overactive thyroid often leads to an underactive thyroid, which is sometimes temporary but often permanent. A permanent underactive thyroid needs long-term thyroid hormone medicine.

    When is an overactive thyroid an emergency?

    Thyroid storm is rare but life-threatening, and can occur when an overactive thyroid is undiagnosed or poorly controlled. Its signs include a rapid heartbeat, high temperature, confusion and loss of consciousness. Anyone with these signs needs an emergency response, so call 999.

    Who cannot have radioactive iodine treatment?

    NHS hospital guidance says radioactive iodine is not suitable in pregnancy or breastfeeding. It is also not suitable where eye disease is severe. Surgery is reserved for cases such as a large goitre, severe eye problems, when other treatments cannot be used, or when symptoms return.

    Can an overactive thyroid be mistaken for something else?

    Yes, many of its symptoms can have other causes, so symptoms alone cannot confirm it. In older adults it can be mistaken for depression or dementia. The symptoms can appear gradually or suddenly and vary in how severe they are. A thyroid function blood test helps to show whether a thyroid problem is responsible.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Bipolar disorder Bipolar disorder is a mental health condition that causes extreme mood changes, with high moods (mania or hypomania) and low moods (depression).
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.
