    Pancreatitis

    Also known as: acute pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis

    Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland, chronic when repeated injury leads to scarring and loss of function.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for severe constant upper abdominal pain radiating to the back with vomiting, especially after alcohol or with known gallstones.

    Open in the 3D explorerPancreas anatomy

    Overview

    Gallstones and alcohol cause four in five cases of acute pancreatitis. Activated enzymes digest the gland and surrounding fat, and severe cases trigger a body-wide inflammatory response with organ failure. Most attacks are mild and settle in days; chronic pancreatitis causes lasting pain, malabsorption and diabetes. Acute pancreatitis is short-term and often resolves within days with treatment; chronic pancreatitis worsens over time, causes lasting damage and can follow one or more acute attacks. In an acute attack, upper abdominal pain spreads to the back with nausea and vomiting; fever and a fast pulse are common and, in severe cases, blood pressure falls, dehydration develops and organ failure, internal bleeding or death can follow. Chronic disease presents differently, with nausea, weight loss and oily stools, because digestive enzymes such as trypsin no longer reach the gut in adequate amounts. A gallstone lodging in the common bile duct blocks pancreatic drainage; alcohol is the leading cause of chronic disease.

    Causes

    • Gallstones blocking the duct
    • Alcohol
    • High triglycerides, high calcium
    • Some drugs (azathioprine, valproate, thiazides)
    • After ERCP
    • Viral infections, trauma, genetic mutations
    • Cystic fibrosis and other inherited disorders
    • Autoimmune pancreatitis
    • Injury or surgery to the pancreas; infections such as mumps; lupus

    Risk factors

    • Heavy drinking
    • Gallstones
    • Smoking (chronic pancreatitis)
    • Obesity

    Symptoms

    Abdominal painNauseaVomitingFever

    What a clinician may find

    • Severe epigastric tenderness
    • Fast pulse, low blood pressure in severe disease
    • Bruising of the flanks or around the navel in haemorrhagic cases
    • Jaundice with a duct stone
    • Dehydration
    • Weight loss and oily stools in chronic disease

    Complications

    • Pancreatic necrosis and infection
    • Pseudocysts
    • Organ failure and death in severe attacks
    • Diabetes and malabsorption in chronic disease
    • Pancreatic cancer risk in chronic pancreatitis
    • Internal bleeding in severe attacks
    • Dehydration and low blood pressure

    Diagnosis

    Typical pain with a serum lipase or amylase more than three times normal; ultrasound for gallstones; CT after 48 to 72 hours if severe to assess necrosis; blood tests to score severity. In NHS practice acute pancreatitis is usually diagnosed from blood tests, with a CT scan in some cases, and further tests are then run to identify the underlying cause so it can be treated.

    Treatment

    Intravenous fluids, pain relief, early feeding when tolerated; ERCP to remove a duct stone with cholangitis; antibiotics and drainage for infected necrosis; cholecystectomy before discharge for gallstone pancreatitis. Chronic disease: stop alcohol and smoking, enzyme supplements, pain management, diabetes care. Treatment in hospital includes fluids given into a vein, nutritional support, painkillers, and antibiotics if an infection develops; most people start to improve within a week. Early feeding is favoured because early enteral nutrition has been shown to improve outcomes. For gallstone pancreatitis the gallbladder is removed during the same admission or soon afterwards to limit the risk of recurrence; alcohol-related disease improves with stopping alcohol and treatment for dependency. Surgery is reserved for complications.

    Prevention

    Moderate alcohol, treat gallstones once symptomatic, control triglycerides, stop smoking.

    Conditions

    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Pancreatic cancer Pancreatic cancer is a cancer that starts in the pancreas, an organ that helps with digestion.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.

    Biomarkers

    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) A membrane enzyme of bile-duct and liver cells that rises with cholestasis, alcohol and many medicines.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Drug classes

    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).
