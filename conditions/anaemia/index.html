    Anaemia

    Also known as: anemia, iron deficiency anaemia, low haemoglobin

    A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for breathlessness at rest, chest pain, fainting, or black or bloody stools; routine review for persistent tiredness and pallor. NHS guidance is that anyone with symptoms of anaemia should see their GP, who can usually diagnose it with a simple blood test; untreated iron deficiency can lead to heart problems, complications in pregnancy and delayed development in children.

    Overview

    Anaemia is a sign, not a diagnosis. Iron deficiency from blood loss or poor intake is the commonest cause worldwide; others include vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, chronic disease, kidney failure, inherited haemoglobin disorders and bone marrow failure. Finding the cause matters because in adults it can be bleeding from a bowel cancer. Haemoglobin inside red cells carries oxygen, so a low concentration leaves tissues short of oxygen and the heart working harder to compensate. How much a person notices depends largely on how quickly the anaemia develops: a slow fall from chronic blood loss may cause few symptoms until it is marked, whereas a rapid fall is felt early. Iron deficiency arises when more blood and iron are lost than the body can replace, when iron is poorly absorbed (as in coeliac disease or Crohn's disease), when the diet is low in iron, or when needs rise, as in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Mild or moderate iron deficiency may produce no symptoms at all and is often picked up on a routine blood count.

    Causes

    • Iron deficiency: heavy periods, gut bleeding, poor diet, malabsorption, pregnancy
    • Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency
    • Chronic inflammation or kidney disease
    • Thalassaemia and sickle cell disease
    • Haemolysis
    • Bone marrow disease
    • Malabsorption from coeliac disease or Crohn's disease
    • Increased requirement in pregnancy and breastfeeding

    Risk factors

    • Menstruation and pregnancy
    • Vegetarian or restricted diets
    • Age over 65
    • Chronic disease
    • Regular NSAID or aspirin use

    Symptoms

    FatigueShortness of breathDizzinessWeaknessTingling (pins and needles)

    What a clinician may find

    • Pale conjunctivae and palms
    • Fast pulse, flow murmur
    • Spoon nails, sore tongue in iron deficiency
    • Jaundice in haemolysis
    • Numb feet and unsteadiness in B12 deficiency
    • Reported cravings for non-food items (pica), hair loss, headache or tinnitus in iron deficiency

    Complications

    • Heart failure in severe anaemia
    • Poor pregnancy outcomes
    • Delayed development in children
    • Missed underlying cancer

    Diagnosis

    Full blood count is the starting point: a low haemoglobin with a reduced mean cell volume (MCV) is the usual first finding in iron deficiency, and red cell size separates the main groups of cause. Serum ferritin reflects iron stores and confirms iron deficiency; B12, folate, reticulocytes and a blood film narrow down other causes. Because anaemia is a sign rather than a diagnosis, the search for the cause matters as much as the count: coeliac tests, and endoscopy or colonoscopy to look for a bleeding source in men and in women past the menopause. A bone marrow examination is reserved for cases where the diagnosis remains unclear after these tests. During treatment, blood tests are repeated every few months to confirm that the haemoglobin is rising.

    Treatment

    Treat the cause; oral iron for three months after correction (or intravenous iron if not tolerated), B12 injections or folate, erythropoietin in kidney disease, transfusion only for severe or symptomatic anaemia. Oral iron tablets are the standard first step and are usually effective; they commonly cause gastrointestinal side effects, and taking them with meals eases this at the cost of reduced absorption. Treatment continues for about three months after the haemoglobin has returned to normal so that iron stores are replenished, with follow-up blood tests to check the response. Intravenous iron is used when tablets are not tolerated or not absorbed. Correcting the count without finding and treating the cause leaves the problem to recur.

    Prevention

    Iron-rich diet with vitamin C, treating heavy periods, iron supplements in pregnancy where advised, avoiding unnecessary NSAIDs. Groups at higher risk, such as women with heavy periods, pregnant women, people on restricted diets and older people, benefit from attention to dietary iron and early testing when symptoms appear.

    What does anaemia feel like?

    Many people with iron deficiency anaemia have only a few symptoms. How much a person notices depends on how quickly the anaemia develops, and iron deficiency can be mild enough to cause no symptoms. Tiredness and a lack of energy are common, and some people also notice shortness of breath, a pounding heartbeat, paler skin or headaches. Less often there may be ringing in the ears, an altered sense of taste, hair loss, a craving to eat non-food items (called pica) or spoon-shaped nails. Low iron has also been linked with restless legs syndrome, even when anaemia is not fully present.

    Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency anaemia can cause tiredness, a sore red tongue, pins and needles and mouth ulcers. It can also cause muscle weakness and problems with vision, memory or judgement. Some of these problems, especially those affecting the nervous system, can become permanent without treatment, and long-term B12 deficiency can damage nerves. The NHS says body stores of B12 can last for a few years, so problems may take a long time to appear.

    What are the main types of anaemia?

    Iron deficiency is the most common type of anaemia, and vitamin B12 and folate deficiency can also cause it. MedlinePlus also lists anaemia of chronic disease, sickle cell anaemia, thalassaemia and aplastic anaemia among the types. Anaemia of chronic disease is linked with long-term illnesses. Sickle cell anaemia is inherited and involves abnormal haemoglobin that changes the shape of red blood cells. Thalassaemia is inherited, while aplastic anaemia involves damage to the stem cells in the bone marrow that make blood cells.

    In the UK, the most common cause of vitamin B12 deficiency is pernicious anaemia. In this condition the immune system attacks healthy cells in the stomach, so the body cannot absorb B12 from food. Diet can also contribute, particularly for people who eat little or no animal-source food, such as vegans, and some medicines, including anticonvulsants and proton pump inhibitors, can reduce absorption. Folate deficiency can also follow alcohol misuse.

    What happens at an appointment, and which tests are used?

    A GP usually asks about lifestyle and medical history and then arranges a blood test. This is a full blood count, which checks the red blood cells, and the GP may also test ferritin, a protein that stores iron. A low ferritin suggests that there is not much iron stored in the body. The NHS advises seeing a GP if iron deficiency anaemia is suspected, and diagnosis relies on blood tests.

    Finding the cause matters because treatment depends on it. Heavy periods and medicines such as ibuprofen or aspirin, which can cause bleeding in the stomach, are among the causes the GP considers. In men and in women after the menopause, bleeding in the gut is the most common cause of iron deficiency, whereas in women before the menopause it is heavy periods. If the cause is unclear, further tests may follow, such as a gastroscopy or colonoscopy to look for a source of bleeding, and testing for coeliac disease may be suggested.

    How is anaemia treated?

    Treatment depends on the type of anaemia and its cause. Iron deficiency anaemia is usually treated with iron tablets and iron-rich foods, while the underlying cause, such as heavy periods or bleeding, is also addressed. Common side effects of iron tablets include constipation or diarrhoea, tummy pain, heartburn, feeling sick and darker than usual poo. Taking tablets with or shortly after food can help, and a GP may adjust the tablets if side effects are troublesome. An iron infusion may be used when a person cannot take tablets or cannot absorb iron through the gut.

    Vitamin B12 deficiency is usually treated with hydroxocobalamin injections, followed by tablets or continuing injections depending on the cause. When the deficiency is not caused by diet, injections may be needed long term. Folate deficiency is treated with folic acid tablets, and doctors check B12 first because folic acid can sometimes hide a B12 deficiency. After iron treatment, a follow-up blood test is used to check the response. MedlinePlus says anaemia of chronic disease may improve when the disease causing it is treated.

    Living with anaemia and what to expect

    Vitamin B12 is found in meat, fish, eggs, dairy products and fortified foods. Good sources of folate include broccoli, brussels sprouts, asparagus, peas, chickpeas and brown rice. NHS 111 Wales notes that an iron overdose in a young child can be fatal, which is why iron tablets are kept out of children's reach.

    NHS 111 Wales says most people with iron deficiency anaemia respond to dietary changes and iron tablets. Untreated iron deficiency anaemia may lead to heart problems, complications in pregnancy and delayed development in children. Delays in treating B12 deficiency can cause lasting nerve damage. Vitamin B12 deficiency anaemia most often responds well to treatment. Overall, the outlook depends on the condition causing the anaemia.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is anaemia the same as iron deficiency?

    No, iron deficiency is only the most common type. Anaemia can also come from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, long-term conditions, inherited conditions such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia, or aplastic anaemia. Treatment depends on the type and cause.

    Why do doctors look for the cause of iron deficiency?

    Finding the cause matters because treatment depends on it. In men and in women after the menopause, bleeding in the gut is the most common cause. Doctors may arrange a gastroscopy or colonoscopy to look for a source of bleeding, including a cancer, if the reason is unclear.

    Do iron tablets have side effects?

    They can, and the NHS lists constipation or diarrhoea, tummy pain, heartburn, feeling sick and darker than usual poo. Taking them with or shortly after food can help. A GP may adjust the tablets if the side effects are troublesome. An iron infusion may be used when a person cannot take tablets or cannot absorb iron through the gut.

    Why might vitamin B12 be given by injection?

    Injections are used when the problem is poor absorption, as in pernicious anaemia, where the immune system attacks healthy stomach cells. The NHS says vitamin B12 injections are called hydroxocobalamin. When low B12 is only due to diet, tablets may be enough.

    Does anaemia always cause symptoms?

    No, many people with iron deficiency anaemia have only a few symptoms. Sources note that iron deficiency can be mild enough to cause no symptoms, so a blood test is the reliable way to confirm it. The NHS advises seeing a GP for anyone who thinks they may have iron deficiency anaemia.

    Conditions

    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Coeliac disease Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system reacts to gluten and damages the lining of the small bowel.
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency anaemia A lack of vitamin B12 or folate that affects the body's ability to make fully working red blood cells.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.
    • Vitamin B12 and folate Two blood tests, usually ordered together, for the vitamins the bone marrow needs to make normal red cells and the nervous system needs to keep…

    Biomarkers

    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Folate The B vitamin from green vegetables and fortified foods that cells need to make DNA; deficiency causes anaemia with large red cells and.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Vitamin B12 The vitamin, absorbed with intrinsic factor in the last part of the small intestine.

    Procedures

    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.

    Medications

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
