    Asthma

    Also known as: bronchial asthma

    A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care if the reliever is not helping, the person cannot speak in sentences, lips are blue, or peak flow is below a third of best.

    Open in the 3D explorerTrachea & bronchial tree anatomy

    Overview

    In asthma the bronchial lining is chronically inflamed and over-reactive. Triggers such as allergens, viruses, exercise or cold air make the smooth muscle constrict and the lining swell and pour out mucus. Between attacks breathing may be normal. It affects about one child in ten and one adult in twelve.

    Causes

    • Genetic susceptibility plus environmental exposure: allergens (dust mite, pollen, pets), early viral infections, tobacco smoke, air pollution, occupational chemicals

    Risk factors

    • Family history of asthma or allergy
    • Eczema and hay fever
    • Smoking or exposure to smoke
    • Obesity
    • Prematurity

    Symptoms

    Shortness of breathCoughChest pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Widespread wheeze on breathing out
    • Fast breathing, use of accessory muscles in an attack
    • A silent chest and exhaustion in a life-threatening attack

    Complications

    • Severe attacks needing hospital care
    • Airway remodelling and fixed narrowing
    • Side effects of repeated steroid courses

    Diagnosis

    Variable symptoms plus evidence of variable airflow obstruction: spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility, peak-flow variability, or raised exhaled nitric oxide and blood eosinophils.

    Treatment

    Inhaled corticosteroids are the foundation, combined with a long-acting bronchodilator as needed; a reliever inhaler for symptoms; a written action plan; trigger avoidance; biologic injections for severe allergic or eosinophilic asthma. Attacks are treated with high-dose reliever, oral steroids and oxygen.

    Prevention

    Avoid smoking and smoke exposure, control allergens, have flu vaccination, use the preventer inhaler daily and check inhaler technique.

    What are the symptoms and triggers of asthma?

    The NHS lists coughing, wheezing, chest tightness and breathlessness as the main symptoms of asthma. They are often worse at night and early in the morning, or when a trigger is present. Asthma involves swelling, called inflammation, of the breathing tubes that carry air in and out of the lungs.

    The NHS says it is not fully understood what causes asthma. Risk factors it lists include a family history of asthma or atopic conditions (conditions linked to allergy), childhood exposure to tobacco smoke, and being born prematurely or at low birth weight.

    The NHS lists common triggers such as infections like colds and flu, allergens such as pollen, dust mites and animals, smoke, pollution, weather, mould or damp, and exercise. Some cases are linked to substances in the workplace, which is called occupational asthma.

    Asthma + Lung UK says frequent use of a reliever inhaler is a sign that asthma is not well controlled, and advises speaking to a GP, nurse or pharmacist.

    How is asthma diagnosed?

    A GP will typically ask about symptoms and triggers, and may ask about eczema, allergies or a family history of them. There is no single test for asthma. A diagnosis is made from the symptom history and the results of breathing tests.

    The NHS lists FeNO, spirometry and peak flow among the main tests. In a FeNO test a person breathes into a machine that measures nitric oxide in the breath, which is a sign of inflammation in the lungs. Spirometry measures airflow and lung volume. Peak flow testing uses a handheld device over a few weeks.

    The UK guideline from NICE, BTS and SIGN sets out an order of tests. For children aged 5 to 16 with a history suggestive of asthma, FeNO comes first, followed by spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility. Peak flow measurements over a period of time are used if spirometry is unavailable or delayed.

    What are the treatments for asthma?

    The NHS says there is no cure for asthma, but treatment can help control the symptoms. Inhalers are the main treatment. Reliever inhalers are used when symptoms occur. Preventer inhalers are taken daily to reduce inflammation in the airways.

    Asthma + Lung UK explains that a blue reliever inhaler opens the airways but contains no steroid, so it cannot reduce inflammation. The charity says NICE guidelines recommend that no one with asthma should have only a blue reliever inhaler, and that anyone using one also needs a steroid preventer.

    Some people use a single combination inhaler called an anti-inflammatory reliever. It contains a steroid and a reliever medicine and is taken only when symptoms appear.

    The NHS says tablets may be added if inhalers alone are not controlling symptoms. If asthma is severe and not controlled by inhalers and tablets, injected medicines called biological therapies may be offered. Only an asthma specialist can prescribe them, and they suit only specific types of severe asthma.

    Living with asthma: action plans, reviews and outlook

    The NHS lists useful steps such as always carrying an inhaler, avoiding known triggers and having vaccinations such as the flu vaccine. A person with asthma will usually have a personal asthma action plan.

    Asthma + Lung UK says an action plan helps a person understand their symptoms and what to do if they get worse. It is arranged in traffic-light sections. Green covers everyday care, amber covers worsening symptoms, and red covers the signs of an asthma attack, what to do, when to call 999 and when to contact the GP afterwards.

    The NHS says steroid tablets may be used as a short course during an attack, or daily over the long term only in very severe asthma. Long-term or frequent use can cause side effects, including fragile bones (osteoporosis), so people are monitored regularly.

    MedlinePlus says there is no cure, but with self-care and medical treatment most people can live normal lives. It adds that symptoms sometimes ease over time, especially in children, and that attacks can be life-threatening and are more common in people with severe asthma.

    What happens in an asthma attack, and when should I call 999?

    The NHS describes an asthma attack as having severe symptoms and difficulty breathing, which can be life-threatening. NHS 111 Wales lists a reliever inhaler that is not helping, and breathlessness that stops a person speaking, eating or sleeping, as signs of an attack.

    NHS 111 Wales advises sitting up, trying to stay calm and taking puffs of the reliever inhaler. It says to call 999 for an ambulance if a person feels worse at any point, or does not feel better after taking the puffs.

    NHS inform says to call 999 or go to A&E for severe breathing difficulty, such as struggling to speak without pausing, gasping or choking. NHS information also says to contact a GP urgently if a person is more short of breath than usual despite using inhalers as prescribed.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can asthma be cured?

    The NHS says there is no cure for asthma, but treatment can help control the symptoms. MedlinePlus says symptoms sometimes ease over time, especially in children, and that with self-care and medical treatment most people can live normal lives. Inhalers are the main treatment.

    What is the difference between a reliever and a preventer inhaler?

    Reliever inhalers are used when symptoms occur and quickly relax the airways to make breathing easier. Preventer inhalers are taken daily to reduce inflammation in the airways. Asthma + Lung UK says a blue reliever inhaler contains no steroid, so it cannot reduce inflammation, and that anyone using one also needs a steroid preventer.

    What tests are used to diagnose asthma?

    The NHS says there is no single test for asthma. A diagnosis is made from the symptom history and the results of breathing tests. The tests it lists are FeNO, which measures nitric oxide in the breath, spirometry, which measures airflow and lung volume, and peak flow testing with a handheld device over a few weeks.

    What is an asthma action plan?

    An asthma action plan helps a person understand their symptoms and what to do if they get worse. Asthma + Lung UK describes green sections for everyday care, amber for worsening symptoms and red for an asthma attack. The red section covers the signs of an attack, what to do, when to call 999 and when to contact the GP afterwards.

    When does an asthma attack need a 999 call?

    NHS inform says to call 999 or go to A&E for severe breathing difficulty, such as struggling to speak without pausing, gasping or choking. NHS 111 Wales says to call 999 for an ambulance if a person feels worse at any point, or does not feel better after taking puffs of the reliever inhaler.

    Conditions

    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.
    • Atopic eczema A skin condition that causes dry, itchy skin and comes and goes in flare-ups.
    • Food allergy and anaphylaxis A food allergy is when the immune system overreacts to certain foods, with reactions that range from mild to life-threatening anaphylaxis.
    • Hay fever (allergic rhinitis) Hay fever is an allergic reaction to pollen, also called seasonal allergic rhinitis.
    • Flu (influenza) Flu is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea Obstructive sleep apnoea is a condition in which the throat narrows or closes during sleep, so breathing stops and starts again.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Chlorphenamine A first-generation antihistamine, sold over the counter, that relieves hay fever.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Montelukast Montelukast is a prescription medicine that helps prevent asthma symptoms.
