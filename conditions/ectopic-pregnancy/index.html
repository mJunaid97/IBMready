    Ectopic pregnancy

    Also known as: tubal pregnancy, extrauterine pregnancy, ruptured ectopic pregnancy, ectopic

    A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube, where it cannot develop normally and can rupture the tissue around it.

    Overview

    After fertilisation in the ampulla of the fallopian tube, the embryo normally travels into the uterus and implants in the endometrium about a week later. In an ectopic pregnancy it implants elsewhere: most often in the tube itself, less commonly in the interstitial part of the tube where it enters the uterus, in the cervix, an ovary, a previous caesarean scar or the abdominal cavity. The NHS estimates that around 1 in every 90 pregnancies in the UK is ectopic. Symptoms usually appear between about the fourth and twelfth week of pregnancy, often in the first weeks after a missed period and sometimes before the pregnancy is known. The tube cannot stretch or support a placenta, so as the pregnancy grows it bleeds and can tear through the tubal wall, causing heavy bleeding into the abdomen. An ectopic pregnancy cannot be moved into the uterus and cannot survive, and a ruptured ectopic pregnancy is a life-threatening emergency. It can occur in anyone who can become pregnant, including trans men and non-binary people with a uterus and tubes, and should be considered in anyone of reproductive age with pelvic pain or bleeding until pregnancy has been excluded.

    Causes

    • Implantation of the fertilised egg outside the uterine cavity, usually because damage or narrowing of the fallopian tube slows or blocks the embryo's passage to the uterus
    • Scarring of the tube after pelvic inflammatory disease, most often from chlamydia or gonorrhoea
    • Previous surgery on the tubes, including sterilisation and its reversal, and a previous ectopic pregnancy
    • Impaired movement of the tube and its cilia, which smoking and hormonal factors are thought to affect
    • In many cases no cause is found

    Risk factors

    • A previous ectopic pregnancy
    • Pelvic inflammatory disease or a history of sexually transmitted infection
    • Surgery on the fallopian tubes, including sterilisation, or other pelvic or abdominal surgery
    • Becoming pregnant with an intrauterine device or system in place, or while taking the progestogen-only pill: these methods make pregnancy itself unlikely, but a pregnancy that does happen is more likely to be ectopic
    • Fertility treatment such as IVF
    • Endometriosis
    • Smoking
    • Increasing age, particularly over 35

    Abdominal painPelvic painUnusual vaginal bleedingDizzinessNausea

    What a clinician may find

    • Tenderness, sometimes with guarding, in one side of the lower abdomen
    • Tenderness on moving the cervix or beside the uterus on pelvic examination, sometimes with a tender mass
    • Pain in the tip of the shoulder, from blood in the abdomen irritating the diaphragm, which points to internal bleeding
    • Pallor, a fast pulse, low blood pressure and collapse when the tube has ruptured
    • Vaginal bleeding that is often lighter, darker and more watery than a period

    Complications

    • Rupture of the tube with heavy bleeding into the abdomen, shock and, if not treated quickly, death
    • Loss of the affected fallopian tube, or damage to it after surgery that keeps the tube
    • Reduced fertility and a higher chance of another ectopic pregnancy in a later pregnancy
    • Persistent trophoblast tissue after salpingotomy or medical treatment, needing further treatment
    • Rhesus sensitisation in a rhesus-negative woman if anti-D is not given after surgery
    • Grief and anxiety about future pregnancies; the loss is a bereavement as well as an emergency

    Diagnosis

    Ectopic pregnancy is suspected in anyone who could be pregnant and has lower abdominal or pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding or unexplained faintness, so a pregnancy test comes first. If it is positive, a transvaginal ultrasound scan in an early pregnancy unit looks for a pregnancy inside the uterus; it may instead show a mass beside the ovary, occasionally with a heartbeat, or free fluid behind the uterus that indicates bleeding. When neither an intrauterine nor an ectopic pregnancy is seen, the situation is called a pregnancy of unknown location: serum hCG is measured and repeated 48 hours later, when a rise of about two thirds or more over 48 hours makes a developing pregnancy in the uterus more likely, though it does not exclude an ectopic and a scan is still arranged; a falling level suggests a pregnancy that is failing, and a slow rise or a plateau raises suspicion of an ectopic. NICE advises interpreting the result alongside the clinical picture rather than on its own. A full blood count and the blood group are checked, and miscarriage, a bleeding or twisted ovarian cyst, appendicitis and urinary infection are considered. Anyone who is shocked or in severe pain is treated as having a rupture without waiting for further tests.

    Treatment

    Treatment follows NICE guidance and depends on how unwell the person is, the size of the ectopic pregnancy and the hCG level. Expectant management, with repeated hCG measurements until the level falls to normal, suits a small unruptured ectopic pregnancy with a low and falling hCG in someone who has little or no pain and can return for follow-up. Medical treatment is an injection of methotrexate, which stops the trophoblast cells dividing so that the pregnancy is reabsorbed; blood tests continue until hCG is undetectable, and pregnancy is avoided for some months afterwards as the team advises. Surgery, almost always laparoscopic, is used for significant pain, a larger mass, a high hCG, a visible heartbeat or rupture: salpingectomy removes the affected tube, while salpingotomy keeps a tube when the other one is damaged, at the cost of residual tissue that needs further monitoring. Rupture is an emergency treated with resuscitation, transfusion if needed and immediate surgery, and rhesus-negative women who have surgery are offered anti-D immunoglobulin. The loss is a bereavement as well as an emergency, and most women conceive again, even after a tube has been removed.

    Prevention

    Most ectopic pregnancies cannot be prevented, but the risk can be lowered by reducing the chance of tubal damage: using condoms to protect against chlamydia and other sexually transmitted infections, being tested and treated promptly if infection is suspected, and not smoking. Anyone who has had an ectopic pregnancy, tubal surgery or pelvic inflammatory disease should tell their GP or midwife as soon as they know they are pregnant so that an early scan can confirm that the pregnancy is in the uterus.

    When to seek care

    Anyone who could be pregnant and has one-sided lower abdominal or pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding or brown watery discharge, or pain in the tip of the shoulder should contact a GP, NHS 111 or an early pregnancy unit the same day, even if no period has been missed yet. Call 999 or go to A&E for sudden severe abdominal pain, feeling faint or collapsing, a racing pulse, or looking very pale and feeling sick and sweaty with pain, as these can mean the tube has ruptured and internal bleeding has started.
