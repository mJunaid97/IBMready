    Polycystic ovary syndrome

    Also known as: PCOS, polycystic ovaries, polycystic ovarian syndrome, Stein-Leventhal syndrome, polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome, PMOS

    A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes, usually in higher amounts in men) than usual and release eggs irregularly or not at all, often with many small immature follicles visible on the ovaries.

    When to seek care

    See a GP for periods that are infrequent, absent or very irregular, excess facial or body hair, persistent acne, unexplained weight gain, or difficulty conceiving after a year of trying. Seek prompt assessment for signs of rapid virilisation, such as a deepening voice or hair growth that develops over a few months, which suggest a different cause. Heavy, prolonged bleeding with dizziness or faintness needs urgent care.

    Overview

    Polycystic ovary syndrome is one of the commonest endocrine conditions in women of reproductive age, affecting about 1 in 10 women in the UK, and anyone with ovaries can have it, including trans men and non-binary people. The name misleads: the so-called cysts are small follicles that have stalled before ovulation rather than true cysts, and some people with the syndrome have normal-looking ovaries. Insulin resistance is central. High insulin levels push the ovaries to make androgens and lower the liver's output of sex hormone-binding globulin, so more testosterone is free to act. The excess androgen disrupts the maturing of follicles and causes acne and excess hair, while irregular ovulation leads to infrequent periods and subfertility. PCOS is also a metabolic condition, with a raised long-term risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and abnormal blood lipids, and it is linked with low mood and sleep apnoea. Features usually begin in the late teens or early twenties and vary widely in severity from one person to another.

    Causes

    • The exact cause is unknown; PCOS is thought to result from a combination of genetic and environmental factors
    • Insulin resistance: the body's cells respond poorly to insulin, so more is produced, and the high insulin level stimulates the ovaries to make androgens and lowers the liver's output of sex hormone-binding globulin, raising free testosterone
    • Altered signalling from the pituitary, with relatively high luteinising hormone (LH), which further drives androgen production by the ovary
    • Excess androgen stops follicles maturing to ovulation, so they accumulate as small follicles around the edge of the ovary
    • Family history: PCOS and type 2 diabetes cluster in families

    Risk factors

    • A mother or sister with PCOS
    • Family history of type 2 diabetes
    • Being overweight or gaining weight, which worsens insulin resistance and symptoms but does not cause the condition on its own
    • Insulin resistance from any cause
    • Reproductive age: features usually appear in the years after puberty

    Symptoms

    Heavy periodsUnusual vaginal bleedingFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Hirsutism: coarse, dark hair on the face, chest, abdomen or back in a male pattern
    • Acne and oily skin, and thinning of scalp hair in a male pattern
    • Acanthosis nigricans: dark, velvety thickening of the skin of the neck, armpits and groin, a marker of insulin resistance
    • Raised body weight, particularly around the waist
    • Enlarged ovaries with many small follicles on ultrasound

    Complications

    • Type 2 diabetes, and gestational diabetes in pregnancy
    • Raised cardiovascular risk: high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol and central weight gain
    • Thickening of the lining of the uterus (endometrial hyperplasia) and, over years, endometrial cancer, because infrequent periods leave the lining exposed to oestrogen without the regular shedding that progesterone brings
    • Subfertility from irregular or absent ovulation, and a higher risk of miscarriage and complications in pregnancy
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea, fatty liver disease, depression and anxiety

    Diagnosis

    PCOS is diagnosed on the Rotterdam criteria when two of three features are present and other causes have been excluded: infrequent or absent ovulation, shown by irregular or absent periods; clinical or biochemical signs of excess androgen, meaning hirsutism, acne or a raised testosterone or free androgen index on blood tests; and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. Ultrasound is unnecessary if the first two are present, and it is not used to make the diagnosis in adolescents, in whom ovaries with many follicles are normal. Blood tests measure testosterone, sex hormone-binding globulin, LH and FSH, and check prolactin and thyroid function, because thyroid disease, a raised prolactin, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome and androgen-secreting tumours can mimic the syndrome; a very high testosterone level or rapid virilisation points to a tumour rather than PCOS. Once the diagnosis is made, blood glucose or HbA1c, a lipid profile, blood pressure and weight are checked and then reviewed regularly.

    Treatment

    There is no cure, so treatment is matched to the symptoms that matter most to the person and to the long-term risks. Weight loss where weight is raised, and regular exercise, improve insulin resistance, ovulation and androgen symptoms and are the foundation of care. The combined hormonal contraceptive pill regulates bleeding and reduces acne and hirsutism; if it is unsuitable, a cyclical progestogen is used to bring on a regular withdrawal bleed, or a hormonal intrauterine system is used to keep the lining thin; either protects the endometrium in anyone who has only a few periods a year. Periods that are infrequent do not mean pregnancy is impossible, so contraception is still needed if pregnancy is not wanted. Metformin can improve insulin resistance and cycle regularity and is used in some people, usually alongside lifestyle change. For fertility, ovulation is induced with letrozole or clomifene under specialist care, with metformin, laparoscopic ovarian drilling or IVF as further options. Excess hair is managed with hair removal, eflornithine cream or, under specialist advice, an anti-androgen, and acne is treated in the usual ways. Screening for diabetes and cardiovascular risk continues lifelong, and mood and sleep problems are asked about and treated.

    Prevention

    PCOS cannot be prevented, but its complications largely can. Keeping to a healthy weight, staying active and eating well reduce insulin resistance and the risk of diabetes and heart disease; regular checks of blood glucose, blood pressure and lipids pick up problems early; and making sure the lining of the uterus is shed regularly, whether by natural periods or a progestogen, protects against endometrial cancer.

    What are the symptoms of PCOS?

    Irregular or infrequent periods, extra hair on the face or body (hirsutism) and difficulty getting pregnant are among the most commonly listed signs. Acne, oily skin, weight gain, particularly around the waist, and thinning scalp hair also appear on some lists.

    NHS leaflets say women with PCOS may ovulate infrequently or not at all, which can make it harder to get pregnant. An NHS hospital leaflet says depression and mood swings can also occur.

    Symptoms vary a lot. NHS hospital leaflets say some women have mild symptoms, while others are affected more severely by a wider range of symptoms. NHS inform now titles its page on the condition polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) and describes it as previously known as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

    How is PCOS diagnosed?

    Diagnosis commonly uses the Rotterdam criteria: at least two of three features (irregular or absent periods, signs of raised male hormones, and polycystic-looking ovaries on a scan), after other causes have been ruled out. A scan is not always needed: when irregular periods and signs of raised male hormones are both present, an ultrasound is not necessary for diagnosis.

    A leaflet from an NHS trust says polycystic ovaries on their own do not need treatment. One NHS leaflet says up to 1 in 4 young women have polycystic-looking ovaries on a scan, and another NHS trust leaflet says polycystic ovaries by themselves do not need treatment.

    A 2025 all-party parliamentary group inquiry into PCOS reported significant gaps in diagnosis, treatment and long-term support.

    How is PCOS treated?

    The NHS says there is no cure for PCOS, but the symptoms can be treated. NHS leaflets say losing weight can help people who are overweight, with more regular periods and less acne and excess hair over time.

    NHS leaflets say the combined contraceptive pill can be used to regulate periods and ease symptoms driven by male hormones, and that metformin is sometimes used off-label. Fertility medicines are available, and cosmetic hair treatments are also used for excess hair growth.

    PCOS and fertility

    PCOS can disrupt ovulation, which can make it harder to conceive.

    NHS information says medicines such as clomifene can be used to trigger ovulation in women who are trying to conceive. If medicines do not work, in vitro fertilisation (IVF) may be offered, and a simple surgical procedure called laparoscopic ovarian drilling may be recommended. The NHS says that with treatment, most women with PCOS are able to get pregnant.

    An NHS trust page says PCOS is one of the recognised causes of gestational diabetes, which can lead to pre-eclampsia.

    PCOS, long-term health and mood

    UK sources list type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnoea and a raised risk of cancer of the womb lining among the possible long-term health risks. Infrequent or absent periods are a risk factor for thickening of the womb lining.

    The 2025 all-party parliamentary group report says psychological support is rarely offered despite well-established mental health risks, and recommends better recognition of and support for mental wellbeing.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I get pregnant with PCOS?

    Most women with PCOS are able to get pregnant with treatment. The NHS says that with treatment most women with PCOS are able to get pregnant. PCOS can disrupt ovulation, which can make it harder to conceive. Medicines such as clomifene can be used to trigger ovulation, and IVF may be offered if medicines do not work.

    Does having polycystic ovaries on a scan mean I have PCOS?

    Not necessarily. Polycystic-looking ovaries are common on a scan, and an NHS trust leaflet says they do not need treatment by themselves. One NHS leaflet says up to 1 in 4 young women have polycystic-looking ovaries on a scan, and polycystic ovaries by themselves do not need treatment. Diagnosis commonly needs at least two of three features, after other causes have been ruled out.

    Is there a cure for PCOS?

    The NHS says there is no cure, but the symptoms can be treated. Losing weight can help people who are overweight, the combined contraceptive pill can be used to regulate periods, and fertility treatment is available.

    Does PCOS raise the risk of type 2 diabetes?

    UK sources list type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnoea and a raised risk of cancer of the womb lining among the possible long-term health risks.

    Can PCOS affect mental health?

    The 2025 all-party parliamentary group report says psychological support is rarely offered despite well-established mental health risks, and recommends better recognition of and support for mental wellbeing.

    Conditions

    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Acne Acne is a common skin condition in which spots, from a few small ones to deep painful lumps, appear mostly on the face, chest and back.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.

    Symptoms

    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.

    Biomarkers

    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Medications

    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.

    Guides and news

    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • NICE draft guideline renames PCOS as PMOS NICE has published a draft guideline on polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), formerly called PCOS, for public consultation.
