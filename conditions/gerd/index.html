    Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease

    Also known as: GERD, GORD, acid reflux, heartburn

    Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.

    When to seek care

    Call 999 or go to A&E for vomiting blood, black or tarry stools, collapse, or chest pain that could be cardiac. See a GP promptly for difficulty or pain swallowing, unexplained weight loss, persistent vomiting, or heartburn that persists despite treatment.

    Overview

    The lower oesophageal sphincter normally keeps acid in the stomach. When it relaxes too often or is weakened, especially with a hiatus hernia or obesity, acid burns the unprotected oesophagus. Heartburn after meals and lying down is the classic symptom; chronic reflux can scar the oesophagus or change its lining (Barrett's oesophagus), a precursor of cancer. The anti-reflux barrier is made up of the lower oesophageal sphincter and the crura of the diaphragm that wrap around it; transient relaxations of the sphincter are the commonest mechanism of reflux, with a hiatus hernia, raised abdominal pressure and poor clearance of refluxed acid from the oesophagus adding to it. Heartburn and regurgitation are the typical symptoms, but reflux can also present with chronic cough, asthma, laryngitis or dental erosion in the absence of heartburn, which makes it harder to recognise. Large or late meals, irregular meal timing, smoking, obesity and some medicines all contribute; the evidence linking specific foods is limited and varies between individuals.

    Causes

    • Weak or over-relaxing lower oesophageal sphincter
    • Hiatus hernia
    • Raised abdominal pressure: obesity, pregnancy
    • Delayed stomach emptying
    • Transient relaxations of the lower oesophageal sphincter
    • Poor clearance of refluxed acid from the oesophagus

    Risk factors

    • Obesity
    • Smoking
    • Alcohol, coffee, fatty and spicy food, large late meals
    • Pregnancy
    • Some drugs: NSAIDs, calcium-channel blockers, nitrates
    • Irregular meal timing

    Symptoms

    Chest painNauseaCoughVomiting

    What a clinician may find

    • Usually none
    • Dental erosion, hoarseness

    Complications

    • Oesophagitis and ulcers
    • Strictures causing difficulty swallowing
    • Barrett's oesophagus and oesophageal adenocarcinoma
    • Chronic cough, asthma worsening, laryngitis

    Diagnosis

    Typical symptoms responding to acid suppression are usually enough. Endoscopy is done for alarm features (difficulty swallowing, weight loss, bleeding, anaemia, onset over 55) or persistent symptoms; pH monitoring quantifies reflux when the diagnosis is unclear. Other causes of similar symptoms, such as eosinophilic oesophagitis, are considered. Upper endoscopy is recommended when alarm features are present or when symptoms do not respond to a proton pump inhibitor; it shows oesophagitis, strictures and Barrett's change and allows biopsy. Oesophageal pH monitoring confirms abnormal acid exposure when the diagnosis remains unclear.

    Treatment

    Weight loss, smaller earlier meals, raising the bed head, avoiding triggers, stopping smoking. Antacids and alginates for occasional symptoms; proton pump inhibitors for regular symptoms and healing. Anti-reflux surgery (fundoplication) for severe cases. Lifestyle changes are a core part of management and can sometimes control symptoms on their own: dietary adjustments, raising the head of the bed, not lying down soon after eating and stopping smoking. Proton pump inhibitors are the standard first-line medicine; concerns about long-term side effects and about symptoms returning when they are stopped have driven research into newer drugs. Symptoms that persist despite a proton pump inhibitor (refractory reflux) prompt further evaluation to exclude other causes before escalating; endoscopic therapies and anti-reflux surgery such as fundoplication help people whose symptoms are clearly reflux-triggered.

    Prevention

    Healthy weight, not smoking, moderate alcohol and coffee, no large meals within three hours of bed.

    What does GORD feel like?

    The NHS describes heartburn as a burning feeling in the middle of the chest, often with a sour taste, caused by stomach acid travelling up towards the throat. It is often worse after eating, when lying down and when bending over. If it keeps happening, it may be called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GORD.

    NHS inform lists other possible symptoms, including bloating, belching, vomiting, a hoarse voice and a sore throat. NIDDK, a United States institute, describes GERD (the American spelling) as a more severe and longer-lasting condition in which reflux of stomach contents into the oesophagus, the food pipe, causes repeated symptoms or complications.

    The NHS says common triggers include coffee, tomatoes, alcohol, chocolate, and fatty or spicy foods. Smoking, being overweight, pregnancy, stress and some painkillers such as ibuprofen are also listed.

    Is reflux different in babies and children?

    NICE guidance on reflux in children and young people describes regurgitation, when a baby brings milk back up, as common and normal in infancy. The NHS says a baby with reflux does not usually need to see a doctor if they are happy, healthy and gaining weight, and that babies usually grow out of it by the age of 1. It suggests a GP visit if a baby is not improving after trying ways to ease reflux, or if reflux is still happening after the age of 1.

    For formula-fed babies who often regurgitate and are very distressed, NICE sets out steps for clinicians. These include reviewing the feeding history, trying smaller, more frequent feeds, and then trying a thickened formula.

    A children's hospital leaflet lists coughing, gagging, trouble swallowing and wheezing among the symptoms to raise with a GP or health visitor.

    How is GORD diagnosed, and are tests needed?

    NHS inform says a GP can often diagnose GORD from the symptoms and may start treatment without any tests. NIDDK likewise says doctors usually diagnose it from symptoms and medical history. Testing is generally kept for people whose symptoms do not improve with lifestyle changes and medicines, or where a complication or another condition is suspected.

    The NHS says tests such as a gastroscopy may be used for severe or persistent cases. A gastroscopy is an examination in which a thin tube with a camera is passed down the throat to look at the lining of the food pipe and stomach.

    NHS inform advises seeing a GP if symptoms happen several times a week, if over-the-counter medicines are not helping, or if there are signs of something more serious such as persistent vomiting, vomiting blood or unexplained weight loss. The NHS notes that severe chest pain can occur and may be mistaken for a heart attack, and says urgent medical help is needed rather than a routine appointment. In an emergency, call 999.

    What treatments are used for GORD?

    The NHS says pharmacies sell antacids, which neutralise stomach acid, and alginates, which form a coating that protects the oesophagus from acid. These ease symptoms but will not cure the problem, and should not be taken regularly for long periods.

    If lifestyle changes and pharmacy medicines do not help, a GP may prescribe a proton pump inhibitor. This is a medicine, such as omeprazole or lansoprazole, that reduces how much acid the stomach makes. The NHS says some people need to take it on a long-term basis. If it does not control symptoms, a medicine known as an H2 receptor antagonist may be recommended.

    The NHS says surgery is rarely needed. NHS inform describes it as an option if medication does not control symptoms.

    Living with reflux: what self-care helps?

    The NHS lists several self-care measures. These include eating smaller, more frequent meals, avoiding trigger foods, and not eating within three to four hours of bed. It also suggests avoiding tight waistbands, not smoking, cutting back on alcohol and losing weight if needed. Raising the head of the bed may also help, but the NHS says extra pillows are not recommended.

    NIDDK says that for people who are overweight or have obesity, a doctor may suggest losing weight. It lists foods and drinks commonly linked to symptoms, including citrus fruits, tomatoes, chocolate, mint, caffeine, alcohol, high-fat foods and spicy foods. It adds that people react differently, so a doctor may suggest cutting out particular foods to see what helps.

    What problems can reflux cause?

    NHS inform says long-term damage to the oesophagus from leaking stomach acid can lead to ulcers, scarring and changes to the cells of the oesophagus, which can increase the risk of oesophageal cancer. A hospital leaflet says narrowings of the oesophagus, called strictures, are most commonly caused by scarring from acid reflux and usually cause difficulty swallowing that develops gradually.

    Barrett's oesophagus is a change in the lining at the lower end of the oesophagus, usually found through endoscopy. Hospital leaflets say long-term acid reflux is thought to be the main driver, and the changed cells are not cancerous but carry a small increased risk of becoming so. NICE guidance says a history of reflux symptoms and obesity are the main risk factors.

    NHS inform says a hiatus hernia, where part of the stomach pushes up through the diaphragm, makes reflux more likely.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is acid reflux the same as heartburn?

    The NHS describes heartburn as the burning feeling in the chest from acid travelling up towards the throat. If it keeps happening, it may be called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GORD. So heartburn is the symptom, and GORD is the longer-lasting condition behind it.

    Do antacids cure GORD?

    The NHS says antacids and alginates ease symptoms but will not cure the problem, and should not be taken regularly for long periods. A pharmacist can advise on them. If lifestyle changes and pharmacy medicines are not helping, the NHS advises seeing a GP, who may prescribe a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that reduces how much acid the stomach makes.

    Is a camera test always needed for reflux?

    No. NHS inform says a GP can often diagnose GORD from symptoms and may start treatment without tests. The NHS says tests such as a gastroscopy, where a thin tube with a camera is passed down the throat, may be used for severe or persistent cases.

    Do babies grow out of reflux?

    The NHS says babies usually grow out of reflux by the age of 1. It says a baby with reflux does not usually need to see a doctor if they are happy, healthy and gaining weight. It suggests a GP visit if the baby is not improving after trying ways to ease reflux, or if reflux is still happening after the age of 1.

    When should someone see a doctor about heartburn?

    The NHS advises seeing a GP if lifestyle changes and pharmacy medicines are not helping, if heartburn happens most days, or if there are other symptoms such as food getting stuck in the throat, frequent sickness or weight loss for no reason. Severe chest pain needs urgent medical help rather than a routine appointment; in an emergency, call 999.

    Conditions

    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Hernia A hernia forms when muscle weakness lets tissue push through.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.

    Drug classes

    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).
