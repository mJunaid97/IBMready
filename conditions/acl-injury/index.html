    ACL injury

    Also known as: ACL tear, torn cruciate, knee ligament injury, Anterior cruciate ligament injury

    A sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, the band inside the knee that stops the tibia sliding forward on the femur and controls rotation.

    When to seek care

    Same-day assessment for a knee that swelled rapidly after a twisting injury, cannot straighten, locks or feels unstable. People are advised to avoid sport until the knee has been clinically assessed.

    Overview

    ACL tears happen in a moment: a pivot, a sudden stop or a landing with the knee twisted inward, often with a pop and rapid swelling. The knee then gives way on turning. Young athletes, especially women, are most affected. Whether to reconstruct the ligament depends on the person's activity and instability. MedlinePlus describes the early picture: a popping sound or sensation at the moment of injury, obvious knee swelling within hours, pain that is worst when trying to put weight on the leg, an inability to carry on with the sport, and a feeling that the knee is unstable. Milder sprains may show up mainly as the knee giving way during use. Because the ligament is torn by a twisting load, the same force frequently damages the menisci, the joint cartilage or the collateral ligaments at the same time, which is why imaging looks beyond the ACL itself.

    Causes

    • Non-contact pivoting, cutting or landing injuries (70%)
    • Direct blows to the knee

    Risk factors

    • Football, skiing, basketball, netball
    • Female sex
    • Poor landing mechanics and hip weakness
    • Previous ACL injury
    • Fatigue

    Symptoms

    Knee painSwelling (oedema)Joint pain

    What a clinician may find

    • A pop at injury, swelling within hours (haemarthrosis)
    • Positive Lachman and anterior drawer tests
    • Giving way on pivoting
    • Positive pivot-shift test (rotational instability)

    Complications

    • Meniscal and cartilage damage
    • Recurrent instability
    • Early osteoarthritis

    Diagnosis

    History and examination are frequently enough to make the diagnosis: the twisting mechanism, the pop, the rapid haemarthrosis and a positive Lachman, anterior drawer or pivot-shift test. Plain X-rays cannot show ligaments but are taken to rule out a fracture. MRI is the reference investigation: it confirms the rupture and shows the meniscal, cartilage, collateral ligament and bone injuries that commonly accompany it and that change the treatment plan.

    MRI scanX-ray

    Treatment

    Immediate: rest, ice, compression, elevation and early physiotherapy. Structured rehabilitation restores function in many people; surgical reconstruction with a tendon graft (hamstring, patellar or quadriceps) is chosen for those with instability or pivoting sports, followed by 9 to 12 months of rehabilitation. NHS Scotland musculoskeletal guidance describes three broad pathways: rehabilitation as the first-line treatment, with reconstruction reserved for people who develop functional instability; reconstruction followed by post-operative rehabilitation as the first choice; or a period of pre-operative rehabilitation and then reconstruction. The choice depends on the person's age and sporting demands, the severity of the damage and the expected long-term outcome. The evidence comparing the routes is mixed: a review found only low-quality data, showing no clear difference in patient-reported knee function between surgical reconstruction and rehabilitation alone. Orthopaedic referral is advised where there is persistent subjective instability, recurrent giving way, ongoing mechanical symptoms such as locking, or failure to regain near-symmetrical strength and hop-test performance.

    Prevention

    Neuromuscular training programmes (landing technique, hip and hamstring strength, balance) cut injury rates by half in athletes.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.

    Symptoms

    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).

    Drug classes

    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.

    Guides and news

    • Knee pain: causes and when to get help Knee pain can often be treated at home, and NHS 111 is the route for a badly swollen knee or a hot, red knee with a high temperature.
