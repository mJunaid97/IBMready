    Type 2 diabetes

    Also known as: adult-onset diabetes, T2D, diabetes mellitus

    A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate, leaving blood glucose persistently high.

    When to seek care

    Call 999 or go to A&E for chest pain, or for drowsiness or confusion with very high glucose readings. Get same-day advice from a GP or NHS 111 for a foot wound that is red, hot or discharging, or sudden vision change.

    Open in the 3D explorerPancreas anatomy

    Overview

    Type 2 diabetes accounts for over 90% of diabetes and is closely tied to excess weight and inactivity, though genes matter greatly. It develops silently over years; many people are diagnosed on a routine test or when a complication appears. Sustained weight loss early on can put it into remission.

    Causes

    • Insulin resistance from visceral fat and inactivity, plus progressive beta-cell failure, on a strong genetic background

    Risk factors

    • Overweight, especially around the waist
    • Inactivity
    • Family history
    • Age over 40 (younger in South Asian, Black and Hispanic populations)
    • Gestational diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome
    • High blood pressure and cholesterol

    Symptoms

    FatigueNumbnessTingling (pins and needles)Weakness

    What a clinician may find

    • Often none
    • Central obesity, acanthosis nigricans
    • Reduced foot sensation and pulses in established disease
    • Retinal changes

    Complications

    • Heart attack and stroke
    • Kidney failure
    • Retinopathy and blindness
    • Neuropathy, foot ulcers and amputation
    • Fatty liver disease
    • Hyperosmolar crisis

    Diagnosis

    HbA1c of 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) or more, or fasting glucose of 7 mmol/L or more, repeated if without symptoms. Annual checks of kidney function, urine albumin, eyes, feet, blood pressure and lipids.

    Treatment

    Diet, weight loss and activity are the foundation; metformin first, then SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas or insulin as needed; statins and blood pressure treatment reduce cardiovascular risk; structured education and foot care.

    Prevention

    Diet and exercise programmes halve the risk in people with prediabetes; keeping a healthy weight and staying active are the key measures.

    What are the symptoms of type 2 diabetes and how does it develop?

    Many people with type 2 diabetes have no symptoms at first, and signs can build slowly over a few years. The NHS lists tiredness, needing to pee more, constant thirst and unintended weight loss as the most common symptoms. Other possible signs are blurred vision, slow-healing cuts, and genital itching or repeated thrush. NHS inform also lists tingling or numbness, which can point to nerve damage.

    NHS 111 Wales describes type 2 diabetes as often associated with being overweight or inactive, or with a family history of the condition. The NHS explains that in type 2 diabetes the body either does not make enough insulin or the insulin it makes does not work correctly. NHS 111 Wales advises speaking to a GP about any of these symptoms or about worries over personal risk.

    How is type 2 diabetes diagnosed, and what do check-ups involve?

    A GP can usually diagnose type 2 diabetes with a blood test and a urine test. NHS Scotland guidance describes HbA1c as the preferred diagnostic test, because it does not require fasting and reflects average blood glucose over the previous few months.

    After diagnosis, NICE describes a yearly check-up called the annual review. NICE says it includes checking blood pressure, blood glucose and cardiovascular risk, with regular foot checks, and covers other long-term complications such as kidney and nerve problems. Diabetes UK and NHS material on annual checks also list a urine test for kidney protein and a retinal eye screen. Eye screening is a photograph of the back of the eye, and it is not the same as a routine optician sight test.

    What treatments are used for type 2 diabetes?

    The NHS says treatment usually combines lifestyle changes with medicines. Its treatment page centres on diet, physical activity, weight and smoking, and suggests eating more wholegrains, pulses, fruit and vegetables.

    For people without other serious conditions, the NHS describes the usual starting point as metformin together with an SGLT2 inhibitor, which lowers blood sugar by letting extra sugar leave the body in urine. If metformin causes side effects, an SGLT2 inhibitor alone may be recommended. A GLP-1 agonist, given by injection, may be offered to people with a history of heart disease or with obesity when other treatments have not lowered blood sugar enough. Local NHS guidance also describes gliclazide, sitagliptin and pioglitazone as further options.

    Can type 2 diabetes go into remission, and when is insulin needed?

    Diabetes UK says type 2 diabetes can sometimes go into remission, mainly through weight loss. It avoids the word cure because there is no evidence that remission could remain permanent, and many people who regain weight come out of remission.

    The NHS England Path to Remission Programme offers a low-calorie total diet replacement plan, using products such as soups and shakes, to eligible people in England who have type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity. NHS England notes that the strict nature of the diet means it is not suitable for everyone. The NHS also advises against very strict low-calorie diets without medical advice, especially for people taking insulin or other diabetes medicines.

    The NHS says insulin is prescribed for type 2 diabetes if other diabetes medicines no longer keep blood glucose within a healthy range.

    What complications can type 2 diabetes cause, and when should someone get help?

    Over time, high blood sugar can damage the blood vessels, nerves, eyes, kidneys, feet and gums. The NHS lists coronary heart disease and stroke, foot problems where untreated ulcers or infections can lead to amputation, sight problems such as diabetic retinopathy, kidney problems and gum disease. It also says that controlling blood sugar and having regular check-ups is the best way to lower the risk of complications.

    Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and HHS are rare in type 2 diabetes but can be life threatening. The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if someone with type 2 diabetes is peeing a lot, feels very thirsty, feels sick, is confused or very sleepy, or is short of breath. NHS inform advises contacting a podiatrist or GP practice urgently for a hot, red, swollen foot or new skin damage, and phoning NHS 111 if the practice is closed.

    Living with type 2 diabetes: eating, activity and daily checks

    The NHS suggests a healthy diet that includes fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods such as wholemeal bread and oats, and pulses such as chickpeas and lentils. It also notes that having diabetes increases the harmful effects of smoking.

    NICE recommends structured education for adults with type 2 diabetes at the time of diagnosis, with yearly reinforcement and review. Diabetes UK says people should be offered a free diabetes education course. NICE says the care team can point people to support groups, and people can ask for help or advice at any time.

    NHS Lanarkshire advises checking the feet every day for cuts, skin breaks or hard skin. NHS 111 Wales says people who take insulin for type 2 diabetes must tell the DVLA, because of the risk of low blood sugar.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can type 2 diabetes go into remission?

    Diabetes UK says type 2 diabetes can sometimes go into remission, mainly through weight loss, though it is not guaranteed to last. The NHS England Path to Remission Programme offers a low-calorie total diet replacement plan, using soups and shakes, to eligible people in England with type 2 diabetes and overweight or obesity.

    How is type 2 diabetes different from type 1?

    The NHS says that in type 2 diabetes the body either does not make enough insulin or the insulin it makes does not work correctly. MedlinePlus describes type 1 as an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the insulin-producing cells, so little or no insulin is made.

    Does everyone with type 2 diabetes need insulin?

    The NHS says treatment usually combines lifestyle changes with medicines, and that insulin is prescribed if other diabetes medicines no longer keep blood glucose within a healthy range. NHS 111 Wales notes that insulin is not often used in the early years.

    Can someone have type 2 diabetes without symptoms?

    Yes. The NHS says symptoms may come on gradually and not everyone gets them, and NHS inform notes that signs often appear slowly over a few years, so some people do not realise they have it. NHS 111 Wales advises speaking to a GP about any symptoms or worries over risk.

    When is type 2 diabetes an emergency?

    The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if someone with type 2 diabetes is peeing a lot, feels very thirsty, feels sick, is confused or very sleepy, or is short of breath, as these can signal DKA or HHS. The NHS says both are rare in type 2 diabetes but can be life threatening.

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Erectile dysfunction Erectile dysfunction, also called impotence, is being unable to get or keep an erection.
    • Fatty liver disease (MASLD) A common condition in which fat builds up in the liver, often linked to being overweight.
    • Gestational diabetes Gestational diabetes is a form of diabetes that starts during pregnancy and typically clears up after the baby is born.
    • High cholesterol (hypercholesterolaemia) High cholesterol, also called hypercholesterolaemia, means there is too much cholesterol in the blood.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Vitamin B12 and folate Two blood tests, usually ordered together, for the vitamins the bone marrow needs to make normal red cells and the nervous system needs to keep…

    Biomarkers

    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.
    • Vitamin B12 The vitamin, absorbed with intrinsic factor in the last part of the small intestine.

    Procedures

    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin is a prescription tablet from the SGLT2 inhibitor group, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Empagliflozin Empagliflozin is a prescription medicine used to treat type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Gliclazide Gliclazide is a sulfonylurea tablet prescribed for type 2 diabetes that prompts the pancreas to make more insulin.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Biguanides Metformin is the only biguanide in routine use and the first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it lowers glucose without causing hypoglycaemia or…
    • GLP-1 receptor agonists GLP-1 receptor agonists are medicines that imitate GLP-1, a hormone the body releases after eating.
    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
    • PDE5 inhibitors PDE5 inhibitors are medicines that include sildenafil and tadalafil, used mainly for erection problems.
    • SGLT2 inhibitors SGLT2 inhibitors are tablets used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
    • Sulfonylureas Sulfonylureas are tablets for type 2 diabetes that prompt the pancreas to make more insulin.
