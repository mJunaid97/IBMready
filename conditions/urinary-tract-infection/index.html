    Urinary tract infection

    Also known as: UTI, cystitis, bladder infection

    Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for fever, shivering, loin pain, vomiting or confusion with urinary symptoms, symptoms in pregnancy or in a man, or blood in the urine that persists.

    Overview

    UTIs are among the commonest bacterial infections, far more frequent in women because the short urethra lets bacteria reach the bladder easily. Cystitis burns and nags but is rarely dangerous; kidney infection causes fever, loin pain and vomiting and can lead to sepsis, especially in the elderly, in pregnancy and with obstruction. Infection begins when bacteria enter the urethra and move upwards to the bladder (cystitis) and sometimes on to the kidneys (pyelonephritis); E. coli is the main agent, and other bacteria, viruses or fungi are rare causes. Presentation varies with age: infants often have fever, irritability or poor feeding, while older adults may have no urinary symptoms and instead show confusion resembling dementia, marked fatigue or incontinence. UTI is the most common hospital-acquired infection, and most of those cases are associated with urinary catheters. Resistance to antibiotics is an increasing problem in treating UTI.

    Causes

    • Gut bacteria (E. coli in 80%) ascending the urethra
    • Catheters and instrumentation
    • Obstruction: stones, enlarged prostate

    Risk factors

    • Female anatomy (a short urethra close to the vagina and anus), sexual activity, spermicides
    • Pregnancy
    • Menopause
    • Diabetes
    • Urinary catheter
    • Incomplete bladder emptying, kidney stones, prostate enlargement

    Symptoms

    Abdominal painFeverBack painNauseaVomiting

    What a clinician may find

    • Suprapubic tenderness
    • Loin tenderness and fever in pyelonephritis
    • Confusion in older adults
    • Cloudy or bloody urine
    • In infants: fever, irritability and reduced feeding
    • In older adults: dementia-like confusion, fatigue or new incontinence without urinary complaints

    Complications

    • Kidney infection and abscess
    • Sepsis
    • Kidney scarring in children
    • Premature labour in pregnancy
    • Recurrent infection, defined as repeated episodes within a few months
    • Hospital-acquired, catheter-associated infection

    Diagnosis

    Symptoms and a urine dipstick (nitrites, leucocytes, blood) in uncomplicated cystitis; urine culture in men, children, pregnancy, recurrent or complicated infection; blood tests and imaging if kidney infection or obstruction is suspected. Diagnosis combines clinical assessment with a urinalysis and, where indicated, a urine culture, which usually takes several days to complete; audits of general practice have found that prescribing antibiotics without any urine testing is common and is a recognised area for improvement.

    Treatment

    A short course of antibiotics (nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim for cystitis; longer courses of a broader agent for kidney infection), fluids and pain relief. Intravenous antibiotics for severe infection; relief of any obstruction. Cystitis often gets better by itself, and UK services may issue a delayed ('back-up') antibiotic prescription to be started only if symptoms do not settle. Paracetamol eases the discomfort. Complicated infection may need a longer course or intravenous antibiotics, and persistent symptoms warrant further testing rather than repeated empirical courses.

    Prevention

    Fluids, emptying the bladder after sex, avoiding spermicides, vaginal oestrogen after the menopause, prophylactic antibiotics or methenamine for frequent recurrences. NHS advice also includes avoiding perfumed bubble bath, soap or talcum powder around the genitals, preferring showers to baths, passing urine as soon as the urge is felt and wiping from front to back; for some women with recurrent cystitis a single antibiotic dose taken within an hour of sex is prescribed.

    What does a UTI feel like?

    NHS guidance lists pain or a burning feeling when passing urine, needing to go more often than usual, cloudy urine, blood in the urine, and pain in the lower tummy or in the back just under the ribs. Feeling tired or weak and smelly urine can also occur. NIDDK, a US health institute, describes similar bladder infection symptoms, including discomfort in the lower abdomen and cloudy, bloody or strong-smelling urine.

    When a bladder infection spreads to one or both kidneys, NIDDK lists fever and chills, nausea or vomiting, and pain in the back, side or groin as warning signs. It says early treatment prevents most complications. MedlinePlus says bladder infection symptoms usually improve within a day or two of starting treatment, while kidney infections can take a week or more to resolve.

    How does a UTI show up in older people and children?

    MedlinePlus says older adults may show only confusion. The NHS says that in older, frail people with memory problems such as dementia, and in people with a urinary catheter, symptoms can include changes in behaviour such as acting agitated or confused, wetting that is worse than usual, or new shivering or shaking.

    In babies and young children, fever is the most common sign and may be the only one. Poor feeding and vomiting can also occur. Children who were toilet trained may start having accidents by day or night. MedlinePlus says antibiotics for a child's UTI should start quickly to protect the kidneys, and a child under six months old, or with other complications, should see a specialist right away.

    How is a UTI diagnosed?

    MedlinePlus describes urinary tract infections as affecting the bladder, kidneys, ureters or urethra, and calls a kidney infection pyelonephritis. A urine culture identifies the bacteria so a clinician can choose an antibiotic. A positive culture, meaning bacteria or yeast grew, likely indicates a UTI, although earlier antibiotic use can produce a falsely negative result.

    For older adults, the NICE quality standard recommends diagnosing UTI by full clinical assessment, without a urine dipstick test, because dipstick accuracy varies. A UK hospital study of older patients found that most positive dipstick results did not match the laboratory result. The NHS England and UKHSA toolkit likewise says dipsticks should be avoided in older adults to support an accurate diagnosis.

    What treatments are used?

    NIDDK says a health professional will likely prescribe antibiotics for a bacterial bladder infection, and the length of treatment depends on how severe the infection is and whether symptoms go away. It advises finishing the whole course, because antibiotic resistance could make an infection harder to treat, and says drinking more liquids, with water best, can speed recovery and ease symptoms.

    The NHS says antibiotics are not always needed. A GP may offer self-care advice and a painkiller, a short course of antibiotics, or a prescription to use after waiting 48 hours in case symptoms go away alone.

    For kidney infection, the NHS says most people are treated at home with antibiotics. Paracetamol helps with pain and high temperature, and ibuprofen-type painkillers are not normally recommended because they may increase the risk of further kidney problems. Hospital treatment with antibiotics through a drip may be needed for people who are pregnant, have a long-term health condition, are severely dehydrated or cannot keep down fluids or medicines.

    Why do some people get UTIs again and again?

    Reviews define recurrent UTI as two infections within six months or three within twelve months. NHS 111 Wales advises seeing a GP if a UTI returns after treatment or follows that pattern. Options it lists include a different antibiotic, a low-dose antibiotic taken for up to six months, or, after the menopause, an oestrogen vaginal product. NHS patient information also describes taking antibiotics after sex where sex seems to trigger infections.

    NIDDK says vaginal oestrogen increases good bacteria and helps prevent infection, and that diaphragms, unlubricated condoms or spermicide may raise the chance of a bladder infection. It says cranberry products do not treat an existing infection and there is not enough evidence to be sure they prevent one.

    Can a UTI become serious, and who should be contacted?

    MedlinePlus lists kidney infection and sepsis, a dangerous overreaction to infection, as risks of a UTI, with sepsis risk greatest in young children, very old adults and people with weakened immunity. NHS England says older adults made up almost three fifths of UTI hospital admissions over five years.

    NHS 111 Wales advises calling 999 or going to A&E if a person with a suspected UTI, or their child, is confused, drowsy or has difficulty speaking, and not driving themselves. NHS guidance advises urgent help from a GP or NHS 111 for a very high or very low temperature, vomiting, blood in the urine, passing no urine all day, or symptoms that get worse quickly or do not improve within 48 hours. NI Direct says to see a GP if symptoms are very uncomfortable or last more than five days.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can a UTI get better without antibiotics?

    NHS 111 Wales says antibiotics may treat UTIs but are not always needed. A GP may offer self-care advice and a painkiller, a short course of antibiotics, or a prescription to use after waiting 48 hours in case symptoms go away alone. NHS guidance says symptoms that do not improve within 48 hours need review by a GP or NHS 111.

    How is a kidney infection different from a bladder infection?

    NIDDK says a bladder infection can spread to one or both kidneys, with warning signs of fever and chills, nausea or vomiting, and pain in the back, side or groin. MedlinePlus says kidney infections can take a week or more to resolve. The NHS describes blood poisoning as a medical emergency.

    What are the signs of a UTI in babies and children?

    Fever is the most common sign in children under one and may be the only one, with poor feeding and vomiting also possible. Children who were toilet trained may start having accidents by day or night. MedlinePlus says a child under six months old, or with other complications, should see a specialist right away.

    When should someone see a GP about a UTI?

    NI Direct says to see a GP if symptoms are very uncomfortable or last more than five days, or if there is a high temperature, symptoms suddenly worsen, or the person is pregnant or has diabetes. NHS 111 Wales advises calling 999 or going to A&E for confusion, drowsiness or difficulty speaking.

    Conditions

    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.
    • Pelvic organ prolapse Descent of one or more of the pelvic organs (the bladder, uterus, rectum, small bowel or, after hysterectomy.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Bladder cancer Bladder cancer is cancer that forms in the tissues of the bladder, the organ that stores urine.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Enlarged prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia) An enlarged prostate is a common condition in which the prostate gland grows larger and can squeeze the urethra, the outlet tube for urine.
    • Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) A kidney infection, also called pyelonephritis, is a urinary tract infection that affects one or both kidneys.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Vaginoplasty Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.
    • Nitrofurantoin Nitrofurantoin is an antibiotic that the NHS describes as a medicine for lower urinary tract infections such as cystitis.
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Drug classes

    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • Oestrogens Steroid hormones that act on the oestrogen receptor.
    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.
    • Urinary antibacterials Antibiotics used for urinary tract infections.

    Guides and news

    • Blood in urine: causes and what happens next Blood in the urine is not usually serious, but the NHS advises seeing a GP every time, even if the amount is small.
    • Cystoscopy: what happens and what to expect A cystoscopy uses a thin camera passed through the urethra to look inside the bladder.
    • Fever in adults: causes and when to get help A high temperature in adults is usually a sign of infection and often settles at home.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
    • UTI and cystitis: what they are A urinary tract infection (UTI) can affect the bladder, urethra or kidneys; cystitis is a UTI of the bladder, and antibiotics are not always needed.
