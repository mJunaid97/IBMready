    Appendicitis

    Also known as: inflamed appendix

    Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.

    When to seek care

    Same-day emergency assessment for pain that moves to the lower right abdomen, worsens over hours, and comes with nausea, loss of appetite or fever. Severe pain with a high fever may mean the appendix has ruptured and needs immediate hospital care; untreated appendicitis can progress to peritonitis or an abscess, so people are advised not to wait to see whether the pain passes.

    Overview

    The appendix is a blind pouch hanging off the caecum in the lower right abdomen. When its lumen is obstructed, by a hard pellet of stool, swollen lymphoid tissue or rarely a tumour, bacteria multiply, pressure rises, the wall becomes inflamed and, within a day or two, can perforate. It is the commonest cause of emergency abdominal surgery. The classic story is pain that begins around the navel and, over hours, shifts to the lower right abdomen, becoming sharper and worse with movement, coughing or sneezing; loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting, a low fever and abdominal swelling commonly follow. The picture is less typical in young children, older adults and pregnant women, in whom the diagnosis is often delayed. In many cases no specific cause is identified.

    Causes

    • Obstruction of the appendix lumen by a faecolith or enlarged lymphoid tissue
    • Rarely a tumour or parasite
    • Multifactorial in many cases; often no specific cause is found

    Risk factors

    • Age 10 to 30
    • Family history
    • Low-fibre diet (weak evidence)

    Symptoms

    Abdominal painNauseaVomitingFever

    What a clinician may find

    • Tenderness and guarding at McBurney's point in the right lower quadrant
    • Rebound tenderness
    • Pain on the right when the left side is pressed (Rovsing's sign)
    • Mild fever and fast pulse
    • Abdominal swelling and reluctance to move

    Complications

    • Perforation and peritonitis
    • Abscess
    • Sepsis
    • Bowel obstruction from adhesions
    • Delayed diagnosis in children, older adults and pregnancy, raising the chance of rupture

    Diagnosis

    Largely clinical: the migrating pain, right lower quadrant tenderness and raised white cells and CRP. Clinicians take a history and examine the abdomen, then use urine tests to exclude a urinary infection or kidney stone, blood tests for inflammation, and a pregnancy test in women to exclude an ectopic pregnancy. Imaging confirms the diagnosis or finds another cause of the pain: ultrasound first in children and in pregnancy, CT in adults when the picture is unclear, and MRI where radiation is to be avoided.

    Treatment

    Prompt removal of the appendix (appendectomy), now usually laparoscopic, with antibiotics before and, if perforated, after surgery. Selected uncomplicated cases can be treated with antibiotics alone, with a risk of recurrence. An abscess may be drained first and the appendix removed later. Prompt surgery lowers the risk of rupture. Appendicectomy is performed laparoscopically through small incisions or, when needed, through an open incision (laparotomy), with antibiotics given before surgery and continued afterwards if the appendix has perforated. A ruptured appendix, an abscess or peritonitis needs specialised care: an abscess may be drained under imaging guidance and the appendix removed at a later date.

    Prevention

    None proven. Because appendicitis cannot be prevented, outcomes depend on early recognition and prompt surgery before the appendix ruptures.

    Conditions

    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.

    Guides and news

    • Feeling sick and vomiting in adults NHS inform says vomiting in adults usually lasts only 1 or 2 days, but blood in vomit means calling 999 or going to A&E.
    • Stomach ache: causes by area and warning signs Where a stomach ache sits helps point to causes such as appendicitis, gallstones or IBS, and sudden severe pain needs 999 or A&E.
