    Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

    Also known as: COPD, emphysema, chronic bronchitis

    Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema), almost always from smoking.

    When to seek care

    Call 999 or go to A&E for severe breathlessness, blue lips, chest pain, or new confusion or drowsiness (a sign of carbon dioxide retention). Get same-day advice from a GP or NHS 111 for worsening breathlessness with more or discoloured sputum, or fever.

    Overview

    Years of inhaled smoke inflame the small airways and dissolve the alveolar walls, so air is trapped and the surface for gas exchange shrinks. Breathlessness on exertion creeps up over decades and is often blamed on age; flare-ups triggered by infections drive hospital admissions. COPD is the third leading cause of death worldwide.

    Causes

    • Tobacco smoking (85 to 90% of cases)
    • Biomass and cooking-fire smoke
    • Occupational dust and fumes
    • Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (inherited)

    Risk factors

    • Pack-years of smoking
    • Childhood respiratory infections and poor lung growth
    • Air pollution
    • Asthma

    Symptoms

    Shortness of breathCoughFatigueSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • Barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing
    • Wheeze and reduced breath sounds
    • Cyanosis and ankle oedema in advanced disease

    Complications

    • Exacerbations, often needing hospital care
    • Respiratory failure
    • Pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure (cor pulmonale)
    • Lung cancer
    • Osteoporosis, muscle wasting, depression

    Diagnosis

    Spirometry showing a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7, in someone with symptoms and a smoking or exposure history. Chest X-ray and CT exclude other causes and grade emphysema.

    Treatment

    Stopping smoking is the only measure that slows decline. Long-acting bronchodilator inhalers (with inhaled steroids for frequent exacerbators), pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccinations, and oxygen at home for low oxygen levels. Exacerbations are treated with steroids and often antibiotics.

    Prevention

    Never smoking, or stopping at any stage; clean cooking fuels; workplace dust control; vaccination against flu, pneumococcus and COVID-19.

    What does COPD feel like, and how does it change over time?

    The NHS describes COPD as a lung disease that develops slowly, so symptoms are often not noticed early on. The main symptoms are breathlessness during activity, a persistent chesty cough with phlegm, frequent chest infections and wheezing. Phlegm is the thick mucus coughed up from the airways. Several other conditions, such as asthma, heart failure and anaemia, can cause similar symptoms.

    The NHS says COPD cannot be cured or reversed. For many people, treatment helps keep it under control so that it does not severely limit daily activities. For some people it keeps getting worse despite treatment and can lead to life-threatening problems. The NHS says that the sooner treatment starts, the less chance there is of severe lung damage.

    What are emphysema and chronic bronchitis, and what causes COPD?

    COPD is the name for a group of lung conditions that cause breathing difficulties. Emphysema and chronic bronchitis are two of the conditions grouped under it. Chronic bronchitis is inflammation of the airways, which leads to a long-term cough with mucus. Emphysema is damage to the tiny air sacs in the lungs, which reduces the elasticity of the lungs. Many people have features of both.

    Smoking is the biggest risk factor. The NHS estimates that it is behind around 9 in 10 cases, and the risk rises with how much and for how long a person smokes. The WHO lists long-term exposure to dust or fumes at work, air pollution, and a rare inherited condition called alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency as other factors. In the United States, CDC data show that some adults with COPD have never smoked. The condition usually emerges after the age of 35.

    How is COPD diagnosed, and what do the tests show?

    The NHS says a GP starts by asking about symptoms and about whether the person smokes or used to smoke. The GP examines the chest, listens to the breathing with a stethoscope, may calculate body mass index and asks about any family history of lung problems. The GP may then do, or arrange, a breathing test called spirometry. NICE guidance says COPD is suspected from symptoms and signs, and that spirometry supports the diagnosis.

    A chest X-ray can look for other lung problems that cause similar symptoms, such as chest infections and lung cancer, although these do not always show. Blood tests can detect conditions such as anaemia, and sometimes check for alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, a rare genetic problem that increases the risk of COPD. A CT scan, which is a detailed scan of the lungs, or a phlegm sample to check for infection is sometimes needed to confirm the diagnosis or judge severity.

    What treatments are used for COPD, and what are they for?

    The NHS says stopping smoking is the most effective way to prevent COPD getting worse. Damage already done to the lungs and airways cannot be reversed, but giving up smoking can help prevent further damage. Short-acting bronchodilator inhalers, which widen the airways, are usually the first treatment. Long-acting bronchodilator inhalers may be recommended if there are regular symptoms or flare-ups. A GP may add a steroid inhaler if breathlessness or flare-ups continue, and this is normally given in a combination inhaler. NICE favours keeping the number and types of inhalers to a minimum, because that makes it easier to use them correctly. It also says inhalers should only be prescribed after people have been trained to use them.

    Pulmonary rehabilitation is a supervised programme of exercise, education and breathing techniques, usually in groups. Oxygen is useful only for people whose blood oxygen is low, and it is not a treatment for breathlessness. Lung volume reduction surgery, which removes badly damaged lung tissue, and lung transplant are options for only a small number of people.

    What is a flare-up, and how do people live with COPD?

    A flare-up, also called an exacerbation, is a time when symptoms are worse than usual. MedlinePlus describes one as increased symptoms lasting two days or more. Infections are a common cause. Treatment may include quick-relief inhalers, steroids or antibiotics taken by mouth, or oxygen if it is prescribed. Asthma + Lung UK describes an agreed written action plan as central to managing flare-ups. MedlinePlus suggests pursed-lip breathing to save energy, slow the breathing and help a person relax.

    Common advice also includes taking medicines as prescribed and avoiding smoke, dust and fumes. The NHS encourages everyone with COPD to have the annual flu jab and the one-off pneumococcal vaccination. NHS Scotland says mental health difficulties are more common in people with COPD than in the general population.

    When should someone get medical help with COPD?

    The NHS advises contacting a GP about persistent symptoms such as breathlessness, a chesty cough with phlegm or frequent chest infections, especially for someone over 35 who smokes or used to smoke. That is a GP appointment rather than an emergency.

    For someone already diagnosed, MedlinePlus lists breathing that is getting harder, faster or shallower, fever, dark mucus, sleepiness, confusion, chest pain or bluish lips or fingertips as reasons to contact a provider. In the UK, urgent but non-emergency advice comes from NHS 111 or the GP the same day. South Tees Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust says to call 999 if someone with COPD is very unwell with severe breathlessness, chest pain or coughing up blood.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can COPD be cured?

    The NHS says COPD cannot be cured or reversed, and damage already done to the lungs and airways cannot be undone. For many people, treatment helps keep it under control so that it does not severely limit daily activities. The NHS lists stopping smoking, inhalers and pulmonary rehabilitation among the options.

    Is COPD the same as emphysema?

    COPD is the name for a group of lung conditions, and emphysema is one of them. Emphysema is damage to the tiny air sacs in the lungs. Chronic bronchitis, which is inflammation of the airways leading to a long-term cough with mucus, is another. Many people have features of both.

    Do only smokers get COPD?

    No. Smoking is the biggest risk factor, and the NHS estimates it is behind around 9 in 10 cases. Other factors include long-term exposure to dust or fumes at work, air pollution, and a rare inherited condition called alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. CDC data from the United States show that some adults with COPD have never smoked.

    What is a COPD flare-up?

    A flare-up, or exacerbation, is a time when COPD symptoms are worse than usual. MedlinePlus describes it as increased symptoms lasting two days or more. Infections are a common cause. Asthma + Lung UK describes an agreed written action plan as central to managing flare-ups.

    What does pulmonary rehabilitation involve?

    Pulmonary rehabilitation is a supervised programme of exercise, education and breathing techniques. It is usually run as group sessions over several weeks, and some people use remote or app-based versions. The NHS lists it among the treatments for COPD, alongside stopping smoking and inhalers.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Abdominal aortic aneurysm An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the aorta, the body's main blood vessel, where it runs through the tummy.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.
    • Flu (influenza) Flu is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea Obstructive sleep apnoea is a condition in which the throat narrows or closes during sleep, so breathing stops and starts again.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Biomarkers

    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.

    Drug classes

    • Bronchodilators Inhaled medicines that relax the muscle around the airways within minutes (salbutamol, terbutaline) or for about 12 hours (salmeterol.
    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.
    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.

    Guides and news

    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • Reliever and preventer inhalers explained A reliever inhaler eases symptoms when they happen, while a preventer is taken every day to reduce airway inflammation.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
