    Sepsis

    Also known as: septicaemia, blood poisoning, septic shock

    Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    When to seek care

    Call emergency services for an infection with slurred speech or confusion, extreme shivering or muscle pain, passing no urine in a day, severe breathlessness, mottled or discoloured skin, or a feeling of impending doom; for children also fast breathing, a fit, blue or pale skin, a non-fading rash, or unusual drowsiness.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Overview

    Any infection can trigger sepsis, most often pneumonia, urinary infection, abdominal infection or skin infection. The immune response spills over into the whole body: blood vessels leak and dilate, clotting is disturbed, and organs fail. Sepsis kills millions a year; each hour of delay in antibiotics raises mortality, so recognition is everything.

    Causes

    • Bacterial infection most often (pneumococcus, E. coli, staphylococci, streptococci, meningococcus)
    • Viral, fungal or parasitic infections

    Risk factors

    • Age under 1 or over 75
    • Weak immune system: chemotherapy, steroids, diabetes, no spleen
    • Recent surgery, wounds or catheters
    • Pregnancy and the weeks after birth
    • Chronic lung, liver, kidney disease

    Symptoms

    FeverShortness of breathDizzinessRashWeaknessVomiting

    What a clinician may find

    • Fast breathing (over 22/min), fast pulse, low blood pressure
    • New confusion or drowsiness
    • Mottled, cold or clammy skin; non-blanching rash
    • Passing little urine
    • Very high or abnormally low temperature

    Complications

    • Septic shock and death
    • Acute kidney injury, lung failure, liver failure
    • Disseminated clotting and limb loss
    • Long-term physical and cognitive effects

    Diagnosis

    Clinical recognition (NEWS or qSOFA scores), blood cultures and cultures from the likely source before antibiotics, lactate, blood counts, kidney and liver function, clotting; imaging to find the source.

    Treatment

    The 'sepsis six' within an hour: oxygen, blood cultures, intravenous antibiotics, intravenous fluids, lactate measurement and urine output monitoring; then source control (drain an abscess, remove an infected line), vasopressors and organ support in intensive care.

    Amoxicillin

    Prevention

    Vaccination, hand hygiene, prompt treatment of infections, careful care of wounds and catheters, and knowing the warning signs.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Cellulitis Cellulitis is an infection of the deeper layers of the skin caused by bacteria.
    • COVID-19 COVID-19 is an illness caused by a virus called coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2), with symptoms that overlap with colds and flu.
    • Gonorrhoea A bacterial sexually transmitted infection caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae that can affect the genitals, rectum, throat and eyes.
    • Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) A kidney infection, also called pyelonephritis, is a urinary tract infection that affects one or both kidneys.
    • Meningitis Meningitis is an infection of the protective membranes that surround the brain and spinal cord.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Lymphocytes The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells).
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Antihistamines Medicines that block histamine's H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and itching.
    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.

    Guides and news

    • Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth all cause rashes in children, but their other signs and the advice on getting help differ.
    • Which NHS service to use: 999, 111, GP, A&E The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies and NHS 111 for urgent problems or when unsure, with pharmacies.
    • Fever in adults: causes and when to get help A high temperature in adults is usually a sign of infection and often settles at home.
    • High white blood cell count: what it means A high white blood cell count is a non-specific sign, most often from infection, and doctors read it with the type of cell raised.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
    • Why antibiotics do not work on viruses Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses, so the NHS says they do not help colds, flu or most coughs and sore throats.
