    Kidney stones

    Also known as: renal calculi, nephrolithiasis, renal colic

    Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for colicky pain with fever or shivering, vomiting preventing fluids, pain not controlled by painkillers, or a single functioning kidney. NHS advice is to ask for an urgent GP appointment or contact NHS 111 for severe pain or for fever and chills. Unrelieved pain, reduced urine output, infection or acute kidney failure in the presence of an obstructing stone are medical emergencies.

    Overview

    Stones form when urine is concentrated and rich in stone-forming salts. A stone leaving the kidney scrapes and blocks the ureter, producing renal colic: waves of agonising pain from the loin to the groin with blood in the urine. Most stones under 5 mm pass on their own; larger or infected obstructions need intervention. Stones form when urine carries high concentrations of calcium, oxalate or phosphate, although in many individuals no specific reason is ever found. Many small stones cause no symptoms and are passed unnoticed. Pain that comes in waves and radiates from the back or side towards the groin usually means a stone has moved into the ureter; blood in the urine, nausea and vomiting and pain on passing urine are common, and fever or chills point to infection alongside the stone, which changes the urgency entirely.

    Causes

    • Low urine volume
    • High urinary calcium, oxalate or uric acid
    • Urinary infection with urease-producing bacteria (struvite stones)
    • Rare inherited causes (cystinuria)
    • Metabolic conditions such as hyperparathyroidism that raise urinary calcium

    Risk factors

    • Dehydration and hot climates
    • Family or personal history
    • Obesity, diabetes, gout
    • High salt, animal protein and oxalate intake
    • Some drugs and bowel disease
    • Vigorous exercise or a hot environment without replacing fluid
    • A first stone at a young age
    • Previous kidney or urinary infections

    Abdominal painBack painNauseaVomitingFever

    What a clinician may find

    • Restless, unable to lie still
    • Loin tenderness
    • Blood in the urine on dipstick
    • Fever if infected

    Complications

    • Obstruction with infection (an emergency)
    • Kidney damage
    • Recurrence in half within ten years
    • Acute kidney injury with reduced urine output when drainage is blocked

    Diagnosis

    Non-contrast CT of the urinary tract is the definitive test; ultrasound in pregnancy and children; urine dipstick and culture, kidney function and calcium and urate levels; analysis of the passed stone. Assessment begins with the history, including existing conditions, current medicines, diet and whether relatives have had stones, and with examination. Blood tests check calcium, phosphate, uric acid and electrolytes; urinalysis looks for crystals and red blood cells. Non-contrast CT is the preferred imaging test to confirm a stone and check for obstruction; ultrasound is used first in children and in pregnancy to avoid radiation.

    Treatment

    Strong analgesia (NSAIDs are most effective), fluids, and drugs to relax the ureter to help small stones pass; shock-wave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy with laser fragmentation or percutaneous surgery for larger stones; urgent drainage with a stent or nephrostomy if obstructed and infected. Stone size is the main determinant of initial treatment, and most small stones pass spontaneously. Alpha blockers and calcium-channel blockers can aid passage by relaxing the ureter. Stones unlikely to pass are candidates for minimally invasive treatment such as shock-wave lithotripsy. For adults with recurrent calcium oxalate stones and high urinary calcium, NICE CKS advises considering a thiazide once salt intake has been reduced; potassium citrate and allopurinol are used for other recurrent stone types.

    Prevention

    Drink enough to pass 2 to 2.5 litres of urine a day, limit salt and animal protein, keep normal calcium intake, lemon juice for citrate; specific drugs for recurrent formers. Recurrence is common, approaching half of people within ten years, which is why fluid intake, dietary review and, for repeated stones, metabolic assessment with targeted medication are emphasised.

    Conditions

    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Bladder cancer Bladder cancer is cancer that forms in the tissues of the bladder, the organ that stores urine.
    • Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) A kidney infection, also called pyelonephritis, is a urinary tract infection that affects one or both kidneys.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.

    Drug classes

    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.

    Guides and news

    • Blood in urine: causes and what happens next Blood in the urine is not usually serious, but the NHS advises seeing a GP every time, even if the amount is small.
    • Kidney stones: symptoms and treatment Kidney stones form when chemicals in urine build up; small ones may cause no symptoms, while larger ones can cause severe pain.
