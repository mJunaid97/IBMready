    Osteoporosis

    Also known as: thin bones, brittle bones

    Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.

    When to seek care

    Any fracture from a minor fall after 50, sudden back pain in an older person, or loss of more than 4 cm in height should prompt assessment.

    Overview

    Bone is constantly remodelled; after the peak mass reached around 30, resorption slowly outpaces formation, sharply so in women after the menopause. Osteoporosis is silent until a bone breaks: the wrist, spine or hip. Hip fractures in older adults carry a high risk of death and loss of independence, and are largely preventable. It is the major cause of fractures in postmenopausal women and in older men, and is often called a silent disease: there are no symptoms until a bone breaks, so it is frequently diagnosed only when a fall or sudden impact causes a fracture. Weakened bone may also break during ordinary activities such as bending, lifting or even coughing. When the spine is involved, height is lost and a stooped posture develops. If the diet does not supply enough calcium the body takes it from bone, and sometimes bone loss occurs without any identifiable cause or simply runs in families.

    Causes

    • Oestrogen loss at the menopause
    • Ageing
    • Long-term steroid treatment
    • Low calcium and vitamin D, malabsorption
    • Overactive thyroid or parathyroid, low testosterone
    • Immobility
    • Chronic conditions and medicines other than steroids; sometimes no cause is found

    Risk factors

    • Female sex, early menopause, or long gaps in sex-hormone exposure (including after removal of the ovaries or testes)
    • Family history of hip fracture
    • Low body weight
    • Smoking and alcohol
    • Previous fragility fracture
    • Rheumatoid arthritis, coeliac disease, anorexia
    • White or Asian ancestry
    • Thin bones running in the family

    Symptoms

    Back pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Loss of height, stooped posture (kyphosis)
    • Fragility fracture after a fall from standing height
    • Fracture from an everyday stress such as bending, lifting or coughing

    Complications

    • Hip, vertebral and wrist fractures
    • Chronic back pain
    • Loss of independence

    Diagnosis

    DXA bone density scan (a T-score of −2.5 or lower), fracture risk calculators (FRAX), spine X-ray for vertebral fractures, and blood tests for vitamin D, calcium, thyroid and other secondary causes. Bone mineral density is measured non-invasively by DXA at the hip and spine, which NIAMS describes as the most common test for measuring bone density, used to diagnose osteoporosis, predict fracture risk and monitor treatment; a heel ultrasound can screen for bone loss but a DXA scan is still needed for the diagnosis. Clinicians also weigh fracture history, lifestyle, medical history and medicines, and may estimate future fracture risk with an online tool such as FRAX or QFracture. Screening is directed at older women and at younger women with additional risk factors.

    Treatment

    Calcium and vitamin D sufficiency, weight-bearing and balance exercise, fall prevention; bisphosphonates (alendronate) first line, denosumab or teriparatide for severe disease; treat secondary causes. Treatment combines lifestyle change (diet, exercise, fall prevention and cutting back on tobacco and alcohol) and calcium and vitamin D supplements with medication. Bisphosphonates are the main medicines used to prevent and treat osteoporosis in postmenopausal women; denosumab lessens bone loss and increases bone density. When osteoporosis comes from another condition or a medicine, the prescriber reviews the cause and whether the medicine is still needed (a medicine is never stopped or changed without their advice), and bone-protective treatment is often started at the same time.

    Prevention

    Build peak bone mass with exercise and calcium in youth, keep active, do not smoke, limit alcohol, ensure vitamin D, review medicines that cause falls or bone loss.

    What does osteoporosis feel like?

    Osteoporosis usually causes no symptoms until a bone breaks. NIAMS describes it as a silent disease, because people may not know they have it until a bone breaks. NHS 111 Wales says it is not usually painful until a bone is broken, although broken bones in the spine are a common cause of long-term pain.

    The hip, spine and wrist are the usual places for fractures. A bone broken after a minor fall or injury is known as a fragility fracture, because a healthy bone would usually survive the same fall. Spinal fractures can be silent, and a person may not always be aware that one has happened. The NHS 111 Wales entry adds that a cough or sneeze can sometimes partly collapse a spinal bone.

    Warning signs of a spinal fracture include severe back pain, loss of height, or a stooped or hunched posture. Some sources say a weakened vertebra may fracture even without a fall or significant force.

    How is osteoporosis diagnosed, and what is a bone scan like?

    A bone density scan, also called a DEXA or DXA scan, uses low-dose X-rays to see how dense, or strong, the bones are. MedlinePlus describes it as painless, and notes that a person receives more radiation from a chest X-ray than from a DEXA scan. For the central scan, the person lies on a soft table while the scanner passes over the lower spine and hip, and must stay still.

    MedlinePlus says people may be told to stop calcium supplements before the test, and the timing varies between sources and facilities. People may eat and drink as normal and continue their usual prescription medicines.

    NHS 111 Wales says the result is usually used alongside a fracture risk assessment. Treatment depends on factors such as age, sex, fracture risk and previous injuries, not on the scan result alone. One research review notes that bone density is a continuous risk factor for fracture, with no natural threshold, and that scores overlap between people who do and do not fracture.

    How is osteoporosis treated, and what are the side effects?

    NHS 111 Wales suggests regular exercise, a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, and fall-prevention steps at home. For medicines, it lists the bisphosphonates alendronic acid, risedronic acid, ibandronic acid and zoledronic acid, which work by slowing bone breakdown.

    Other medicines exist. Versus Arthritis says denosumab is recommended for postmenopausal women who cannot take bisphosphonates, and the NHS says it may be recommended if other medicines cannot be taken or osteoporosis is severe. Teriparatide, a bone-building medicine, is generally positioned for severe osteoporosis at high risk of further fracture, according to NHS guidance for primary care.

    Side effects of bisphosphonates can include irritation of the gullet, according to NHS 111 Wales, which also says osteonecrosis of the jaw, a problem with the jaw bone, is rare. Research reviews report that the risk of an unusual thigh bone fracture, called an atypical femoral fracture, rises with the length of bisphosphonate treatment, although absolute risks are small. A task force report from the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research suggests periodic reassessment of treatment.

    Living with osteoporosis: exercise, food and avoiding falls

    Versus Arthritis says weight-bearing exercise is good for maintaining bone strength and reducing the risk of osteoporosis. It adds that vitamin D is needed for the body to absorb and process calcium. The NHS describes weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening activities as the strongest options for bone strength, with walking, hiking and dancing as examples. MedlinePlus lists balance training, which can include tai chi and yoga, alongside weight-bearing activity and resistance work.

    MedlinePlus advises avoiding exercise that carries a risk of falling and high-impact exercise that could cause fractures in older adults.

    NHS 111 Wales suggests a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, stopping smoking and cutting down alcohol, and fall-prevention steps at home. MedlinePlus examples include removing throw rugs, adding grab bars and non-slip flooring in bathrooms, leaving night lights on, and wearing low-heeled, well-fitting shoes, including slippers.

    Why do the menopause, medicines and lifestyle affect bone?

    The NHS says women are more at risk than men because hormone changes at the menopause directly affect bone density, and describes oestrogen as essential for healthy bones. It also lists an early menopause as a risk factor.

    Versus Arthritis lists steroids, especially those taken by mouth, as a risk factor,. NIAMS notes that some cancer medicines and glucocorticoid steroids may increase the risk.

    NIAMS calls chronic heavy drinking a significant risk factor and says smoking is a risk factor for osteoporosis and fracture. It adds that researchers are still studying whether the effect of smoking comes from tobacco use alone or because people who smoke have more risk factors. NIAMS also notes that the risk may increase if a parent has a history of osteoporosis or hip fracture.

    Where does care for a fracture start?

    A suspected hip fracture should go to an Emergency Department rather than a minor injury unit, according to an NHS referral document. In an emergency, such as a suspected hip fracture or a severe injury, the UK emergency number is 999.

    A patient leaflet on spinal fractures says people with serious symptoms should go to the nearest Emergency Department immediately by ambulance, and move as little as possible until seen there. For most spinal fractures, follow-up is usually with the GP, and painkillers and mobility advice come from there.

    The NHS 111 Wales entry names the Royal Osteoporosis Society as the UK charity with more detailed information about the condition.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can osteoporosis cause symptoms before a bone breaks?

    Osteoporosis usually causes no symptoms until a bone breaks, and the break often follows a minor fall. Warning signs of a spinal fracture include severe back pain, loss of height, or a stooped or hunched posture. Follow-up for spinal fractures is usually with the GP, while a suspected hip fracture goes to an Emergency Department (A&E); in an emergency the UK number is 999.

    Does a bone density scan hurt?

    A DEXA scan is described as painless and uses a low dose of X-ray. For the central scan, the person lies on a soft table while the scanner passes over the lower spine and hip, and stays still. MedlinePlus notes that a chest X-ray gives more radiation than a DEXA scan.

    Why are women affected more often than men?

    The NHS says women are more at risk because hormone changes at the menopause directly affect bone density, and oestrogen is essential for healthy bones. Other risk factors named by NIAMS include glucocorticoid steroids, chronic heavy drinking, smoking and a parent with a history of osteoporosis or hip fracture.

    Is exercise good for people with osteoporosis?

    The NHS describes weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening activities as the strongest options for bone strength, with walking, hiking and dancing as examples. MedlinePlus lists balance training, which can include tai chi and yoga. MedlinePlus also advises avoiding exercise that carries a risk of falling and high-impact exercise that could cause fractures in older adults.

    Do osteoporosis medicines have side effects?

    NHS 111 Wales says bisphosphonate side effects can include irritation of the gullet, and that osteonecrosis of the jaw is rare. Research reviews report that the risk of an unusual thigh bone fracture rises with the length of treatment, although absolute risks are small. A task force report suggests periodic reassessment of treatment.

    Conditions

    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.
    • Coeliac disease Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system reacts to gluten and damages the lining of the small bowel.
    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.
    • Gender dysphoria (gender incongruence) The distress that can arise when a person's gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth.
    • Menopause and perimenopause Menopause is when periods stop altogether, and perimenopause is the stage before this when periods change and symptoms begin.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Vitamin D deficiency A shortage of vitamin D in the body, which can weaken bones and muscles over time.

    Symptoms

    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.
    • 25-hydroxyvitamin D The storage form of vitamin D that the liver makes, the best single measure of vitamin D status and the value the vitamin D test reports.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.

    Medications

    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Androgens Steroid hormones that act on the androgen receptor.
    • Bisphosphonates Medicines that slow bone loss by disabling the cells that dissolve bone.
    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Oestrogens Steroid hormones that act on the oestrogen receptor.
    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.

    Guides and news

    • Bone health: what helps keep bones strong NHS pages link bone health to weight-bearing exercise, calcium, vitamin D, not smoking and keeping alcohol low.
    • DXA bone density scan: what it shows A DXA scan measures bone density, usually at the hip and spine, using low-dose X-rays.
    • Falls in older people and reducing the risk Strength and balance exercises and simple changes at home can lower the risk of falls in older people, according to the NHS.
    • Menopause: symptoms and the help available HRT is the main medicine for menopause symptoms, and non-hormone options exist.
    • NICE publishes osteoporosis risk guideline NICE published guideline NG259 on 29 July 2026, covering how adults at risk of fragility fractures should be assessed.
    • How much exercise adults need each week The NHS says adults aged 19 to 64 should do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus strength work.
    • Vitamins and supplements: who may need them The NHS says a varied and balanced diet should provide enough of several vitamins, but advises supplements for some people.
    • Vitamin D blood test: who needs one? NHS guidance does not recommend routine vitamin D tests for people without symptoms, and testing is kept for specific symptoms or conditions.
