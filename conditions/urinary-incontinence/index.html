    Urinary incontinence

    Also known as: bladder leakage, stress incontinence, urge incontinence, overactive bladder, bladder weakness, loss of bladder control

    The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control, caused by weakness of the pelvic floor and urethral sphincter, an overactive bladder muscle, obstruction to the outflow of urine or damage to the nerves that control the bladder.

    Overview

    Continence depends on the bladder storing urine at low pressure while the urethral sphincter and the pelvic floor muscles keep the outlet closed, and on the brain and spinal cord timing relaxation of the outlet with contraction of the bladder wall (the detrusor). Incontinence follows when any part of this fails. Stress incontinence is leakage when coughing, sneezing, laughing, lifting or exercising raises the pressure in the abdomen above what a weakened sphincter and pelvic floor can resist; it is the commonest type in women, typically after childbirth and the menopause, and it also follows prostate surgery in men. Trans women keep the prostate after vaginoplasty; leakage after gender-affirming genital surgery relates to the shortened, repositioned urethra and the pelvic floor rather than to removal of the prostate. Urgency incontinence is leakage with a sudden, compelling need to pass urine, usually with frequency and waking at night, caused by the detrusor contracting before the bladder is full (overactive bladder). Mixed incontinence combines the two. Overflow incontinence is the constant dribbling of a bladder that cannot empty, usually because of an enlarged prostate or nerve damage, and functional incontinence is leakage in someone whose bladder works but who cannot reach the toilet in time. Incontinence is common, affects women more often than men and becomes more frequent with age, but it is not a normal or inevitable part of ageing and most people can be helped.

    Causes

    • Weakness of the pelvic floor and urethral sphincter after pregnancy and vaginal birth, with the menopause, or after prostate surgery in men (stress incontinence)
    • Overactivity of the detrusor muscle, often without a clear cause, or from irritation of the bladder by infection, stones or a tumour (urgency incontinence)
    • Neurological disease that disrupts bladder control: stroke, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury and nerve damage from diabetes
    • Obstruction to outflow from an enlarged prostate, a urethral stricture or a severe prolapse, leading to chronic retention with overflow
    • Medicines that increase urine output or affect bladder control, including diuretics such as furosemide, sedatives, some antidepressants, alpha-blockers and drugs with anticholinergic effects
    • Pelvic organ prolapse, pelvic surgery or radiotherapy, and rarely a fistula between the bladder and the vagina
    • Temporary causes such as urinary tract infection, constipation, or excess alcohol and caffeine

    Risk factors

    • Pregnancy and vaginal childbirth, especially with a large baby or an instrumental delivery
    • Increasing age and the menopause
    • Being overweight or obese
    • Prostate enlargement or prostatectomy in men
    • Chronic cough, constipation and smoking
    • Neurological conditions, diabetes, and limited mobility or cognitive impairment
    • A family history of incontinence

    Symptoms

    Abdominal pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Leakage seen from the urethra when the person coughs with a comfortably full bladder
    • Weak pelvic floor muscle contraction, or pelvic organ prolapse, on vaginal examination
    • An enlarged prostate on rectal examination, or a painless distended bladder that can be felt in chronic retention
    • Vaginal atrophy after the menopause
    • Neurological signs such as leg weakness, altered sensation around the perineum or abnormal reflexes when a nerve cause is suspected
    • Sore, irritated skin in the groin and perineum from repeated wetting

    Complications

    • Skin irritation, rashes and pressure damage from persistent wetness
    • Recurrent urinary tract infections, particularly when the bladder does not empty fully
    • Falls and fractures in older adults hurrying to the toilet, especially at night
    • Disturbed sleep, embarrassment, social withdrawal, depression and reduced physical activity
    • Kidney damage from back-pressure in long-standing, untreated retention with overflow
    • Loss of independence; incontinence is a common reason for admission to a care home

    Diagnosis

    The history sorts the leakage into stress, urgency or mixed by asking when it happens, and covers fluid, caffeine and alcohol intake, medicines, bowel habit, childbirth, prostate symptoms and any neurological symptoms. A bladder diary kept for at least three days records the volumes drunk and passed, episodes of urgency and leakage, and night-time voids. Urinalysis excludes infection and blood, and a urine culture is sent if infection is suspected. Examination includes the abdomen for a distended bladder, a vaginal examination for prolapse, atrophy and pelvic floor strength, a rectal examination of the prostate in men, and a neurological check if the story suggests a nerve cause. A bladder scan after voiding measures the residual urine when emptying seems incomplete. NICE recommends urodynamic studies before surgery for stress incontinence when the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms are mixed, and cystoscopy for blood in the urine, bladder pain or recurrent infection; blood in the urine, a pelvic mass, previous pelvic surgery or radiotherapy and suspected neurological disease are reasons for specialist referral.

    Treatment

    Treatment starts with conservative measures; NICE NG123 covers women and the same principles apply to men. Advice includes less caffeine, fluid intake that is neither excessive nor restricted, weight loss if overweight, stopping smoking and treating constipation. For stress incontinence a supervised programme of pelvic floor muscle training for at least three months is first line and is then continued as a habit; if it is not enough, the options are surgery (colposuspension, an autologous fascial sling or a mid-urethral sling, with mesh use restricted in the UK), urethral bulking injections, or duloxetine, which NICE does not recommend routinely. Vaginal oestrogen helps postmenopausal women with vaginal atrophy and urgency symptoms. For urgency incontinence and overactive bladder, bladder training for at least six weeks comes first, then an antimuscarinic or the beta-3 agonist mirabegron; for resistant symptoms, botulinum toxin injected into the bladder wall, which can leave the bladder unable to empty and so is offered only to people who are willing and able to learn clean intermittent self-catheterisation, or percutaneous tibial or sacral nerve stimulation in specialist services. Antimuscarinics commonly cause dry mouth and constipation and can affect memory and balance, so NICE advises avoiding immediate-release oxybutynin in frail older people; mirabegron needs blood pressure monitoring. Overflow incontinence is treated by relieving the cause, with intermittent self-catheterisation if the bladder cannot empty. Men with leakage after prostate surgery may be offered a male sling or an artificial sphincter, and pelvic floor physiotherapy is also used after gender-affirming genital surgery.

    Prevention

    Pelvic floor muscle training during and after pregnancy, and continued through life, reduces the risk of stress incontinence; NICE advises a supervised programme of pelvic floor muscle training in a first pregnancy as a preventive measure. Keeping to a healthy weight, not smoking, avoiding constipation, limiting caffeine and alcohol, and drinking neither far too much nor too little fluid all help. Treating a chronic cough, reviewing medicines that affect the bladder and good control of diabetes lower the risk, and men can learn pelvic floor exercises before prostate surgery.

    When to seek care

    See a GP about any leakage of urine that bothers you, however small; it is common, rarely mentioned and usually improvable, and a GP can refer to continence services or pelvic health physiotherapy. Seek urgent help for a sudden inability to pass urine with a painful, swollen lower abdomen (acute retention); for new incontinence with severe back pain, numbness around the buttocks or genitals, or weakness in the legs, which can mean compression of the nerves at the base of the spine and is an emergency; and for blood in the urine, pain on passing urine with fever, or sudden confusion in an older person, which may indicate infection.
