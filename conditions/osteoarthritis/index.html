    Osteoarthritis

    Also known as: OA, degenerative joint disease, wear-and-tear arthritis

    The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness, most often in the knees, hips, hands and spine.

    When to seek care

    See a doctor for joint pain limiting daily activities; urgent care for a hot, red, swollen joint with fever.

    Overview

    Osteoarthritis is not simply wear: it is a failed repair process in which cartilage thins, bone thickens and forms spurs (osteophytes), and the joint lining becomes intermittently inflamed. Pain builds over years, worse with use and at the end of the day. It affects most people over 65 to some degree. Pain and stiffness are the hallmark. Early on, symptoms may appear only after exercise, but over time they can become constant, and they often come and go in episodes related to activity levels and even the weather. Swelling, a reduced range of motion and weakness or wasting of the muscles around the joint also occur. The joints most often affected are those near the ends of the fingers, the base of the thumb, the neck, the lower back, the knees and the hips. The disease is thought to stem from mechanical stress on the joint combined with low-grade inflammation, and the problems usually come on over years.

    Causes

    • Mechanical load on a susceptible joint: age, previous injury, malalignment, obesity
    • Genetic factors, especially in hand OA
    • Abnormal development or alignment of the joint or limb

    Risk factors

    • Age over 50
    • Obesity (knees and hips)
    • Previous joint injury or surgery
    • Heavy manual work or elite sport
    • Female sex
    • Family history

    Symptoms

    Joint painKnee painBack painSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • Bony enlargement, crepitus, reduced range of motion
    • Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes on the fingers
    • Effusion in a flared knee
    • Antalgic gait
    • Weakness or wasting of the muscles around the affected joint

    Complications

    • Disability and loss of independence
    • Falls
    • Chronic pain and depression

    Diagnosis

    Clinical in a person over 45 with activity-related pain and brief morning stiffness. X-rays show joint-space narrowing, sclerosis and osteophytes but correlate poorly with symptoms; blood tests are normal and are used to exclude inflammatory arthritis. Early joint damage does not usually show on X-ray. MRI shows soft-tissue damage and is especially useful when a joint locks or gives way. Blood tests and joint-fluid sampling are used to rule out other causes such as infection or gout rather than to confirm osteoarthritis.

    Treatment

    Exercise and strengthening, weight loss, education and pacing are the core treatments. Topical NSAIDs, paracetamol and oral NSAIDs for pain; walking aids and braces; steroid injections for flares. Joint replacement when pain limits life despite these. There is no cure. The NHS describes three main strands: lifestyle measures, medicines to relieve pain, and supportive therapies that make everyday activities easier; surgery is considered only in some cases where other treatments have not helped. Regular exercise that keeps a person active, builds up muscle and strengthens the joints usually improves symptoms. NIAMS advises starting slowly with a plan from a doctor or physiotherapist that combines range-of-motion and stretching work with strengthening using weights or resistance bands, to reduce pain and stiffness and increase flexibility, strength and endurance.

    Prevention

    Healthy weight, regular low-impact exercise, strong muscles around the joints, and avoiding joint injury.

    What does osteoarthritis feel like, and does it get worse?

    Pain and stiffness in the affected joints are the main features of osteoarthritis. NHS sources say the symptoms may come and go in episodes, which can be related to activity levels and even the weather. Pain is often worse with activity, and stiffness is typically worst after rest or in the morning, easing once a person starts to move. Versus Arthritis says a short spell of stiffness after rest is common.

    The knees, hips and small joints of the hands are the most common areas affected, and the big toes and feet can also be involved.

    The NHS says the condition does not necessarily get any worse over time. Symptoms vary from person to person and between different joints, and in more severe cases they can be continuous. Some people have osteoarthritis without many symptoms at all.

    How is osteoarthritis diagnosed?

    A GP or other healthcare professional asks about the symptoms and examines the joints. NHS inform says there is no single definitive test for osteoarthritis. A NICE draft evidence review says that, without features pointing to another problem, osteoarthritis can be diagnosed through history and examination alone.

    NHS inform says an X-ray is not currently recommended for diagnosing osteoarthritis, although one can show how much joint damage there is. For the hip and knee, an X-ray is sometimes used to help the diagnosis. Versus Arthritis says doctors rarely use an X-ray or blood test for knee problems, mainly to rule out other causes. MedlinePlus notes that X-rays may show loss of joint space and bone spurs, and that some people who have these changes have no symptoms.

    MedlinePlus says blood tests do not help diagnose osteoarthritis, but they can check for other conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or gout. NHS inform lists joint pain, tenderness, stiffness, swelling and restricted movement as reasons to contact a GP practice.

    What treatments are used for osteoarthritis?

    The NHS says osteoarthritis cannot be cured but that a number of treatments can relieve the symptoms. It groups the main options into lifestyle measures, pain medication and supportive therapies. NICE guidance calls therapeutic exercise and weight management core treatments, and NHS inform describes keeping the joint active and strengthening the muscles around it as the best way to manage symptoms.

    For pain, the NHS describes creams containing an anti-inflammatory medicine (topical NSAIDs) as often used for the knees and hands, and NICE advises offering one for knee osteoarthritis. Capsaicin cream is another option, and burning is its most common side effect. The NHS describes paracetamol as suitable for short-term use. NSAID tablets are generally considered if creams do not help, and because they can wear down the stomach lining, a medicine that reduces stomach acid may be given alongside them. Opioids such as codeine are generally limited to short-term use because of their side effects.

    Steroid injections are sometimes offered when other treatments have not worked, and the NHS says they can ease pain for several weeks or months. NICE says to consider a corticosteroid injection into the joint when other medicines are ineffective or unsuitable.

    What can help with osteoarthritis day to day?

    The NHS says supportive aids such as shock-absorbing footwear, insoles, braces and walking sticks can reduce strain on the joints during everyday activities. Patient leaflets from NHS services describe pacing, which means balancing rest and activity, and note that taking on too much on a low-pain day can lead to worse pain afterwards. Supportive shoes are described as improving comfort and balance, and a walking stick can take pressure off an affected hip.

    The leaflets describe heat as often suggested for stiffness or aching and cold for a sudden flare-up or swelling, with packs wrapped in a cloth. A physiotherapist can help build a plan guided by a person's symptoms, physical function and goals.

    The NHS says GPs no longer prescribe glucosamine and chondroitin supplements on the NHS, because there is no strong evidence that they work.

    When is surgery considered?

    NHS inform says surgery is reserved for cases where other treatments have not been effective or where a joint is severely affected. It may greatly improve symptoms, but it cannot be guaranteed to resolve them fully. Versus Arthritis describes lifestyle changes, medication, therapies and, rarely, surgery as the ways to help.

    MedlinePlus says non-surgical treatments often delay surgery or keep symptoms mild enough that it is not needed. The operations it lists include trimming damaged cartilage through a keyhole procedure (arthroscopy), realigning bone (osteotomy), fusing a joint, and partial or total joint replacement. NHS inform says joint replacement is the most common surgical option and that the decision is made with an orthopaedic surgeon, a doctor who specialises in bone and joint surgery.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can osteoarthritis be cured?

    The NHS says there is no cure for osteoarthritis, but treatments can relieve the symptoms. It groups the main options into lifestyle measures, pain medication and supportive therapies. The NHS also says the condition does not necessarily get any worse over time, and symptoms vary from person to person. NICE guidance describes therapeutic exercise and weight management as core treatments.

    Is an X-ray needed to diagnose osteoarthritis?

    NHS inform says an X-ray is not currently recommended for diagnosing osteoarthritis, although one can show how much joint damage is present. A GP asks about the symptoms and examines the joints, and there is no single definitive test. MedlinePlus notes that some people who have X-ray changes have no symptoms. Blood tests are used to look for other conditions, not to confirm osteoarthritis.

    Do osteoarthritis symptoms come and go?

    NHS sources say the symptoms may come and go in episodes, which can be related to activity levels and even the weather. Pain is often worse with activity, and stiffness is typically worst after rest or in the morning, easing with movement. Severity varies between people and between joints, and some people have osteoarthritis without many symptoms.

    Do glucosamine and chondroitin supplements help?

    The NHS says GPs no longer prescribe glucosamine and chondroitin on the NHS, because there is no strong evidence that they work. The options the NHS does describe are lifestyle measures, pain medication such as creams, and supportive therapies and aids such as footwear, insoles, braces and walking sticks.

    Does everyone with osteoarthritis need surgery?

    No. NHS inform says surgery is reserved for cases where other treatments have not been effective or where a joint is severely affected. MedlinePlus says non-surgical treatments often delay surgery or keep symptoms mild enough that it is not needed. When surgery is considered, the options include fusing a joint and partial or total joint replacement, decided with an orthopaedic surgeon.

    Conditions

    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • ACL injury A sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, the band inside the knee that stops the tibia sliding forward on the femur and controls rotation.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.
    • Fibromyalgia A long-term condition that causes pain all over the body.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Knee replacement The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when…
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Naproxen Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis.
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.

    Drug classes

    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.

    Guides and news

    • Getting active with a long-term condition UK guidance describes building up activity gradually from the current level.
    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Knee pain: causes and when to get help Knee pain can often be treated at home, and NHS 111 is the route for a badly swollen knee or a hot, red knee with a high temperature.
    • Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.
