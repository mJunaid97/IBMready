    Chronic kidney disease

    Also known as: CKD, kidney failure, renal failure

    Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine, for more than three months.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for breathlessness, chest pain, severe drowsiness, or a sudden fall in urine output; routine review for swelling, frothy urine or persistently high blood pressure. CKD is usually found by routine testing rather than symptoms, so people in at-risk groups are advised to ask about kidney checks.

    Overview

    The kidneys lose nephrons slowly and silently, most often from diabetes and high blood pressure. Symptoms appear only when function is severely reduced, so CKD is found by blood and urine tests in at-risk people. Progression can be slowed for years; a minority reach kidney failure needing dialysis or transplantation. Chronic kidney disease means the kidneys are damaged or structurally abnormal and cannot filter blood as they should, with function declining over months to years. Because early disease causes no symptoms, NHS guidance recommends testing for people with high blood pressure, diabetes or a family history of kidney disease. When symptoms do appear in advanced disease they are non-specific: tiredness, swollen ankles, feet or hands from fluid retention, shortness of breath, nausea, loss of appetite and blood in the urine, all of which overlap with many less serious conditions. An episode of acute kidney injury can also leave lasting damage that becomes CKD.

    Causes

    • Diabetes
    • High blood pressure
    • Glomerulonephritis
    • Polycystic kidney disease
    • Obstruction (prostate, stones)
    • Recurrent infection, drugs (NSAIDs, lithium), reflux
    • Acute kidney injury that does not fully recover

    Risk factors

    • Age
    • Cardiovascular disease
    • Obesity, smoking
    • Family history
    • Black, South Asian and Indigenous ancestry
    • Episodes of acute kidney injury
    • Heart disease

    Symptoms

    FatigueSwelling (oedema)NauseaWeaknessShortness of breath

    What a clinician may find

    • High blood pressure
    • Oedema
    • Pallor from anaemia
    • Late: itching, muscle twitching, drowsiness
    • Blood in the urine

    Complications

    • Cardiovascular disease (the leading cause of death in CKD)
    • Anaemia
    • Bone disease and fractures
    • High potassium, acidosis, fluid overload
    • End-stage kidney failure
    • Stroke
    • Increased susceptibility to infection
    • Low calcium and high phosphate levels
    • Loss of appetite and depression

    Diagnosis

    Estimated GFR from blood creatinine and the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, repeated over three months; the two together both make the diagnosis and set its severity, staged G1 to G5 by filtration rate and A1 to A3 by albuminuria. Albumin is a protein that passes into the urine when the filtering units are damaged, so it is an early marker even while the eGFR is still normal. Testing is the only reliable way to know how well the kidneys are working, which is why at-risk groups are screened rather than waiting for symptoms. Ultrasound of the kidneys shows size, obstruction and cysts, and further tests look for the cause, because the cause changes management.

    Treatment

    Blood pressure control with ACE inhibitors or ARBs, SGLT2 inhibitors, statins, glucose control, salt restriction, stopping smoking, avoiding NSAIDs, dose adjustment of medicines; treating anaemia and bone disease; preparing for dialysis or transplantation as function declines. CKD cannot be cured, but progression can often be slowed for years. NIDDK describes blood pressure control as the most important single step; ACE inhibitors or ARBs are the usual choice because they also reduce protein leak, and loop diuretics manage fluid. Blood glucose and cholesterol are treated alongside. Lifestyle measures include lower-salt food, limiting alcohol, staying physically active and not smoking. Anaemia, bone and mineral disorder and fluid overload are treated as they arise. When function falls far enough (kidney failure), dialysis or a kidney transplant is needed for survival, and preparation begins well in advance.

    Prevention

    Control diabetes and blood pressure, keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, avoid regular NSAIDs, stay hydrated during illness, have kidney checks if at risk. People with diabetes, high blood pressure or a family history of kidney disease are offered regular eGFR and urine albumin checks so that damage is found early.

    How does chronic kidney disease feel and progress?

    Early chronic kidney disease usually causes no symptoms, and it is often found by chance when a blood or urine test is done for another reason. Symptoms can appear at a more advanced stage, including tiredness, swollen ankles, feet or hands, shortness of breath, feeling sick and blood in the urine. Later kidney failure can also bring itching and muscle cramps.

    Doctors describe the condition in stages, and a higher stage means more severe disease. In stage 1 the filtering is normal but there is other evidence of kidney damage, and stage 2 is a slight decrease in function. Stage 3 is split into 3a and 3b, and stage 5 is kidney failure. Only a small proportion of people with the condition go on to kidney failure, which the NHS puts at around 2 in 100 people. When the kidneys do stop working, this usually happens slowly.

    How is it checked?

    Chronic kidney disease can be diagnosed with blood and urine tests. The blood test is used to estimate how well the kidneys are filtering, and the urine test looks for protein and blood. One abnormal result is not usually enough, and repeated tests are used to confirm the diagnosis. The blood test result, called the eGFR, is used to give a stage from 1 to 5.

    In many cases the condition is only found when a routine blood or urine test, done for another problem, shows that the kidneys may not be working normally. NHS guidance lists high blood pressure, diabetes and heart disease as reasons for regular testing. The NHS says most people with the condition can control it with medicine and regular check-ups.

    What treatments are used?

    There is no cure for chronic kidney disease, but treatment can help relieve the symptoms and stop it getting worse. The NHS describes lifestyle changes to stay as healthy as possible, together with medicines to manage problems such as high blood pressure and high cholesterol. ACE inhibitors are often used for blood pressure in this condition, and statins are prescribed to reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease, meaning heart and blood vessel problems.

    Anaemia, a shortage of red blood cells, can develop in kidney disease, and the NHS says tablets or injections may be given to help the body make more red blood cells. Medical reviews describe anaemia in kidney disease as traditionally treated with iron preparations or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents. Medicines can also be used for other symptoms of kidney failure, such as breathlessness, loss of appetite and itchy skin.

    What are dialysis and a kidney transplant?

    For a small number of people the kidneys eventually stop working, and dialysis or a transplant is then considered. Dialysis is a way of removing waste products and extra fluid from the blood. NIDDK describes haemodialysis as a machine filtering the blood outside the body, while peritoneal dialysis uses the lining of the abdomen to filter waste and extra fluid.

    A kidney transplant is the other main option, and the kidney can come from a living or a deceased donor. NHS Blood and Transplant says living donors account for roughly 30 in 100 kidney transplants, with a much shorter wait than for a kidney from a deceased donor. People with a transplant take anti-rejection medicines, and NIDDK notes these can cause other health problems. Some people choose not to have dialysis or a transplant, and conservative care then continues their care without either, including treatment for symptoms.

    Living with chronic kidney disease

    A hospital leaflet from an NHS trust says that with good care many people with chronic kidney disease live long and active lives. It favours plenty of fruit and vegetables, including pulses, with smaller amounts of animal protein such as meat, fish and eggs. Kidney Care UK says a diet low in salt and rich in fibre and potassium from fruit, vegetables, wholegrains, nuts, seeds and pulses helps to lower blood pressure and reduce the risk of complications.

    Kidney Care UK also notes that studies have shown exercise can improve kidney function. NHS 111 Wales lists some long-term medicines, including NSAIDs and lithium, among the causes of chronic kidney disease. The hospital leaflet says over-the-counter anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen are avoided unless a medical professional advises otherwise, because these medicines can harm the kidneys in people with kidney disease.

    Complications and outlook

    Heart and blood vessel disease is a major concern in chronic kidney disease. One review says cardiovascular complications are the most common cause of death in people with kidney failure on regular dialysis, and that most people with progressive chronic kidney disease die before reaching kidney failure because of the high rate of cardiovascular death.

    Many people with stage 3, 4 and 5 disease develop anaemia. Mineral and bone disorder can also develop in advanced kidney disease and is linked to a higher risk of bone fractures, heart and blood vessel events and death. NHS 111 Wales gives the proportion of people with the condition who reach kidney failure as around 1 in 50.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can chronic kidney disease be cured?

    There is no cure for chronic kidney disease, but treatment can help relieve symptoms and stop it getting worse. The NHS says most people can control the condition with medicine and regular check-ups. A hospital leaflet from an NHS trust adds that with good care many people live long and active lives.

    Does it always lead to kidney failure?

    No, only a small proportion of people do. The NHS says around 2 in 100 people with the condition reach kidney failure, and NHS 111 Wales gives the figure as around 1 in 50. When the kidneys do stop working, this usually happens slowly rather than suddenly.

    What is the difference between the two kinds of dialysis?

    Dialysis removes waste products and extra fluid from the blood. In haemodialysis, a machine filters the blood outside the body. In peritoneal dialysis, the lining of the abdomen is used to filter waste and extra fluid. NIDDK describes a kidney transplant and conservative management as the other treatment paths for kidney failure.

    Does everyone with kidney failure have dialysis or a transplant?

    No. NIDDK describes conservative management as continuing a person's care without dialysis or a kidney transplant. The NHS says medicines can be used to treat symptoms of kidney failure such as breathlessness, loss of appetite and itchy skin, as part of supportive care.

    Which painkillers can affect the kidneys?

    A hospital leaflet from an NHS trust says over-the-counter anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen can harm the kidneys in people with kidney disease and are avoided unless a medical professional advises otherwise. NHS 111 Wales also lists long-term use of some medicines, including NSAIDs and lithium, among the causes of chronic kidney disease.

    Does diet matter in chronic kidney disease?

    A hospital leaflet from an NHS trust favours plenty of fruit and vegetables, including pulses, with smaller amounts of animal protein such as meat, fish and eggs. Kidney Care UK describes a diet low in salt and rich in fibre and potassium from fruit, vegetables, wholegrains, nuts, seeds and pulses as helping to lower blood pressure and reduce the risk of complications.

    Conditions

    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Lupus (systemic lupus erythematosus) A long-term autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks healthy tissue, most often causing joint aches, skin rashes and tiredness.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Vitamin D deficiency A shortage of vitamin D in the body, which can weaken bones and muscles over time.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin is a prescription tablet from the SGLT2 inhibitor group, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Empagliflozin Empagliflozin is a prescription medicine used to treat type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Biguanides Metformin is the only biguanide in routine use and the first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it lowers glucose without causing hypoglycaemia or…
    • Contrast agents Substances given to make blood vessels, organs, the gut or joints stand out on imaging: iodinated agents for X-ray and CT, gadolinium agents for MRI.
    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
    • SGLT2 inhibitors SGLT2 inhibitors are tablets used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.
