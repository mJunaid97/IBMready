    Cervical cancer

    Also known as: cancer of the cervix, cervical carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix, cervical adenocarcinoma

    A malignant tumour of the cervix, the neck of the uterus, that develops almost always from persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV) after years of precancerous change.

    Overview

    Cervical cancer arises at the transformation zone, where the lining of the cervical canal meets that of the outer cervix. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV, mainly types 16 and 18, turns the cells there abnormal (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, CIN). Most infections clear and most CIN regresses, but some progresses to invasive cancer over years, which is why screening can find and treat the change first. It affects anyone with a cervix, including trans men and non-binary people.

    Causes

    • Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus, mainly types 16 and 18, passed on by skin-to-skin sexual contact
    • Viral proteins that disable the cell's tumour-suppressor controls, so that abnormal cells (CIN) accumulate further mutations over years
    • A very small number of cervical cancers are not associated with HPV

    Risk factors

    • Not being vaccinated against HPV and not attending cervical screening
    • Smoking, which makes it more likely that an HPV infection persists and progresses
    • A weakened immune system: HIV infection, organ transplant or long-term immunosuppressive medicines
    • Earlier first sexual intercourse and a greater number of sexual partners, because these increase exposure to HPV
    • Long-term use of the combined contraceptive pill (a small increase that falls after stopping)
    • Having had several full-term pregnancies
    • A previous high-grade abnormality (CIN) or another HPV-related cancer

    Unusual vaginal bleedingPelvic painBack pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Often none: precancer and early cancer are usually found only by screening
    • Bleeding between periods, after sex or after the menopause, or periods that become heavier
    • A persistent, unusual or smelly vaginal discharge, and pain or discomfort during sex
    • On examination, an abnormal-looking cervix that bleeds on contact; a hard, irregular mass in advanced disease
    • In advanced disease, swelling of a leg, blood in the urine or symptoms of kidney obstruction where the tumour blocks a ureter

    Complications

    • Spread to the vagina, parametrium, bladder, rectum and pelvic lymph nodes, and later to lung, liver and bone
    • Blockage of one or both ureters causing hydronephrosis and kidney failure
    • Fistulas between the bladder or rectum and the vagina, from tumour or from radiotherapy
    • Loss of fertility after radical surgery or chemoradiotherapy, and early menopause where the ovaries are removed or damaged by radiotherapy; a higher chance of preterm birth after cone biopsy or LLETZ
    • Long-term bowel, bladder and sexual effects of pelvic radiotherapy

    Diagnosis

    Screening detects HPV and abnormal cells before cancer develops: a sample of cells from the transformation zone is tested first for high-risk HPV and, if positive, examined under the microscope, and abnormal results lead to colposcopy. Symptoms such as unusual bleeding are investigated by speculum examination of the cervix and referral for colposcopy, not by a screening test. At colposcopy the cervix is examined under magnification after acetic acid is applied, and a biopsy or a loop excision (LLETZ) provides tissue for the pathologist. If cancer is confirmed, staging uses examination, MRI of the pelvis, CT of the chest and abdomen and sometimes PET-CT, with blood tests including kidney function; the extent of spread (the FIGO stage) determines treatment.

    Treatment

    Precancerous change (CIN) is treated by removing or destroying the transformation zone, usually with a large loop excision (LLETZ) under local anaesthetic or with a cone biopsy, after which HPV testing continues as a test of cure. Very early cancers can be removed by cone biopsy or, for those who wish to preserve fertility, by trachelectomy, which removes the cervix and upper vagina and leaves the uterus in place. Most early-stage cancers are treated by radical hysterectomy with removal of the pelvic lymph nodes, or by radiotherapy where surgery is unsuitable. Locally advanced disease is treated with chemoradiotherapy: external beam radiotherapy and brachytherapy to the cervix, with weekly platinum chemotherapy to sensitise the tumour. Cancer that has spread or returned is treated with chemotherapy, sometimes with targeted or immunotherapy drugs, and with palliative care to control symptoms.

    Prevention

    Two measures prevent most cervical cancer. HPV vaccination, offered in the UK to all children aged 12 to 13 and to some adults at higher risk, protects against the types that cause the great majority of cases and works best before exposure to the virus. It does not protect against every high-risk type, so everyone with a cervix still needs cervical screening even if they have been vaccinated. Cervical screening is offered in England to everyone with a cervix aged 25 to 64, including trans men and non-binary people (who may need to ask their GP practice if they are registered as male), at intervals that depend on age and the HPV result; it finds and treats precancer before it becomes cancer. Not smoking, and using condoms, which reduce but do not remove HPV transmission, also help. The WHO strategy for eliminating cervical cancer combines high coverage of vaccination, screening and treatment of precancer and cancer.

    When to seek care

    See a GP about bleeding between periods, after sex or after the menopause, a persistent unusual discharge, or pelvic pain. These are usually not cancer, but they need an examination and cannot be assessed by a screening test. Any bleeding after the menopause needs prompt assessment. NICE advises an urgent suspected cancer referral for post-menopausal bleeding in anyone aged 55 or over, mainly to rule out cancer of the womb lining, and bleeding below that age is still investigated. NICE also advises considering an urgent suspected cancer referral when the appearance of the cervix on examination is consistent with cervical cancer. Attend screening when invited, and ask to be included if you have a cervix but have not been invited.
