    Bone fracture

    Also known as: broken bone, fractured bone

    A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for deformity, a bone through the skin, numbness or a cold pale limb, or a suspected hip, spine or pelvic fracture; same-day care for any inability to bear weight or use a limb after injury. Compartment syndrome, a rare but serious rise in pressure within the muscle compartments, most often develops in the first days after a fracture and causes sudden, intense pain and swelling; it needs emergency care. Redness, warmth, pus or fever around a wound suggest infection, particularly after an open fracture.

    Overview

    Bones break when the force exceeds their strength: a fall on the outstretched hand fractures the wrist, a twist the ankle, a car crash the femur. In weakened bone (osteoporosis, cancer) a minor force is enough. Bone heals by forming a callus that is remodelled over months; the aim of treatment is to hold the fragments in a good position while it does. Symptoms vary with the bone involved: intense pain, a limb that looks out of place, swelling and tenderness, bruising and difficulty moving the part are typical, and some people feel a snap or grinding at the moment of injury. A simple crack may cause little pain, so a break is not always obvious. Healing takes weeks for minor fractures and months for severe ones, with the surrounding soft tissues often taking longer to recover than the bone itself; smoking and diabetes slow the process.

    Causes

    • Trauma: falls, sport, road accidents, assaults
    • Repeated stress (stress fractures in runners and soldiers)
    • Weakened bone: osteoporosis, tumours, infection

    Risk factors

    • Osteoporosis and older age
    • Contact and high-speed sports
    • Poor balance, medicines that cause falls
    • Smoking, low vitamin D

    What a clinician may find

    • Pain, swelling, bruising, deformity
    • Inability to bear weight or use the limb
    • Crepitus
    • Open wound over the bone in open fractures
    • A snap or grinding sensation reported at the time of injury
    • Numbness or tingling below the injury, a warning sign of nerve or vessel damage

    Complications

    • Damage to nerves and vessels
    • Compartment syndrome
    • Infection in open fractures
    • Non-union or malunion
    • Fat embolism, DVT
    • Post-traumatic osteoarthritis
    • Delayed healing, prolonged by smoking and diabetes

    Diagnosis

    X-rays in two views; CT for complex joint, spine and pelvic fractures; MRI for occult fractures such as the scaphoid or stress fractures; assessment of circulation and nerves beyond the break. X-rays are the usual way to confirm a fracture and judge its position and severity, though it can be hard to tell clinically whether a bone is broken, so imaging is needed for most suspected breaks. CT shows the detail of complex joint, spine and pelvic fractures, and MRI shows soft tissues, bone bruising and stress fractures that X-rays miss.

    Treatment

    Realign (reduce) if displaced, then hold: a cast or splint for stable fractures, or surgery with plates, screws, nails or external fixation for unstable ones. Pain relief, elevation, early movement of neighbouring joints, and physiotherapy. Open fractures need urgent cleaning and antibiotics. The common approach is immobilisation: a cast or splint keeps the bone from moving while it heals. Surgery is used when the bone needs realigning or holding, with metal rods, screws or plates securing the fragments. Open fractures, where the bone has broken the skin, need urgent surgery to remove debris, clean the wound and cut away unhealthy tissue, with antibiotics against infection. Pain relief and anti-inflammatory medicines are used, and physiotherapy afterwards restores movement, strength and function. Until help arrives, first aid is to keep the person still and calm, not to move the injured part or try to line up the bone, and to use ice and elevation for swelling.

    Prevention

    Fall prevention and bone health in older adults, protective equipment in sport, road safety, treating osteoporosis. A balanced diet, hydration, avoiding smoking and heavy alcohol use, and following advice on when to bear weight support healing after a fracture.

    Conditions

    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Anterior cruciate ligament injury A sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, the band inside the knee that stops the tibia sliding forward on the femur and controls rotation.
    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • 25-hydroxyvitamin D The storage form of vitamin D that the liver makes, the best single measure of vitamin D status and the value the vitamin D test reports.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.

    Drug classes

    • Bisphosphonates Medicines that slow bone loss by disabling the cells that dissolve bone.
    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.

    Guides and news

    • Bone health: what helps keep bones strong NHS pages link bone health to weight-bearing exercise, calcium, vitamin D, not smoking and keeping alcohol low.
    • DXA bone density scan: what it shows A DXA scan measures bone density, usually at the hip and spine, using low-dose X-rays.
    • NICE publishes osteoporosis risk guideline NICE published guideline NG259 on 29 July 2026, covering how adults at risk of fragility fractures should be assessed.
    • Sprains and strains: treatment and recovery The NHS describes PRICE care for the first 2 to 3 days, and most sprains and strains feel better after about 2 weeks.
