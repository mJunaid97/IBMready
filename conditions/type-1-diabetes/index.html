    Type 1 diabetes

    Also known as: insulin-dependent diabetes, juvenile diabetes, T1D

    An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed, so the body cannot regulate blood glucose without injected insulin.

    When to seek care

    See a GP urgently, the same day for a child or young person, for increased thirst, passing urine more often, tiredness and weight loss: these can be the first signs of type 1 diabetes. Emergency care for vomiting, abdominal pain, deep breathing, drowsiness or confusion in someone with diabetes, or for severe hypoglycaemia (confusion, seizure, unconsciousness). NHS advice is to call 999 or go to A&E if symptoms worsen quickly, or if the person becomes sleepy or confused or starts breathing faster and deeper than usual.

    Overview

    Type 1 diabetes usually appears in childhood or young adulthood over days to weeks: thirst, frequent urination, weight loss and tiredness as glucose spills into the urine. Without insulin the body burns fat uncontrollably and produces acid (ketoacidosis), which is fatal untreated. Lifelong insulin replacement, increasingly with pumps and continuous glucose monitors, allows a full life. The NHS lists passing urine more than usual, especially at night, intense thirst, marked tiredness and unintended weight loss as the classic early signs, with blurred vision, fruity-smelling breath, slow-healing cuts and recurrent infections also possible; symptoms develop over a few days or weeks. Thirst and dehydration arise because the kidneys draw water from the body to clear excess glucose into the urine. Diabetic ketoacidosis is sometimes the first sign in someone not yet diagnosed: without insulin, ketones accumulate and make the blood acidic, and infection, injury, serious illness, missed insulin doses or the stress of surgery can tip someone with known diabetes into it.

    Causes

    • Autoimmune destruction of beta cells in genetically susceptible people, possibly triggered by viral infection

    Risk factors

    • Family history and HLA genes
    • Other autoimmune diseases (thyroid, coeliac)
    • Northern European ancestry

    Symptoms

    FatigueVomitingAbdominal painWeaknessNumbness

    What a clinician may find

    • Weight loss, dehydration
    • Ketone smell, deep sighing breathing and drowsiness in ketoacidosis
    • Blurred vision, slow-healing cuts and recurrent infections
    • Fruity-smelling breath

    Complications

    • Diabetic ketoacidosis
    • Hypoglycaemia from insulin
    • Retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy
    • Cardiovascular disease
    • Foot ulcers
    • Ketoacidosis triggered by infection, injury, serious illness, missed insulin or surgery

    Diagnosis

    Raised blood glucose with symptoms; ketones in blood or urine; antibodies (GAD, IA-2) and low C-peptide confirm type 1. HbA1c tracks control over months. Where a raised glucose is found in someone without symptoms, testing is repeated on a later day to confirm it. Autoantibody testing helps distinguish type 1 from other forms of diabetes, which is harder in children who are also obese.

    Treatment

    Insulin from diagnosis, as multiple daily injections (long-acting plus rapid-acting with meals) or a pump, matched to carbohydrate intake; glucose monitoring; structured education; annual screening of eyes, kidneys and feet; blood pressure and cholesterol control. Ketoacidosis is treated in hospital with insulin and fluids, and the underlying trigger, such as an infection, is treated at the same time. Early hypoglycaemia causes anxiety, sweating, hunger, tremor, palpitations and pallor; untreated it can progress to confusion, behavioural change, loss of consciousness, seizures and coma, which is why people on insulin and those around them are taught to recognise and treat it promptly.

    Prevention

    None yet, although immunotherapy can delay onset in high-risk relatives; good control lowers the risk of complications.

    What causes type 1 diabetes?

    Type 1 diabetes is a lifelong condition in which the pancreas produces little or no insulin. It is an autoimmune condition: the immune system damages the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas.

    Type 1 diabetes can run in families, but Diabetes UK says most people with type 1 have no family history of it. Diabetes UK says the exact cause of type 1 diabetes is not known, and that things in the environment, such as viruses, may play a part in triggering it.

    In the UK, Diabetes UK reports that about 90% of people diagnosed with diabetes have type 2 and about 8% have type 1. The NHS lists the classic early signs as needing the toilet more, being thirsty, feeling tired and losing weight, often remembered as the four Ts. NIDDK notes that some people first find out they have type 1 diabetes when they develop signs of diabetic ketoacidosis.

    How is type 1 diabetes diagnosed?

    A GP will usually test a urine sample and may check blood glucose, then send the person to hospital for assessment. The NHS says people are usually kept in hospital until their blood test results are back.

    NIDDK says symptoms of type 1 diabetes usually develop quickly, over a few days or weeks. The other signs it lists are blurred vision, sweet or fruity-smelling breath, slow-healing cuts and frequent infections.

    Because diabetic ketoacidosis can be the first sign, the NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if symptoms worsen quickly, or if stomach pain, sleepiness or confusion, or fast, deep breathing develop. The NHS advises seeing a GP about these symptoms, and calling 999 or going to A&E if they get worse quickly.

    How is type 1 diabetes treated day to day?

    The NHS says people with type 1 diabetes take insulin, either with pens or with a pump. Most people take both a long-acting insulin and a rapid-acting insulin before meals. This pattern is known as basal-bolus treatment.

    Insulin can be given with pens or by a pump, which gives small amounts of rapid-acting insulin throughout the day. Pump therapy can be used by adults, teenagers and children with adult supervision.

    The NHS says the dose of rapid-acting insulin at a meal is worked out from the amount of carbohydrate to be eaten, which is called carbohydrate counting.

    One NHS 111 Wales page stresses that treatment should not be stopped during illness, and that the treatment plan may say whether a temporary increase is needed.

    What is a hypo?

    A hypo is a lower-than-normal level of glucose in the blood. The NHS lists feeling hungry, dizzy, sweaty and shaky among the signs of a hypo, and says that if it is not treated it can lead to drowsiness or loss of consciousness.

    Common triggers include too much insulin, a skipped meal, vigorous exercise and drinking alcohol on an empty stomach.

    Mild hypos are usually treated with something sugary. In a severe hypo a person may become drowsy, confused or lose consciousness and may need a glucagon injection into a muscle or glucose given into a vein; the NHS says to call 999 and ask for an ambulance if they do not come round within 10 minutes of a glucagon injection.

    NICE says the annual review should check that the person can recognise the symptoms of a hypo.

    What is diabetic ketoacidosis?

    Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a medical emergency that can follow untreated type 1 diabetes and is treated in hospital with insulin and fluids. Diabetes UK lists being very thirsty, needing to wee more often, feeling tired and sleepy, confusion, blurred vision, stomach pain, feeling or being sick, and sweet or fruity-smelling breath; the NHS also lists breathing more deeply than usual. Diabetes UK and the NHS say DKA symptoms usually develop over about 24 hours but can come on faster.

    The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if the symptoms are getting worse quickly, or if there is stomach pain, sleepiness or confusion, or fast, deep breathing. The NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if ketone levels are high, or if there are symptoms of DKA and the ketone level is not known.

    NHS inform says untreated DKA can lead to unconsciousness, so the NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if the warning signs appear.

    What are the long-term complications and checks?

    Over time, high glucose can damage blood vessels and nerves. The NHS describes complications affecting the eyes, feet and kidneys. In the eyes, damage to blood vessels can cause sight problems (diabetic retinopathy) and, uncommonly, blindness, so adults and children aged 12 or over are offered eye screening every year. In the feet, nerve damage means small cuts may go unnoticed and can become ulcers, so the NHS advises checking the feet every day and having a foot examination at least once a year. In the kidneys, damage usually causes no symptoms at first, so a test is advised every year.

    NICE says the annual review for adults includes foot checks and checks for complications such as kidney and nerve problems, and advice about the higher risk of gum disease. NICE says children with type 1 diabetes should have their blood pressure checked every year after they turn 12.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is type 1 diabetes the same as type 2?

    No. Type 1 is an autoimmune condition in which the body makes little or no insulin. Type 2 is a different condition; in the UK about 90% of people diagnosed with diabetes have type 2, according to Diabetes UK.

    What are the four Ts of type 1 diabetes?

    The NHS lists needing the toilet more, thirst, tiredness and weight loss as the core signs, often remembered as the four Ts. Blurred vision, fruity-smelling breath, slow-healing cuts and frequent infections can also occur.

    Why do hypos happen?

    Common triggers include taking too much insulin, skipping a meal, vigorous exercise and drinking alcohol on an empty stomach.

    Should insulin carry on during illness?

    One NHS 111 Wales page says it is very important not to stop treatment when ill. The treatment plan may say whether a temporary increase is needed, and the diabetes care team is the place to ask.

    When is diabetic ketoacidosis an emergency?

    The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if symptoms are getting worse quickly, or if there is stomach pain, sleepiness or confusion, or fast, deep breathing. DKA is a medical emergency that is treated in hospital.

    Conditions

    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Coeliac disease Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system reacts to gluten and damages the lining of the small bowel.
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Thrush (candidiasis) A common yeast infection caused by Candida, a fungus that normally lives harmlessly on the body, which can affect the mouth.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.

    Procedures

    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.

    Medications

    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
