    Peptic ulcer

    Also known as: stomach ulcer, gastric ulcer, duodenal ulcer

    A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for vomiting blood or coffee-ground material, black tarry stools, sudden severe abdominal pain, or collapse. MedlinePlus lists sudden sharp abdominal pain, a hard, rigid, tender abdomen, fainting, heavy sweating or confusion, vomiting blood, and maroon or black tarry stools as reasons for urgent care.

    Overview

    Most peptic ulcers are caused by the bacterium Helicobacter pylori or by NSAID painkillers, both of which strip the mucus barrier that protects the lining from acid. Duodenal ulcers are commoner and classically hurt when hungry; gastric ulcers hurt after eating. Ulcers can bleed or perforate. Only a minority of people infected with H. pylori go on to develop ulcer disease. Symptoms include pain or discomfort in the upper abdomen, feeling full too soon during or too full after a meal, nausea, bloating and belching, though some people have no pain and small ulcers may cause no symptoms at all. Bleeding can be slow and subtle, so the only clue may be tiredness and weakness from anaemia rather than visible blood. As awareness and treatment of H. pylori have grown, bleeding ulcers are increasingly linked to NSAID use, older age and other illnesses.

    Causes

    • Helicobacter pylori infection
    • NSAIDs and aspirin
    • Rarely: Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, severe physiological stress, Crohn's disease

    Risk factors

    • Smoking
    • Alcohol
    • Older age
    • Steroids and anticoagulants taken with NSAIDs
    • Other illnesses (comorbidity) in older people, now a leading context for bleeding ulcers

    Symptoms

    Abdominal painNauseaVomitingFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Epigastric tenderness
    • Signs of anaemia if bleeding chronically
    • Rigid abdomen if perforated
    • Pallor and tiredness from slow blood loss
    • Early fullness, bloating and belching reported in the history

    Complications

    • Bleeding: vomiting blood or passing black stools
    • Perforation and peritonitis
    • Gastric outlet obstruction
    • Gastric cancer (with H. pylori)

    Diagnosis

    Testing for H. pylori (breath, stool or blood test); endoscopy to see the ulcer, take biopsies and treat bleeding, especially in those over 55 or with alarm features. Testing for H. pylori is advised for anyone with a current or past ulcer. At endoscopy a rapid urease test on a biopsy, with or without histology, is usually the first test; if it is negative, a urea breath test or serology follows. An upper GI contrast series is an alternative where endoscopy is not performed.

    Treatment

    Eradicate H. pylori with a proton pump inhibitor plus two antibiotics for one to two weeks; stop NSAIDs; proton pump inhibitor for four to eight weeks to heal. Bleeding ulcers are treated endoscopically; perforation needs surgery. Eradicating H. pylori heals the ulcer and reduces the chance of rebleeding. For NSAID-related ulcers the NSAID is stopped and acid suppression given; if the NSAID cannot be stopped, a proton pump inhibitor is continued alongside it, and misoprostol or a lining-protecting medicine such as sucralfate may be added. Prescribed aspirin or NSAIDs are not stopped without medical advice, since they may need adjusting rather than withdrawal.

    Prevention

    Avoid NSAIDs when possible or take a protective proton pump inhibitor with them, stop smoking, limit alcohol.

    Conditions

    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Biomarkers

    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.
    • Naproxen Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.
    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).

    Guides and news

    • Gastroscopy: what happens and what to expect A gastroscopy uses a thin camera to look at the gullet and stomach.
    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
    • Stomach ache: causes by area and warning signs Where a stomach ache sits helps point to causes such as appendicitis, gallstones or IBS, and sudden severe pain needs 999 or A&E.
