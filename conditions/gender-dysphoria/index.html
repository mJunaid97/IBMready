    Gender dysphoria (gender incongruence)

    Also known as: gender incongruence, gender identity incongruence, gender-related distress

    The distress that can arise when a person's gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth. The mismatch itself is called gender incongruence and is not an illness.

    When to seek care

    Ask a GP for referral to a gender service if gender-related distress is persistent or affecting daily life. Seek urgent help from emergency services or a crisis line for thoughts of suicide or self-harm. Tell a doctor about any hormones taken without a prescription so that monitoring can be arranged. Anyone on hormone therapy with a swollen painful leg, chest pain or sudden breathlessness needs urgent assessment for a clot.

    Overview

    Gender identity is a person's inner sense of being a man, a woman, both, neither or another gender; when it does not match the sex recorded at birth, the unease can be intense and lasting, though many trans and non-binary people have little or no dysphoria. The WHO's ICD-11 lists gender incongruence under conditions related to sexual health and not among mental or behavioural disorders. Support, social transition, hormones and surgery can relieve dysphoria for those who want them; lack of support, care and acceptance is associated with worse mental health.

    Causes

    • The origin of gender identity is not fully understood; biological and developmental influences are studied but no single cause is established
    • There is no evidence that it is caused by parenting or family circumstances, and gender identity is not a choice
    • Distress arises from the mismatch between identity and the body's sex characteristics, and from how others respond: misgendering, rejection and discrimination
    • Rarely, gender incongruence occurs alongside a difference of sex development, but most people with gender dysphoria have typical chromosomes and hormones

    Risk factors

    • Puberty, when secondary sexual characteristics that do not match identity develop
    • Rejection by family or peers, bullying, harassment and discrimination
    • Long waits for specialist assessment and care
    • Co-existing depression or anxiety, which deepen the distress
    • Isolation from other trans people and from reliable information and support

    Symptoms

    Fatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Persistent discomfort with the body's primary or secondary sex characteristics, or with being treated as the assigned gender
    • A strong and lasting wish to live as, and be recognised in, the experienced gender, often expressed through name, pronouns, clothing and social role
    • Low mood, anxiety, poor sleep, fatigue, social withdrawal or self-neglect accompanying the distress
    • Steps taken to change appearance, such as binding, tucking, padding or choice of clothing and hair

    Complications

    • Depression, anxiety, self-harm and raised suicide risk, especially with rejection, discrimination or long delays in care
    • Social isolation and leaving education or work
    • Harm from unregulated hormones bought online and used without monitoring
    • Reduced fertility on hormone therapy, which may not be fully reversible, and permanent loss of fertility after removal of the gonads, if eggs or sperm were not stored beforehand
    • Treatment risks that need monitoring: venous thromboembolism on oestrogen, a raised red cell count on testosterone, bone loss after gonadectomy if hormones are interrupted

    Diagnosis

    There is no test for gender identity. Assessment by a specialist gender service, usually after GP referral, covers how gender identity has developed, the distress it causes, physical and mental health, and what the person hopes for. ICD-11 describes gender incongruence as a marked and persistent incongruence between experienced gender and assigned sex; the DSM-5-TR diagnosis of gender dysphoria asks for incongruence lasting at least six months with clinically significant distress or impairment, whereas ICD-11 gender incongruence does not require distress and is classified outside the mental disorders. The blood tests linked from this page are used to monitor hormone therapy and general health, not to diagnose gender incongruence.

    Treatment

    Care aims to help people live comfortably in their gender; the mix is individual, and some want none of it. It can include psychological support, social transition (name, pronouns, clothing, documents), voice and communication therapy, hair removal, gender-affirming hormone therapy (oestradiol with a testosterone-suppressing medicine, usually stopped once the testes are removed, for trans women and some non-binary people; testosterone for trans men and some non-binary people) prescribed and monitored by a specialist team, and genital, chest, breast and facial surgery. Hormones and gonadal surgery reduce fertility, so storing sperm or eggs is discussed first. Care for under-18s is provided by specialist services, and policies differ between countries and have changed: in England, puberty blockers for gender incongruence are available only through specialist research pathways, and prescribing outside those pathways is restricted.

    Prevention

    Gender incongruence is not something to prevent or cure. What can be prevented is the harm that comes with it: acceptance and support from family, school and workplace, prompt access to assessment and care, respectful use of the person's name and pronouns, mental health support when needed, and protection from bullying and discrimination all reduce distress and the risk of self-harm.

    Conditions

    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.

    Biomarkers

    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.

    Procedures

    • Vaginoplasty Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.
    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.
