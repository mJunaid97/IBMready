    Lung cancer

    Also known as: bronchial carcinoma

    A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.

    When to seek care

    NHS advice is to see a GP for a cough lasting longer than three weeks, unexplained breathlessness, weight loss or a new hoarse voice, especially with a history of smoking; coughing up blood warrants an urgent GP appointment or a call to NHS 111; and struggling to breathe or pain in the chest or upper back warrants 999 or A&E. Anyone with symptoms is advised not to wait for a screening appointment.

    Overview

    Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death because it is usually found late: symptoms appear only when a tumour blocks an airway, invades the chest wall or spreads. Smoking causes about eight in ten cases. Low-dose CT screening of eligible heavy smokers can find lung cancer at an earlier stage, when treatment is more likely to be curative. Early lung cancer often causes no symptoms at all. The NHS lists a persistent cough, breathlessness, coughing up blood and chest or shoulder pain as the main features; less common ones are swelling of the face or neck, difficulty swallowing from pressure on the oesophagus, a hoarse voice that does not go away from pressure on the laryngeal nerves, and clubbing of the fingers. Bone pain and facial or eyelid changes tend to appear late. The cause is not always known: lifestyle, genetic changes and environmental factors all contribute, with smoking the largest single risk. A targeted screening programme using low-dose CT is being rolled out in England for eligible older adults, aimed at finding cancer before symptoms appear.

    Causes

    • Tobacco smoke
    • Radon gas
    • Asbestos, diesel exhaust and other occupational carcinogens
    • Air pollution
    • Family history
    • Arsenic and other industrial chemicals

    Risk factors

    • Smoking, including second-hand smoke
    • COPD and pulmonary fibrosis
    • Previous radiotherapy to the chest
    • Age over 60
    • HIV infection
    • A previous cancer elsewhere

    Symptoms

    CoughShortness of breathChest painFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Finger clubbing
    • Hoarse voice
    • Enlarged lymph nodes in the neck
    • Signs of a pleural effusion or collapsed lobe
    • Weight loss
    • Swelling of the face or neck
    • Wheeze, or difficulty swallowing from a tumour pressing on the oesophagus
    • Bone pain and facial or eyelid changes in late disease

    Complications

    • Spread to lymph nodes, liver, bones, brain and adrenals
    • Airway obstruction and pneumonia
    • Superior vena cava obstruction
    • Paraneoplastic syndromes
    • Compression of the oesophagus (swallowing difficulty) and of the laryngeal nerves (hoarseness)

    Diagnosis

    Chest X-ray then CT of the chest and abdomen; tissue by bronchoscopy or CT-guided biopsy; PET-CT for staging; molecular tests on the tumour to guide targeted therapy. A GP asks about symptoms and general health and may check lung function with a spirometer. A chest X-ray is usually the first test but cannot give a definitive answer on its own, so a specialist arranges a CT scan and a bronchoscopy, in which a thin tube with a camera is passed through the mouth or nose into the airways to take a biopsy that shows the cancer type; a PET-CT scan shows where active cancer cells are. Pathology is reviewed by a specialist lung cancer pathologist before treatment because small-cell cancer can be mistaken for other types. Staging uses the TNM system (tumour size, lymph node spread, distant metastasis) and tells the team whether a complete cure is possible; small-cell cancer is described as limited stage when confined to one half of the chest within a single radiotherapy field, otherwise extensive stage.

    Treatment

    Depends on type and stage: surgery or radical radiotherapy for early non-small-cell cancer; chemotherapy, immunotherapy and targeted drugs (for EGFR, ALK and other mutations) for advanced disease; chemotherapy and radiotherapy for small-cell cancer; palliative care for symptoms throughout. The choice depends on the type, size and position of the cancer, whether it has spread, specific genetic changes and the person's general health. Surgery is the main curative treatment for non-small-cell cancer and may be combined with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted medicines or immunotherapy. Small-cell cancer responds well to chemotherapy and is generally not treated surgically. Radiotherapy can cause breathlessness and a hoarse cough during treatment, which usually ease in the weeks afterwards.

    Prevention

    Not smoking is the single most effective measure; radon testing of homes; workplace protection; CT screening for eligible heavy smokers.

    What are the main types of lung cancer?

    Lung cancer is mostly divided into two groups, called non-small cell and small cell lung cancer. MedlinePlus says non-small cell lung cancer accounts for 85 percent of lung cancer, and small cell lung cancer accounts for the remaining 15 percent. Non-small cell lung cancer includes adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma is the most common of these.

    Knowing the type matters. The NHS says treatment depends on the size of the cancer and where it is, the type of lung cancer, whether it has spread, whether it has certain genetic changes and the person's general health. It also says surgery is not usually used to treat small cell lung cancer, because the cancer has often already spread to other areas of the body by the time it is diagnosed.

    How is lung cancer staged?

    Cancer Research UK says the TNM system is the most common way to stage lung cancer, and that TNM stands for Tumour, Node and Metastasis. The number staging system uses TNM to group lung cancer into different stages, which run from 1 to 4.

    Small cell lung cancer may also be described as limited or extensive stage. Cancer Research UK says this is a simpler and older system, and that doctors are using it less often as they learn more about small cell lung cancer. Macmillan describes limited stage as cancer cells that can be seen in one lung and in nearby lymph nodes, and extensive stage as cancer that has spread outside the lung, to the chest area or to other parts of the body. The National Cancer Institute defines extensive stage as TNM stage IV with distant metastases.

    Roy Castle says that stage 4 lung cancer is not curable, but it is treatable, and many people can live well with advanced cancer.

    What happens during tests for lung cancer?

    A chest X-ray is usually the first test for lung cancer. It often cannot give a definitive diagnosis on its own. A CT scan is usually the next test.

    If a CT scan suggests a tumour in the central part of the lung, a bronchoscopy lets the doctor look inside the airways and remove a small sample of cells. Another option is a CT-guided needle biopsy, in which a thin hollow needle is passed through the chest wall into the lung. Macmillan says a doctor usually numbs the skin first with an injection of local anaesthetic. A chest X-ray afterwards is the standard way to check for a collapsed lung (pneumothorax), which is a known risk of the biopsy.

    A PET-CT scan is typically used when a CT scan has shown cancer. A biopsy is usually how doctors confirm lung cancer.

    What treatments are used for lung cancer?

    The NHS says that you may be offered a combination of treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and targeted medicines, and that immunotherapy may also be used.

    For non-small cell lung cancer, the NHS says surgery is generally the first option when the cancer is in one lung and the person is in good health, and it may be followed by a course of chemotherapy. If the cancer has spread too far for surgery or radiotherapy to be effective, chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy is usually recommended. Cancer Research UK says radiotherapy can be used on its own, instead of surgery, for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer, and that it can also be combined with chemotherapy.

    Small cell lung cancer is usually treated with chemotherapy, either on its own or in combination with radiotherapy. Targeted medicines are used when a specific molecular target is found in the tumour, so cancer cells are tested to see whether a targeted drug may suit. NHS 111 Wales says targeted therapies cannot cure lung cancer, but they can slow its spread.

    What is living with lung cancer like?

    Cancer Research UK says that a cough or breathlessness can be early symptoms after radiotherapy to the chest, and that these settle within a few weeks of finishing treatment. Scarring or inflammation in the treated lung can cause breathlessness or a cough later on, and this might start months after treatment, so Cancer Research UK advises telling a doctor about any changes in breathing or increased mucus.

    A lung cancer clinical nurse specialist can give information and emotional support and can refer you on to other services. Your GP is responsible for your care at home, community or district nurses can make regular home visits, and palliative care nurses can also phone or visit you at home to check that symptoms are being managed.

    After treatment, Cancer Research UK says you are likely to have an appointment with your doctor within 4 to 6 weeks after the treatment has finished, and that visits then become less frequent over time..

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between non-small cell and small cell lung cancer?

    MedlinePlus says non-small cell lung cancer accounts for 85 percent of lung cancer and small cell lung cancer for the remaining 15 percent. Non-small cell lung cancer includes adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and large cell carcinoma. The NHS says small cell lung cancer is usually treated with chemotherapy, either on its own or in combination with radiotherapy, and that surgery is not usually used for it.

    Is advanced lung cancer treatable?

    Roy Castle says that stage 4 lung cancer is not curable, but it is treatable, and many people can live well with advanced cancer. The NHS says that if non-small cell lung cancer has spread too far for surgery or radiotherapy to be effective, chemotherapy and/or immunotherapy is usually recommended.

    Why are tumours tested for genetic changes?

    Targeted medicines are used when a specific molecular target is found in the tumour, so cancer cells are tested to see whether a targeted drug may suit. NHS 111 Wales says targeted therapies cannot cure lung cancer, but they can slow its spread.

    What happens after lung cancer treatment ends?

    Cancer Research UK says that after surgery, radiotherapy or chemotherapy you are likely to have an appointment with your doctor within 4 to 6 weeks after the treatment has finished and that visits then become less frequent over time. Tests at some visits might include blood tests, X-rays, a CT scan or a PET-CT scan.

    What happens in a lung needle biopsy?

    A CT-guided needle biopsy passes a thin hollow needle through the chest wall into the lung, with a CT scan guiding where the needle goes. A chest X-ray afterwards is the standard way to check for a collapsed lung (pneumothorax), which is a known risk of the procedure.

    Conditions

    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    Symptoms

    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.
    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.

    Biomarkers

    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.

    Drug classes

    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
