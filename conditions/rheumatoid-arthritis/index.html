    Rheumatoid arthritis

    Also known as: RA

    A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints, causing symmetrical inflammation that can destroy cartilage and bone.

    When to seek care

    See a doctor within weeks for swelling of several joints with morning stiffness; urgent care for a single hot joint with fever or new neck pain with tingling in the arms.

    Overview

    Rheumatoid arthritis typically starts in the small joints of the hands and feet with prolonged morning stiffness and swelling, and can involve the eyes, lungs, heart and blood vessels. Untreated it deforms and disables; started early, disease-modifying drugs now prevent most damage. Arthritis is the cardinal feature, but extra-articular disease is common: interstitial lung disease is among the most frequent manifestations and is more likely in smokers, in men and in people positive for anti-CCP antibodies and rheumatoid factor. Treatment with disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) is recommended early for every patient because it slows joint destruction and prevents deformity, although these medicines carry considerable side effects and cost.

    Causes

    • Autoimmunity against citrullinated proteins, in genetically susceptible people, often triggered by smoking or infection

    Risk factors

    • Female sex
    • Age 30 to 60
    • Smoking
    • Family history
    • Periodontal disease
    • Obesity
    • Smoking, male sex and antibody positivity raise the risk of lung involvement

    Symptoms

    Joint painSwelling (oedema)FatigueFeverKnee pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Symmetrical swelling of metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints
    • Morning stiffness over an hour
    • Rheumatoid nodules
    • Later: ulnar deviation, swan-neck deformities

    Complications

    • Joint destruction and deformity
    • Cardiovascular disease
    • Lung fibrosis, pleural disease
    • Anaemia, osteoporosis
    • Cervical spine instability (atlanto-axial subluxation)
    • Interstitial lung disease, the most common form of lung involvement
    • Drug toxicity: marrow suppression, infection, liver damage and pneumonitis with methotrexate

    Diagnosis

    Clinical pattern plus rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies, raised CRP and ESR, and ultrasound or X-ray showing synovitis and erosions. Early referral to rheumatology within weeks changes outcomes. Lung involvement is diagnosed from clinical and radiological findings, and pulmonary function tests may show a restrictive pattern.

    Treatment

    Methotrexate started early, with short-term steroids to control symptoms; biologic drugs (anti-TNF, anti-IL-6, B-cell depletion) or JAK inhibitors if disease stays active; NSAIDs for pain; physiotherapy, occupational therapy and cardiovascular risk management. Methotrexate is the most commonly used DMARD, with leflunomide and hydroxychloroquine as alternatives; it reduces pain and X-ray joint damage, but a substantial minority of patients do not respond well despite optimal dosing. DMARDs fall into three groups: conventional synthetic (methotrexate, hydroxychloroquine, sulfasalazine), targeted synthetic (JAK inhibitors) and biologic (TNF and IL-6 inhibitors). Methotrexate can suppress the bone marrow and cause serious infections, liver damage, mouth and skin problems and hypersensitivity pneumonitis. NSAIDs reduce swelling and pain well but do not prevent joint damage, so they are used for short periods at low doses alongside a DMARD.

    Prevention

    Not smoking and good dental health reduce risk; early treatment prevents damage.

    What does rheumatoid arthritis feel like?

    The NHS describes rheumatoid arthritis (RA) as a long-term condition that causes pain, swelling and stiffness in the joints. It commonly involves the hands, wrists and feet, and the small joints of the hands and feet are often affected first. Joint pain, swelling and stiffness are the core symptoms. Stiffness is often worse in the morning or after rest, and it usually lasts longer than the stiffness seen in osteoarthritis.

    Inflamed joints can swell and feel warm and tender, and firm lumps called rheumatoid nodules sometimes form under the skin. Symptoms most often begin slowly over weeks to a few months. Some people also have general symptoms, including tiredness, fever, sweating, poor appetite and weight loss. Inflammation can also affect the eyes, causing dryness, or the heart and lungs, causing chest pain. Symptoms vary between people and can come and go.

    What is a flare-up and what can help?

    Symptoms can come back or get worse, which is known as a flare-up. One NHS trust lists stopping or winding down treatment, doing too much, stress, infection and poor sleep as causes of flares.

    Guidance broadly describes heat as easing stiffness and cold as easing swelling, and sources stress placing a towel between the skin and the heat or cold source. Sources recommend gentle movement rather than rest alone. NRAS, a rheumatoid arthritis charity, says exercise is important because it helps reduce the risk of further joint damage. NRAS advises speaking to the rheumatology team about regular flares, or a flare that is not going down with self-management. A Worcestershire hospital trust adds that recurrent flares can show that a change to treatment is needed.

    How is rheumatoid arthritis diagnosed?

    Rheumatoid arthritis can be difficult to diagnose because many conditions cause joint stiffness and inflammation, and there is no definitive test for it. The NHS advises seeing a GP if these symptoms appear. The GP carries out a physical examination, checking the joints for swelling and how easily they move. If the GP thinks the person has rheumatoid arthritis, they refer them to a specialist called a rheumatologist.

    Blood tests include ESR and CRP for inflammation, a full blood count, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP. People who test positive for anti-CCP are very likely to develop rheumatoid arthritis, but not everybody with the condition has this antibody. A Manchester guide for GPs notes that a negative result does not exclude the condition and a positive result does not equate to a diagnosis. X-rays and MRI scans may be used to check for joint inflammation and damage, and ultrasound is used in some early arthritis clinics. Seropositive means these antibodies are found in the blood, and seronegative means they are not.

    What medicines are used to treat it?

    The NHS says treatments can help reduce inflammation in the joints, relieve pain, prevent or slow down joint damage, reduce disability and enable people to be as active as possible. Care usually involves the GP and several specialists. The disease-modifying medicines fall into two main groups, disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) and biological treatments.

    Initial treatment is usually a combination of DMARD tablets, and methotrexate is usually the first medicine given. Biological treatments are generally used alongside methotrexate or another DMARD, typically when DMARDs alone have not worked. JAK inhibitors are offered to adults with moderate to severe disease who cannot take DMARDs or biological treatments, or who found them ineffective. Steroids can be given as tablets or injections and are usually used for short periods. The NHS says long-term steroid use can have serious side effects.

    Monitoring and supportive care

    The NHS says methotrexate can affect blood cells and the liver, so regular blood tests are used to monitor this. MedlinePlus says DMARDs may have serious side effects, so frequent blood tests and other monitoring are needed. MedlinePlus also says corticosteroids work very well to reduce joint swelling and inflammation, but they can have long-term side effects and do not prevent joint damage if used alone.

    NHS inform lists physiotherapy, which helps improve muscle strength, flexibility and fitness. If the condition causes problems with everyday tasks, occupational therapy may help, and an occupational therapist can provide training and advice to protect the joints at home and at work. A support for the joints, such as a splint, may also be recommended.

    Surgery and outlook

    The NHS says surgery may be needed to improve joint movement, reduce pain or correct joint damage, and some people need a hip, knee or shoulder joint replaced. NHS inform lists hand and wrist surgery and arthroscopy among the options. The NHS notes that some function may not be restored after a damaged joint is replaced by a prosthetic one.

    NHS inform says there is no cure for rheumatoid arthritis, but with treatment symptoms such as joint pain, swelling and stiffness improve. Other NHS sites agree that early treatment reduces the risk of joint damage. One review says rheumatoid nodules in the lung are usually symptom-free and often resolve on their own. One source says clinical remission is reached and sustained in a minority of people, and that drug-free remission remains rare.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can rheumatoid arthritis be cured?

    NHS inform says there is no cure for rheumatoid arthritis, but with treatment symptoms such as joint pain, swelling and stiffness improve. The NHS says treatments can help reduce inflammation, relieve pain, prevent or slow down joint damage and reduce disability. One source says clinical remission is reached and sustained in a minority of people, and drug-free remission remains rare.

    What does seropositive rheumatoid arthritis mean?

    Seropositive means rheumatoid factor or anti-CCP antibodies are found in the blood, and seronegative means they are not. People who test positive for anti-CCP are very likely to develop rheumatoid arthritis, but not everybody with the condition has this antibody.

    Why are regular blood tests needed during treatment?

    The NHS says methotrexate can affect blood cells and the liver, so regular blood tests monitor this. MedlinePlus says DMARDs may have serious side effects, so frequent blood tests and other monitoring are needed. One NHS trust leaflet says hydroxychloroquine does not need blood monitoring checks, although people taking it still have regular checks for other reasons.

    Does rheumatoid arthritis only affect the joints?

    Joints are the main target, but the NHS lists general symptoms that can occur, including tiredness, fever, sweating, poor appetite and weight loss. It says inflammation can also affect the eyes, causing dryness, or the heart and lungs, causing chest pain.

    Does everyone with rheumatoid arthritis need surgery?

    The NHS says surgery may be needed to improve joint movement, reduce pain or correct joint damage, and some people need a hip, knee or shoulder joint replaced. NHS inform describes surgery as sometimes needed, alongside medicines, physiotherapy and occupational therapy, so it is not described as needed by everyone.

    What can trigger a flare?

    One NHS trust lists stopping or winding down treatment, doing too much, stress, infection and poor sleep. Another source notes that a flare is often the result of several factors building up together. NRAS advises speaking to the rheumatology team about regular flares, or a flare that is not going down with self-management.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Fibromyalgia A long-term condition that causes pain all over the body.
    • Lupus (systemic lupus erythematosus) A long-term autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks healthy tissue, most often causing joint aches, skin rashes and tiredness.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Hydroxychloroquine Hydroxychloroquine is a medicine used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and some skin conditions.
    • Methotrexate Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease.
    • Naproxen Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Antimalarial DMARDs MedlinePlus places hydroxychloroquine in a group of medicines called antimalarials and also describes it as an antirheumatic drug.
    • Antimetabolite DMARDs Antimetabolite DMARDs are a group of disease-modifying medicines; methotrexate is the member covered on this site.
    • Bisphosphonates Medicines that slow bone loss by disabling the cells that dissolve bone.
    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.

    Guides and news

    • High CRP: what it means and what happens next A high CRP means there is inflammation or infection somewhere in the body, but not where or why, so doctors read it with symptoms and other tests.
    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.
