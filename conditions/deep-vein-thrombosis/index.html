    Deep vein thrombosis

    Also known as: DVT, blood clot in the leg

    A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.

    When to seek care

    Same-day assessment for a swollen painful calf; emergency care if breathlessness or chest pain develops. NHS guidance is to call 999 or go to A&E if leg symptoms are accompanied by breathlessness or chest pain, since this can mean the clot has travelled to the lungs.

    Overview

    Clots form when blood flow slows, the vein wall is injured or the blood clots too easily (Virchow's triad). A leg DVT causes pain and swelling; its danger is that a fragment can break off and lodge in the lungs as a pulmonary embolism. Most clots start in the deep veins of the calf, where flow is slow; damage to the vein from surgery or injury adds to the risk, and pregnancy, ageing and prolonged immobility after an operation or on a long flight are among the commonest triggers. NHS guidance describes the typical leg: swelling, usually at the back of the leg, a throbbing pain or heavy ache in the calf or thigh, skin that is warm or red, and tender, swollen surface veins. Many DVTs cause no symptoms at all, so their absence does not exclude the diagnosis. Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism are together called venous thromboembolism (VTE), a serious but largely preventable condition.

    Causes

    • Immobility: surgery, long flights, bed rest, plaster casts
    • Cancer and chemotherapy
    • Pregnancy, the combined pill and hormone replacement
    • Inherited clotting tendencies (factor V Leiden and others)
    • Injury to the vein

    Risk factors

    • Age
    • Obesity
    • Smoking
    • Previous DVT
    • Dehydration

    Symptoms

    Swelling (oedema)Knee pain

    What a clinician may find

    • One calf swollen more than 3 cm larger than the other, warm, tender, with dilated surface veins
    • Warm, red or discoloured skin over the clot
    • Visibly enlarged surface veins
    • No abnormal findings in a proportion of cases

    Complications

    • Pulmonary embolism
    • Post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic pain and heaviness, swelling, skin discolouration, new varicose veins and in severe cases leg ulcers

    Diagnosis

    The clinician assesses the probability from the history, risk factors and examination, rules out other causes of a swollen leg such as cellulitis, a Baker's cyst, a muscle or joint injury or lymphoedema, and then tests. A D-dimer blood test is used to rule DVT out in low-probability cases; compression ultrasound of the leg veins confirms it. If a pulmonary embolism is suspected, CT angiography images the lung arteries. Because VTE can occur without obvious signs, a low threshold for testing is kept in people with risk factors.

    Treatment

    Anticoagulation for at least three months, usually with a direct oral anticoagulant (apixaban, rivaroxaban) or warfarin; compression stockings for symptoms; early mobilisation. Large clots occasionally need catheter-directed clot removal. Anticoagulants do not literally thin the blood; they alter clotting proteins so that new clots form less easily and the existing clot stops extending while the body breaks it down. Compression socks can ease pain and swelling. Clot-dissolving drugs (thrombolysis), mechanical clot removal (thrombectomy) and inferior vena cava filters are less common options used alongside anticoagulation in selected cases. Current guidance does not recommend routine compression stockings to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome, though a trial is reasonable when leg swelling is troublesome.

    Prevention

    Move early after surgery, use prescribed stockings and injections in hospital, walk and hydrate on long journeys, stop smoking. In hospital, people judged to be at higher risk are given preventive anticoagulant medicine or compression stockings.

    How is a DVT assessed in practice?

    NICE says that people with a likely DVT should have a leg vein ultrasound scan within 4 hours of it being requested, or within 24 hours if that is not possible.

    NHS inform says that if a doctor thinks you have DVT, you will need tests, including a blood test called a D-dimer and/or an ultrasound scan. The doctor first estimates the likelihood of a clot using a points score called the Wells score. In NHS guidance, people with a low Wells score but a positive D-dimer test are recommended to have an ultrasound scan. If the Wells score shows that a DVT is likely, NICE says the person should have a leg vein ultrasound scan.

    DVT can occur in an arm as well as a leg. Typical symptoms include throbbing pain, usually in the calf or thigh, and swelling in one leg, often with swollen veins, although a clot can sometimes cause no symptoms. The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if leg symptoms come with breathlessness or chest pain.

    How is a DVT treated?

    Blood-thinning medicines (anticoagulants) are the main treatment. NHS hospital leaflets describe treatment with blood-thinning tablets such as rivaroxaban or apixaban, or with warfarin or heparin injections. The NHS says people usually need to take them for at least three months to reduce the risk of another clot.

    NHS hospital leaflets say that if it is not a first DVT, treatment is normally recommended for life.

    Anticoagulants reduce the risk of the clot getting worse and give the body time to break the clot down. The body breaks the clot down over time. The medicine stops the clot growing and reduces the risk of another clot. Clot-busting drugs or surgery are reserved for severe cases.

    Living with blood thinners

    NHS hospital leaflets list minor bruising or bleeding as the most common side effect of blood-thinning injections such as dalteparin. NHS hospital leaflets list warning signs of bleeding on blood thinners, including blood when vomiting or coughing, black or bloody stools, blood in the urine, prolonged nosebleeds and unexplained or severe bruising, and advise seeking medical advice.

    NHS hospital leaflets say that combined contraceptive pills and HRT containing oestrogen can increase the risk of a DVT or pulmonary embolism.

    Some NHS hospital leaflets say aspirin has not been proven to reduce the risk of clots on long journeys.

    What are the long-term effects of a DVT?

    The most serious complication is a pulmonary embolism, in which a clot travels to the lungs. It can be life-threatening and needs to be treated quickly.

    Post-thrombotic syndrome is a common long-term complication of DVT in the leg. A UCLH leaflet describes post-thrombotic syndrome as causing symptoms that include leg swelling, varicose veins and occasionally leg ulcers. Post-thrombotic syndrome usually appears within about six months to two years of the clot. Estimates of how many people develop post-thrombotic syndrome vary: a UCLH leaflet says 20 to 50 in 100, usually after extensive clots, while another hospital leaflet says about 1 in 4.

    A UCLH leaflet says compression stockings may help with these symptoms. A UCLH leaflet says compression stockings should not be worn until the initial leg swelling has settled.

    An NHS hospital leaflet advises regular exercise, with walking especially helpful, and regular short walking breaks on long journeys.

    Who is at risk of a DVT, and what about pregnancy?

    The NHS lists risk factors for DVT including being over 60, obesity, smoking, hormonal contraception or HRT, cancer and a previous DVT. The NHS also lists smoking, being over 60 and taking hormone replacement therapy as risk factors.

    NHS trust guidance says warfarin should not be used in pregnancy and that there is too little research on the newer oral anticoagulants in pregnancy. NHS trust guidance describes low-molecular-weight heparin injections as the usual choice for treating a clot in pregnancy.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does a blood thinner dissolve the clot?

    No. A hospital leaflet says anticoagulants reduce the risk of the clot getting worse and let the body break it down. The medicine stops the clot growing and reduces the risk of another clot. Clot-busting drugs or surgery are kept for severe cases.

    How long are blood thinners taken for?

    The NHS says people usually need them for at least three months. NHS hospital leaflets say that if it is not a first DVT, treatment is normally recommended for life. People prescribed a blood thinner are advised not to stop it without talking to a doctor.

    When does a DVT become an emergency?

    The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if leg symptoms come with breathlessness or chest pain, as this can mean the clot has reached the lungs. If a doctor thinks a leg clot is likely, NICE says an ultrasound scan should be available within 4 hours of it being requested, or within 24 hours if that is not possible.

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.

    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Biomarkers

    • INR The prothrombin time expressed as a standardised ratio, the number used to dose warfarin and to gauge the liver's production of clotting factors.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
