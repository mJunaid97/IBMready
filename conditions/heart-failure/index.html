    Heart failure

    Also known as: congestive heart failure, cardiac failure

    A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    When to seek care

    Seek urgent help for breathlessness at rest, waking at night gasping, pink frothy sputum, chest pain, fainting, or rapid weight gain (more than 2 kg in three days).

    Overview

    Heart failure is the end result of damage from heart attacks, high blood pressure, valve disease or cardiomyopathy. Blood backs up behind the failing ventricle: fluid in the lungs causes breathlessness, fluid in the tissues causes swelling. It is a chronic condition managed with drugs that reduce the heart's workload and, in some cases, devices. Coronary artery disease is the commonest cause, followed by high blood pressure, valve disease and cardiomyopathy; lung disease, congenital heart disease and, rarely, heart tumours also contribute, and obesity, diabetes, smoking, heavy alcohol and cocaine use raise the risk. People notice breathlessness on exertion or at rest, fatigue that worsens with activity, swelling of the feet and ankles that can spread up the legs, and sometimes light-headedness or fainting; some mainly have reduced exercise tolerance, others mainly swelling. There is no cure, but treatment controls symptoms, slows progression and improves quality of life.

    Causes

    • Coronary artery disease and previous heart attacks
    • High blood pressure
    • Valve disease
    • Cardiomyopathies (genetic, viral, alcohol, chemotherapy)
    • Atrial fibrillation
    • Congenital heart disease
    • Lung disease

    Risk factors

    • Age
    • Diabetes
    • Obesity
    • Smoking
    • Excess alcohol
    • Cocaine use

    Symptoms

    Shortness of breathSwelling (oedema)FatigueCough

    What a clinician may find

    • Raised jugular venous pressure
    • Crackles at the lung bases
    • Pitting ankle oedema
    • Enlarged liver
    • Third heart sound
    • Weight gain from fluid retention

    Complications

    • Pulmonary oedema (acute breathlessness)
    • Kidney impairment
    • Arrhythmias and sudden death
    • Repeated hospital admissions
    • Acute decompensation triggered by missed medicines or excess salt and fluid

    Diagnosis

    Blood natriuretic peptide (BNP) is raised; echocardiography measures the ejection fraction and finds the cause. ECG, chest X-ray and blood tests for kidney function and anaemia complete the picture. The diagnosis needs both symptoms or signs and objective evidence of heart dysfunction, which is why echocardiography is central: it measures the ejection fraction, separating reduced from preserved ejection fraction, and shows valve disease and the wall-motion abnormalities left by previous infarcts. A 24-hour ECG is used when a rhythm disturbance is suspected, and breathing tests check whether a lung problem is contributing to the breathlessness.

    Treatment

    Diuretics relieve fluid overload; ACE inhibitors or ARNI, beta blockers, mineralocorticoid antagonists and SGLT2 inhibitors prolong life in reduced ejection fraction. Salt and fluid moderation, daily weights, vaccination and exercise rehabilitation. Selected patients receive pacemakers, defibrillators or, rarely, transplantation. Treatment depends on the stage. Early on the aim is to prevent progression through risk-factor control and medicines such as statins, beta blockers and renin-angiotensin system inhibitors; later, procedures such as valve repair or an implantable defibrillator are added. Acute deteriorations (decompensation) are often triggered by missed medicines or by exceeding salt and fluid limits, so identifying and treating the trigger is part of each admission, and daily weighing lets fluid gain be caught early. Lifestyle changes, including lower salt intake, run alongside the medicines throughout.

    Prevention

    Treating blood pressure and coronary disease early, limiting alcohol, and controlling diabetes. Because coronary disease causes most heart failure, the measures that prevent heart attacks (not smoking, blood pressure and cholesterol control, physical activity and a healthy weight) prevent most heart failure too.

    What does heart failure feel like, and how does it progress?

    Heart failure means the heart cannot pump blood properly. The NHS says it does not mean the heart has stopped working or is about to stop. Symptoms the NHS lists include breathlessness during everyday activity or when lying down, unusual tiredness, light-headedness, swelling in the feet, ankles, legs or tummy, sudden weight gain and a cough at night. MedlinePlus adds that the pulse may feel fast or irregular.

    The US National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) says symptoms usually get worse as the heart grows weaker. It adds that weight gain, ankle swelling or increasing breathlessness may mean fluid is building up in the body.

    NHS 111 Wales describes heart failure as a long-term condition that usually gets slowly worse over time, although it is very difficult to tell how it will progress in any one person.

    What are the types of heart failure?

    NHLBI says heart failure can develop suddenly, which is called acute, or over time as the heart gets weaker, which is called chronic. It can affect one or both sides of the heart.

    NHLBI describes two forms of left-sided heart failure, named after the ejection fraction. This is the percentage of the blood in the left ventricle that is pumped out with each heartbeat. In heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, weakened heart muscle is the usual driver. In heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, the ventricle is stiff rather than weak, so it cannot fill with enough blood to pump out to the body.

    NHLBI says left-sided heart failure is the main cause of right-sided heart failure, because pressure backs up into the lungs and strains the right side. Other causes of right-sided heart failure include congenital heart defects, abnormal heart valves and lung damage such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

    How is heart failure diagnosed?

    The NHS says a GP should check the symptoms and offer a blood test to see how well the heart is working. NHS England advises GPs to arrange an NT-proBNP blood test for people with suspected heart failure. NICE says a low result in a person who is not on treatment makes heart failure less likely, while higher results lead to faster referral for specialist assessment and an echocardiogram.

    The NHS lists blood tests, an electrocardiogram (ECG), an echocardiogram, breathing tests and a chest X-ray among the tests used. Its main heart failure page also mentions urine tests and MRI scans. An echocardiogram is an ultrasound scan of the heart. NICE says it is used to exclude important valve disease, among other purposes.

    What treatments are used for heart failure?

    The NHS lists healthy lifestyle changes, medicines, implanted devices and surgery among the treatments for heart failure, and describes medicines as the main one. It says diuretics reduce fluid build-up, ankle swelling and breathlessness, and that beta blockers slow the heart.

    Implanted devices can help control or monitor the heart rhythm. They include pacemakers, cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT) devices and implantable cardioverter defibrillators. Surgery can repair or replace damaged valves or improve blood flow from blocked arteries. For severe cases, options include a left ventricular assist device and a heart transplant. The NHS says a transplant may be needed when severe heart failure cannot be treated effectively with medicines or other surgery, and that it carries serious risks and is not suitable for everyone.

    The NHS says people should be offered an exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation programme, which often includes education and emotional support.

    What is it like living with heart failure?

    The NHS says treatment will usually need to continue for the rest of life. It describes taking medicines as prescribed, weighing regularly, staying active, eating well and stopping smoking as helpful.

    The NHS says people can usually still drink alcohol within the recommended limits, but may be advised to stop completely if their heart failure is directly related to drinking. NHLBI says people may be asked to limit salt and liquids to reduce fluid build-up. The NHS says everyone with heart failure should be offered a flu vaccine every year and a one-off pneumococcal vaccine.

    NHLBI says living with heart failure may cause fear, anxiety, depression and stress, and that relaxation techniques, counselling and support groups can help lower stress and anxiety. The NHS says worries can be raised with the GP or care team, and that a heart support group may be useful.

    What is the outlook, and when should someone get help?

    The NHS says that for most people heart failure is a long-term condition that cannot be cured, but treatment can help keep the symptoms under control, possibly for many years. NHLBI says treatment can help many people have a higher quality of life.

    The NHS advises booking a GP appointment for symptoms of heart failure. It advises asking for an urgent GP appointment or contacting NHS 111 for breathlessness when lying down or during everyday activity, coughing up frothy pink phlegm, or sudden weight gain. The NHS says to call 999 for severe breathlessness, such as gasping, choking or being unable to get words out, for pale, blue or grey lips or skin, or if someone has passed out and is not responding normally. It advises people not to drive themselves to A&E.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does heart failure mean the heart has stopped?

    No. The NHS says heart failure means the heart cannot pump blood properly, but it does not mean the heart has stopped working or is about to stop working. For most people it is a long-term condition, and the NHS says treatment can help keep symptoms under control, possibly for many years.

    Can heart failure be cured?

    The NHS says that for most people heart failure is a long-term condition that cannot be cured. Treatment can help keep symptoms under control, possibly for many years. The NHS says treatment will usually need to continue for the rest of life.

    Will treatment be needed for life?

    The NHS says treatment will usually need to continue for the rest of life. It describes medicines as the main treatment, alongside healthy lifestyle changes. Care teams may suggest daily weighing so that changes in weight are caught quickly, and the NHS lists regular monitoring as part of care.

    Who looks after people with heart failure?

    A GP usually starts by checking the symptoms and offering a blood test. NICE links a raised NT-proBNP result to referral for specialist assessment and an echocardiogram. The NHS says worries can be raised with the GP or care team, and that a heart support group may be useful. Carers can attend appointments too.

    Is exercise part of heart failure care?

    The NHS says people with heart failure should be offered an exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation programme, which often includes education and emotional support. The NHS says everyone with heart failure should be offered a flu vaccine every year and a one-off pneumococcal vaccine.

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.

    Medical tests

    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin is a prescription tablet from the SGLT2 inhibitor group, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Empagliflozin Empagliflozin is a prescription medicine used to treat type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Anticoagulants Medicines that slow the clotting cascade to prevent and treat clots in veins and the heart: strokes in atrial fibrillation.
    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
    • Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, also called aldosterone antagonists, are medicines that act against the hormone aldosterone.
    • Nitrates Vasodilators that relieve angina within minutes by relaxing veins and coronary arteries.
    • SGLT2 inhibitors SGLT2 inhibitors are tablets used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
