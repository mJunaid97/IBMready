    Migraine

    Also known as: migraine headache

    A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours, with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound.

    When to seek care

    Emergency care for a headache that is the worst ever or peaks within a minute, or comes with fever, stiff neck, weakness, confusion or a first-ever aura after 50.

    Overview

    Migraine is a disorder of brain excitability rather than of blood vessels alone. A wave of altered activity spreading across the cortex explains the aura that a third of sufferers experience, and activation of the trigeminal pain pathway explains the headache. It affects about one person in seven and is the leading cause of disability in young women. Attacks pass through phases: a prodrome, hours to days beforehand, with food cravings, mood changes or yawning; an aura in some people; the headache itself; and a postdrome. Not every phase occurs in every attack. Aura typically begins within the hour before the headache and usually lasts less than an hour, with visual change, difficulty speaking, tingling in the hands or face or confusion. It is thought to arise from cortical spreading depression, and researchers still debate whether that same process causes the headache. Triggers such as stress or sensory overload usually precede an attack by less than two days, and a headache diary is the practical way to identify an individual's triggers.

    Causes

    • Inherited susceptibility with triggers: sleep change, missed meals, stress and stress release, hormones, alcohol, certain foods, bright light, weather

    Risk factors

    • Female sex
    • Family history
    • Age 20 to 50
    • Depression and anxiety
    • Medication overuse

    Symptoms

    HeadacheNauseaVomitingDizzinessNumbnessTingling (pins and needles)

    What a clinician may find

    • Normal examination between and usually during attacks

    Complications

    • Chronic migraine (15 or more headache days a month)
    • Medication-overuse headache
    • Slightly raised stroke risk in migraine with aura, especially with smoking and the combined pill
    • Coexistence with, or misdiagnosis as, stroke or transient ischaemic attack, depression and anxiety disorders

    Diagnosis

    Clinical, from the pattern of attacks; a headache diary helps. Scans are needed only for red-flag features such as a change in pattern, abnormal examination or a thunderclap onset. There is no single confirmatory test. The clinician reviews the health history, performs physical and neurological examinations, and asks when headaches start, how long they last, what they feel like, what triggers them and whether nausea or visual change accompany them. Migraine is often confused with, or coexists with, other conditions including ischaemic stroke and transient ischaemic attack, depression, generalised anxiety and panic disorder, which can complicate the diagnosis.

    Treatment

    Attacks: a triptan taken early, plus an NSAID or paracetamol and an anti-sickness drug; rest in a dark room. Prevention when attacks are frequent: propranolol, amitriptyline, topiramate, candesartan, CGRP antibody injections, and avoiding identified triggers. Gepants are a newer class used alongside triptans for acute attacks, and painkillers work best taken as soon as the headache starts. Preventive medicine has to be taken every day to be effective, and a combination of treatments is sometimes needed before one works. Migraine cannot be cured, but treatment can manage attacks and reduce their number.

    Prevention

    Regular sleep and meals, hydration, limiting caffeine and alcohol, exercise, stress management, and limiting painkillers to fewer than 10 days a month. NINDS and the NHS emphasise healthier sleep habits (enough sleep and a consistent bedtime), not skipping meals, avoiding identified food triggers, managing stress and losing weight if overweight.

    What does a migraine attack feel like?

    The NHS says a migraine attack typically brings a throbbing headache, often on one side of the head, with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. The cause is unknown, but a migraine happens when there are changes to nerves and blood vessels in the brain, the NHS says.

    An attack can pass through several stages, and not every stage occurs every time. The NHS lists early warning symptoms such as unusual tiredness, yawning, food cravings, mood changes, a stiff neck and needing to pass urine more often. These can start hours, or up to two days, before the headache.

    The NHS says about 1 in 3 people with migraine have an aura. It can involve zigzag patterns, flashing lights, pins and needles or difficulty speaking. Aura should not last longer than an hour. The Migraine Trust says recovery after the headache can take hours or days.

    What types of migraine are there?

    The Migraine Trust says migraine without aura is the most common type. Migraine with aura covers several forms, including brainstem aura, retinal migraine, hemiplegic migraine and silent migraine.

    A typical aura involves changes in vision, other sensory symptoms such as tingling, or problems with speaking. NINDS says migraine with brainstem aura needs at least two symptoms during an episode, such as vertigo (a spinning feeling), seeing double, trouble with balance or slurred speech.

    The Migraine Trust says some people have aura symptoms but no headache, sometimes called silent migraine. Hemiplegic migraine is a rare, severe type involving temporary weakness or paralysis on one side of the body. In vestibular migraine, vertigo, dizziness or unsteadiness are often the main symptoms. The Trust defines chronic migraine as headache on at least 15 days each month.

    How is migraine diagnosed?

    NICE says a clinician may ask a person to keep a headache diary to help identify the type of headache. The diary records how often headaches happen, how long and how severe they are, and all medicines taken. NICE advises keeping it for a minimum of eight weeks. For migraine that usually happens around a period, the diary should cover at least the next two periods.

    A secondary headache is one where another health problem is behind the headaches. NICE says a person may be referred for tests or to a specialist if this is suspected. NICE lists warning signs such as a head injury in the past three months, headaches that start very suddenly, and speech or balance problems that keep getting worse.

    NICE advises against referring people with migraine for brain scans solely for reassurance.

    What treatments are there for migraine?

    The NHS says migraine cannot be cured, but treatment can help with managing attacks and preventing them. For attacks, the NHS lists painkillers, triptans, gepants and anti-sickness medicine, and advises taking painkillers as soon as the headache starts.

    The Migraine Trust says gepants can be an option for people who cannot take triptans or for whom triptans do not work, and that they appear not to cause medication overuse headache. It says gepants, CGRP antibodies and flunarizine are usually prescribed by headache specialists rather than GPs.

    For prevention, the NHS lists beta blockers, antidepressants, epilepsy medicines, acupuncture, relaxation techniques, cognitive behavioural therapy and vitamin B2. NHS Scotland guidance also lists candesartan and botulinum toxin for chronic migraine.

    The Migraine Trust describes medication overuse headache as a headache caused by taking acute migraine medicines too often. It says over-the-counter painkillers, triptans and opioids can cause it. NICE asks clinicians to be alert to it in people whose headaches developed alongside regular use of pain medicines.

    Living with migraine: what helps day to day?

    The Migraine Trust says triggers usually combine several factors rather than one cause. It names stress, disrupted sleep and skipped meals among the most common, and says shifts in routine often seem to set off attacks. The NHS names periods, stress, tiredness and certain foods or drinks.

    The Trust says too little and too much sleep can each trigger attacks, and that regular bedtimes and wake times may help. Some people have attacks when stress eases, such as at weekends. MedlinePlus, a US health site, says regular sleep, exercise and stress management may help.

    Foods such as chocolate and cheese are often blamed, but the Trust says the evidence is thin. MedlinePlus describes a headache diary as a way to find triggers.

    When should someone get help for migraine?

    The NHS says to call 999 for a sudden, extremely painful headache, speech or memory problems, loss of vision or double vision, drowsiness or confusion, a seizure, weakness on one side of the body, or a recent head injury. NHS inform likewise advises calling 999 or going to A&E for one-sided weakness or slurred speech.

    The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 if an attack lasts longer than 72 hours, if aura lasts longer than an hour, or if the person is pregnant or has recently given birth.

    A non-urgent GP appointment is advised if attacks are severe, getting worse, lasting longer than usual, happening more than once a week, hard to control, or regularly happening around a period.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can migraine be cured?

    The NHS says migraine cannot be cured, but treatment can help with managing attacks and preventing them. MedlinePlus likewise says there is no cure, and treatment focuses on relieving symptoms and preventing further attacks. The NHS says the treatments available depend on how severe the migraine is and how often attacks happen.

    Is migraine just a bad headache?

    The NHS says a migraine attack typically brings a throbbing headache, often on one side of the head, with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. Some people also have an aura, such as zigzag patterns, flashing lights or pins and needles. Early symptoms can include tiredness, food cravings and mood changes.

    Can someone have an aura without a headache?

    Yes. The Migraine Trust says some people have aura symptoms but no headache, which is sometimes called silent migraine or migraine aura without headache, and that it is more common in older people. The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 if aura lasts longer than an hour.

    Is a brain scan needed to diagnose migraine?

    NICE advises against referring people diagnosed with migraine for brain scans solely for reassurance. Its quality standard says people with tension-type headache or migraine should not be imaged if they have no signs or symptoms of a secondary headache. A clinician may refer for tests or to a specialist if a secondary cause is suspected.

    Can painkillers make headaches worse?

    The Migraine Trust describes medication overuse headache as a headache caused by taking acute migraine medicines too often. It says over-the-counter painkillers, triptans and opioids can cause it, while gepants appear not to. The Trust recommends speaking to a GP for anyone taking acute medicines frequently.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Vertigo and balance problems Vertigo is the feeling that you, or the world around you, is moving or spinning, and it is often caused by a problem in the inner ear.

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Amitriptyline Amitriptyline is a prescription-only medicine from a group called tricyclic antidepressants, used for nerve pain and to help prevent migraine.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Metoclopramide Metoclopramide is an anti-sickness medicine used to stop nausea (feeling sick) and vomiting (being sick).
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Combined hormonal contraceptives Combined hormonal contraceptives contain both an oestrogen and a progestogen, and include the combined pill, a skin patch and a vaginal ring.
    • Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines are used to treat nausea and vomiting, and metoclopramide is the member covered on this site.
    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
    • Triptans Triptans are medicines for treating migraine attacks, and sumatriptan is the triptan covered on this site.

    Guides and news

    • Dizziness and vertigo: causes and warning signs Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure.
    • Headaches: types, causes and warning signs Tension-type headache is the most common type, and a headache that starts suddenly and is very severe needs a 999 call.
    • Migraine and tension headache compared Tension headache is usually a dull, tight pressure on both sides of the head, while migraine often throbs and brings nausea or sensitivity to light.
