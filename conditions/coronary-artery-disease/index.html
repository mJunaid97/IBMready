    Coronary artery disease

    Also known as: coronary heart disease, ischaemic heart disease

    Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.

    When to seek care

    Call emergency services for chest pain lasting more than a few minutes, or angina that is new, more frequent or occurs at rest.

    Open in the 3D explorerCoronary vessels anatomy

    Overview

    Plaque builds up over decades in the arteries that supply the heart. When a narrowing limits flow during exertion the muscle becomes short of oxygen and signals this as angina; when a plaque ruptures and a clot blocks the artery, the result is a heart attack. It is the leading cause of death worldwide, and largely preventable.

    Causes

    • Atherosclerosis: cholesterol-rich plaque forming in the artery wall, driven by inflammation

    Risk factors

    • Smoking
    • High LDL cholesterol
    • High blood pressure
    • Diabetes
    • Family history of early heart disease
    • Age and male sex
    • Obesity, inactivity, poor diet
    • Chronic kidney disease

    Symptoms

    Chest painShortness of breathFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Often none between attacks
    • Signs of risk factors: high blood pressure, xanthelasma, nicotine staining

    Complications

    • Myocardial infarction
    • Heart failure
    • Arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death

    Diagnosis

    The story of exertional chest pressure relieved by rest is the key. An ECG may be normal at rest; a stress test, CT coronary angiography or invasive angiography shows the narrowings. Blood tests assess cholesterol, glucose and kidney function.

    Treatment

    Lifestyle change and medication for everyone: stop smoking, statins to lower LDL, aspirin to prevent clots, blood pressure and diabetes control, and drugs to relieve angina (nitrates, beta blockers). Significant narrowings can be opened with angioplasty and a stent or bypassed with surgery.

    Prevention

    Not smoking, regular exercise, a diet low in saturated fat and salt, healthy weight, and treating blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes.

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.
    • Abdominal aortic aneurysm An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the aorta, the body's main blood vessel, where it runs through the tummy.
    • Erectile dysfunction Erectile dysfunction, also called impotence, is being unable to get or keep an erection.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • High cholesterol (hypercholesterolaemia) High cholesterol, also called hypercholesterolaemia, means there is too much cholesterol in the blood.
    • Peripheral arterial disease A long-term condition in which the arteries that supply blood to the legs become narrowed or blocked.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.

    Biomarkers

    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Clopidogrel An antiplatelet medicine that stops platelets clumping by blocking their P2Y12 receptor, taken to prevent clots after a heart attack, stent.
    • Ezetimibe Ezetimibe is a prescribed cholesterol-lowering medicine that works by reducing how much cholesterol the gut absorbs.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.

    Drug classes

    • Antiplatelet drugs Medicines that stop platelets clumping into the clots that block arteries.
    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.

    • Calcium channel blockers Medicines that relax the muscle in artery walls (and, for some members.
    • Cholesterol absorption inhibitors Cholesterol absorption inhibitors are medicines that lower cholesterol by partly blocking its absorption in the small intestine.
    • Contrast agents Substances given to make blood vessels, organs, the gut or joints stand out on imaging: iodinated agents for X-ray and CT, gadolinium agents for MRI.
    • Nitrates Vasodilators that relieve angina within minutes by relaxing veins and coronary arteries.
    • PDE5 inhibitors PDE5 inhibitors are medicines that include sildenafil and tadalafil, used mainly for erection problems.
    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.

    Guides and news

    • Chest pain: causes and when to get help Most chest pain does not come from the heart, but sudden pain that does not ease or spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back needs a 999 call.
    • High cholesterol: diet, tests and medicines The NHS says cutting down on saturated fat can help lower cholesterol.
    • Cholesterol test results explained A cholesterol test reports total, HDL and non-HDL cholesterol plus triglycerides, and doctors read them with other risk factors.
    • Coronary angiogram: what happens and risks A coronary angiogram is an X-ray test that uses dye to show narrowed or blocked heart arteries.
    • The Eatwell Guide: a balanced diet explained The Eatwell Guide shows the proportions of food groups in a balanced UK diet, with fruit.
    • Erection problems and seeing a GP Erection problems are common and can have physical causes, and the NHS advises seeing a GP if they keep happening.
    • Healthy weight: what BMI shows and what it misses BMI is an NHS check on whether weight suits height, but it cannot tell muscle from fat, so waist size and a GP discussion add to it.
    • Heart attack, cardiac arrest and angina A heart attack is a blocked blood supply to the heart muscle, cardiac arrest is the heart stopping, and angina is chest pain that eases with rest.
    • How much exercise adults need each week The NHS says adults aged 19 to 64 should do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus strength work.
    • Statins: common questions answered The NHS says statins lower the cholesterol the body makes, to reduce the risk of heart attacks and strokes, and they are usually taken long term.
