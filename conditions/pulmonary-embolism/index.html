    Pulmonary embolism

    Also known as: PE, blood clot in the lung

    Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.

    When to seek care

    Emergency services for sudden breathlessness, chest pain on breathing, coughing blood or collapse, especially with a swollen leg or recent surgery.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Overview

    A pulmonary embolism cuts off blood flow to part of the lung: the affected segment is ventilated but not perfused, oxygen levels fall and the right ventricle strains against the obstruction. Small emboli cause pleuritic pain and breathlessness; a large one can cause collapse and death within minutes. Symptoms are not specific. Chest pain and breathlessness are the commonest, with dizziness or light-headedness, a fast heartbeat, coughing up blood or collapse also possible, so PE can neither be confirmed nor excluded from symptoms alone, and some emboli cause no symptoms and are found incidentally on a scan. Nearly all cases arise from a deep vein thrombosis in the legs or pelvis. A substantial proportion of cases are 'unprovoked', with no identifiable cause; the rest are 'provoked' by a clear trigger such as surgery, immobility or serious illness, and this distinction shapes how long treatment continues.

    Causes

    • Deep vein thrombosis of the leg or pelvis
    • Rarely fat, air or amniotic fluid emboli

    Risk factors

    • Recent surgery or immobility
    • Cancer
    • Pregnancy and the postpartum period
    • Oestrogen-containing medicines
    • Previous clots or inherited thrombophilia
    • Obesity, smoking, long-haul travel
    • Major surgery, especially gynaecological operations and surgery on the pelvis and legs
    • Other serious illness
    • Hormone replacement therapy

    Symptoms

    Shortness of breathChest painCoughDizzinessSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • Fast breathing and pulse
    • Low oxygen saturation
    • Signs of DVT in a leg
    • Low blood pressure in massive PE
    • A normal examination does not exclude PE; some emboli are found incidentally on imaging

    Complications

    • Collapse and cardiac arrest
    • Right heart failure
    • Chronic pulmonary hypertension
    • Significant bleeding in a small proportion of people on anticoagulant treatment
    • Recurrent clots, more likely when the first was unprovoked or the risk persists

    Diagnosis

    Probability scoring and D-dimer, then CT pulmonary angiography to show the clot; ventilation-perfusion scanning in pregnancy or kidney disease. ECG and echocardiography assess strain on the right heart. In someone judged at low clinical probability, a negative D-dimer makes PE very unlikely. A positive result is not conclusive, because D-dimer also rises with inflammation and infection, so people at higher risk or with a positive test proceed to lung imaging for a definitive answer.

    Treatment

    Immediate anticoagulation (low-molecular-weight heparin, then a direct oral anticoagulant or warfarin for three to six months or longer); clot-dissolving drugs or catheter removal for massive PE with shock; oxygen and support. Anticoagulants do not thin the blood or dissolve the clot; they alter clotting so that the clot cannot grow and new clots do not form while the body clears it. The choice of drug depends on kidney function, body weight and other medicines being taken, and significant bleeding occurs in a small proportion of people treated. Clots provoked by a transient risk factor are treated for a limited period, whereas unprovoked clots or persisting risks such as cancer, chronic immobility or a strong clotting disorder may need long-term treatment to prevent recurrence. Oxygen helps breathlessness and low oxygen levels early on. Untreated PE carries a high death rate; with treatment the outlook is much better.

    Prevention

    DVT prevention: early mobilisation, prophylactic injections in hospital, compression stockings, keeping moving on long journeys.

    What are the symptoms of a pulmonary embolism?

    The NHS lists sudden breathlessness, chest pain that gets worse when breathing in and coughing up blood as the main symptoms, with leg pain or swelling also possible if a DVT is present. The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E for sudden difficulty breathing, chest or upper back pain, a very fast heartbeat or passing out.

    Hospital leaflets note that a small pulmonary embolism may cause no symptoms at all. NICE guidance on suspected pulmonary embolism describes using a clinical probability score, followed by tests such as a D-dimer blood test or a scan.

    What causes a pulmonary embolism?

    NHS hospital leaflets say the clot is usually a deep vein thrombosis (DVT), most often in the leg or pelvis, that has travelled to the lungs. A few come from an arm vein or from a clot in the heart.

    Risk factors include pregnancy and the weeks after birth, oestrogen-containing contraception or hormone replacement therapy, and cancer. Some people have an inherited tendency for their blood to clot (thrombophilia), such as factor V Leiden.

    Sometimes a pulmonary embolism happens for no obvious reason. Doctors describe these as unprovoked, and the likely cause is important in deciding how long treatment continues.

    How is a pulmonary embolism diagnosed?

    NICE guidance describes estimating the clinical probability of pulmonary embolism with a score, then using a D-dimer blood test or imaging depending on the result. A Whittington Health policy says a negative D-dimer test reliably excludes pulmonary embolism in people with a low clinical probability, so further imaging is not needed in that group.

    The usual confirming scan is a CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA).

    NICE guidance says that when a scan cannot be done straight away, people thought likely to have a pulmonary embolism are offered interim anticoagulant treatment until it can be done. Treatment can therefore begin before the scan result is back.

    How is a pulmonary embolism treated?

    Once the diagnosis is confirmed, treatment usually continues with anticoagulant tablets or injections, depending on what suits the person. Anticoagulants do not dissolve the clot. The body clears it over time. They stop existing clots growing and help prevent new ones forming. Clot-busting drugs (thrombolysis) are considered for people whose circulation is affected by a severe pulmonary embolism.

    Hospital leaflets say treatment can last anywhere from three months to the rest of a person's life, depending on the cause and the person's risk of another clot.

    Anticoagulants raise the risk of bleeding. Hospital leaflets list black or bloody stools, vomiting or coughing up blood, and heavy bleeding that does not stop as reasons to get urgent medical help, by calling NHS 111 or going to A&E.

    What is recovery from a pulmonary embolism like?

    The NHS says a full recovery is expected if a pulmonary embolism is spotted and treated early. Hospital leaflets say it is common to feel breathless for a few weeks or months after a pulmonary embolism. Hospital leaflets advise talking to a GP if breathlessness does not improve, and note that a minority of people have symptoms for a prolonged period.

    Some people have longer-term problems. One NHS leaflet notes that a minority of people continue to have symptoms for a long time, which may be due to a condition called chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).

    Questions about long-term treatment, travel or hormone medicines belong with the treating team or GP.

    Frequently asked questions

    Where do the clots in a pulmonary embolism come from?

    NHS hospital leaflets say the clot is usually a deep vein thrombosis (DVT), most often in the leg or pelvis, that has travelled to the lungs. A few come from an arm vein or from the heart.

    Can a pulmonary embolism cause no symptoms?

    Yes. Hospital leaflets note that a small pulmonary embolism may cause no symptoms at all. NICE guidance describes using a clinical probability score and tests such as a D-dimer blood test and a scan to diagnose it.

    Why is a D-dimer test not enough on its own?

    A negative D-dimer test reliably excludes pulmonary embolism in people with a low clinical probability, according to a Whittington Health policy. NICE guidance recommends imaging, usually a CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA), when a pulmonary embolism is thought likely.

    How long does treatment last?

    Hospital leaflets say it can last from three months to the rest of a person's life. The length depends on the cause of the clot and the risk of another one, and the treating team makes the decision.

    How long does recovery take?

    Hospital leaflets say it is common to feel breathless for a few weeks or months after a pulmonary embolism. The NHS says people should make a full recovery if the condition is spotted and treated early, though hospital leaflets say it is common to feel breathless for a few weeks or months.

    Conditions

    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.

    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Warfarin The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.
    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.

    Drug classes

    • Anticoagulants Medicines that slow the clotting cascade to prevent and treat clots in veins and the heart: strokes in atrial fibrillation.
    • Combined hormonal contraceptives Combined hormonal contraceptives contain both an oestrogen and a progestogen, and include the combined pill, a skin patch and a vaginal ring.
    • Contrast agents Substances given to make blood vessels, organs, the gut or joints stand out on imaging: iodinated agents for X-ray and CT, gadolinium agents for MRI.

    Guides and news

    • Long flights and DVT: risk and how to lower it Long journeys can raise the chance of a leg blood clot (DVT); the NHS and TravelHealthPro describe moving, drinking water and sometimes stockings.
    • Chest pain: causes and when to get help Most chest pain does not come from the heart, but sudden pain that does not ease or spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back needs a 999 call.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
    • Swollen ankles and legs: causes and red flags Swollen ankles and legs often come from sitting or standing for long periods, but can signal a clot or a heart, kidney or liver problem.
    • Warfarin and apixaban: medicines and food Bleeding is the main risk of both, so the NHS says other medicines, alcohol and, for warfarin, some foods and INR blood tests all matter.
