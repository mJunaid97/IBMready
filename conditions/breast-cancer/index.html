    Breast cancer

    Also known as: breast carcinoma, ductal carcinoma, lobular carcinoma, DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ), breast cancer in men

    A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast. It may remain within the ducts (in situ) or invade the surrounding tissue and spread to the axillary lymph nodes and beyond.

    When to seek care

    See a GP promptly about any new lump in the breast or armpit, skin dimpling, a change in a nipple or blood-stained nipple discharge, in anyone with breast tissue, at any age and whatever their gender. Chest masculinisation removes most but not all of the breast tissue, so a new lump or change in the chest wall still needs the same assessment. NICE recommends urgent referral to a breast clinic for an unexplained breast lump in anyone aged 30 or over and for discharge, retraction or other changes of one nipple in anyone aged 50 or over. A hot, red, swollen breast with fever suggests infection and should be seen the same day; a red, inflamed breast that does not settle with antibiotics also needs referral.

    Overview

    Breast cancer is the commonest cancer in women worldwide, and most cases occur after 50. Most tumours start in the milk ducts, fewer in the milk-producing lobules; ductal carcinoma in situ stays within the duct, while invasive cancer has broken through it. Every invasive cancer is tested for oestrogen receptors, progesterone receptors and the HER2 protein, which with grade and stage decides treatment. It is rare in men, and anyone with breast tissue can develop it.

    Causes

    • Accumulated DNA damage in duct or lobule cells, driven in part by lifetime exposure to oestrogen and progesterone
    • Inherited faults in BRCA1, BRCA2 and a few other genes, responsible for a minority of cases
    • Previous radiotherapy to the chest, particularly in youth
    • No single cause is found in most cases; ageing is the strongest factor

    Risk factors

    • Increasing age: most cases are diagnosed after 50
    • A close relative with breast or ovarian cancer, or a known BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation
    • Longer lifetime oestrogen exposure: an early first period, a late menopause, no pregnancies or a first pregnancy late in life
    • Combined hormone replacement therapy and, to a small degree, the combined contraceptive pill: the increase is small, relates to how long they are used, and falls after stopping
    • Alcohol, and being overweight after the menopause
    • Dense breast tissue on mammography, a previous breast cancer or some benign breast conditions
    • In trans women, several years of feminising hormone therapy: the risk is thought to be higher than in cis men but lower than in cis women; in men, conditions that raise oestrogen levels such as obesity, Klinefelter syndrome and cirrhosis

    Symptoms

    Breast lumpSwelling (oedema)

    What a clinician may find

    • A firm, irregular, usually painless lump in the breast or armpit
    • Dimpling, puckering or an orange-peel texture of the skin over the breast
    • A newly inverted nipple, a rash or crusting on the nipple, or blood-stained discharge
    • A change in the size or outline of one breast
    • In advanced disease, bone pain, breathlessness or jaundice from spread to bone, lung or liver

    Complications

    • Spread to the axillary lymph nodes and to bone, lung, liver or brain (metastatic breast cancer)
    • Lymphoedema of the arm after axillary surgery or radiotherapy
    • Bone loss and menopausal symptoms from endocrine therapy; heart effects of some chemotherapy and HER2-targeted drugs
    • Recurrence in the breast, chest wall or elsewhere, sometimes many years later
    • Anxiety, altered body image and loss of fertility in younger patients

    Diagnosis

    A suspicious lump or nipple change is assessed in a one-stop breast clinic by triple assessment: clinical examination, imaging (mammography, with ultrasound for younger women and dense breasts) and a core needle biopsy of anything abnormal. The biopsy confirms invasive or in situ disease, grade and receptor status (oestrogen receptor, progesterone receptor and HER2). Ultrasound of the axilla with needle sampling of suspicious nodes, and MRI in selected cases, complete local staging; CT and bone scans are reserved for larger tumours, involved nodes or symptoms suggesting spread. Genetic testing for BRCA1 and BRCA2 is offered when the age at diagnosis, tumour type or family history suggests an inherited cause. Screening in England invites women, and anyone else registered female with a GP who has breast tissue, for mammography every three years; the first invitation arrives between the ages of 50 and 53, routine invitations stop after 70, and anyone aged 71 or over can still ask their local breast screening service for screening. Trans women who have taken hormones for some years and trans men who have not had chest surgery may need to ask to be included.

    Treatment

    Early breast cancer is treated with surgery: either wide local excision, removing the tumour with a margin of normal tissue and followed by radiotherapy to the rest of the breast, or mastectomy, in each case with sentinel lymph node biopsy to check the axilla and clearance of the nodes if they are involved. Reconstruction can be immediate or delayed. Treatment given after surgery, or before it to shrink a large tumour, is chosen by receptor status and the risk of recurrence: chemotherapy; endocrine therapy for oestrogen-receptor-positive cancers (tamoxifen, or an aromatase inhibitor after the menopause), taken for years; and trastuzumab or similar antibodies for HER2-positive disease. Metastatic breast cancer is not usually curable but is often controlled for years with endocrine, targeted and chemotherapy, radiotherapy for painful bone deposits and bone-protecting drugs. Care is planned by a multidisciplinary team following NICE guidance.

    Prevention

    Not all breast cancer can be prevented, but keeping to a healthy weight, being physically active, limiting alcohol and breastfeeding all lower the risk, and hormone replacement therapy is best reviewed regularly and continued only for as long as it is needed. Attending screening finds cancers before they can be felt. People at high risk because of family history or a BRCA mutation can be offered earlier and more frequent imaging, risk-reducing medicines or risk-reducing surgery, planned with a genetics service.

    What do the first signs of breast cancer feel like?

    The NHS says the first symptom most women notice is a lump or an area of thickened tissue in the breast. Most breast lumps are not cancer, but the NHS advises having any new lump checked by a doctor.

    Other changes can include a different size or shape of the breast, fluid coming from the nipple, a lump or swelling in the armpit, and changes to the skin or nipple.

    Breast pain is not usually a symptom of breast cancer. Pain linked to the menstrual cycle usually affects both breasts and tends to begin up to two weeks before a period, then eases when the period ends.

    The NHS advises contacting a GP urgently if breast pain comes with a very high temperature, if any part of the breast is red, hot or swollen, or if there is a hard lump that does not move.

    What are the types, grades and stages of breast cancer?

    Ductal and lobular carcinoma are the two most common types of invasive breast cancer. Inflammatory breast cancer is rarer.

    Staging describes how far a cancer has grown and spread. Stage 0 is ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), where the cells are inside the breast ducts and have not spread into the surrounding breast tissue. Stages 1 to 3 describe invasive cancer of increasing size and spread. Stage 4 means the cancer has spread to distant parts of the body.

    Cancer Research UK explains that breast cancer is also graded from 1 to 3. Grade 1 cancers tend to grow more slowly, while grade 3 cancers grow faster and are more likely to spread.

    Breast cancer cells are also usually tested for oestrogen receptors, progesterone receptors and a protein called HER2. These results help to plan treatment.

    What happens at a breast clinic appointment?

    NHS England guidance says people with suspected breast cancer should be seen in a one-stop clinic with same-day access to clinical examination, mammography, ultrasound and biopsy. Combining a clinical examination, imaging and a biopsy is called triple assessment.

    A mammogram is an x-ray of the breast. One London cancer centre describes a mammogram as the usual scan for patients over 40, and another NHS trust says women under 40 have an ultrasound scan if a scan is needed.

    A biopsy takes a small sample of breast cells or tissue with a needle.

    All the results are discussed at a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting. The team includes breast surgeons, specialist nurses and radiologists.

    How is breast cancer treated?

    The NHS says surgery is usually the first type of treatment, although chemotherapy or hormone therapy is sometimes given first. After breast-conserving surgery, which removes the cancer but not the whole breast, people are usually offered radiotherapy to destroy any remaining cancer cells. The NHS describes this combination as being as successful as removing the whole breast for early-stage breast cancer.

    A sentinel lymph node biopsy removes the first lymph nodes in the armpit to check whether cancer has spread there. Chemotherapy may be given before surgery to make the cancer smaller.

    Hormone therapy is used for hormone-sensitive breast cancers and has no effect if the cancer is not sensitive to hormones. In most cases it is taken for five years or more. It may be the only treatment if general health prevents surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

    Cancer Research UK says radiotherapy can cause skin problems that usually start to improve three to four weeks after treatment. Hot flushes are a frequent effect of hormone therapy.

    Living with breast cancer after treatment

    The Royal Marsden says people can feel more tired than usual for several months after treatment ends. NHS Scotland guidance says exercise is safe after breast cancer treatment and can help with fatigue and lymphoedema.

    Lymphoedema is swelling caused by a build-up of fluid. NHS Scotland says it typically begins within the first four years after breast cancer treatment. Macmillan advises having any swelling in the hand, arm, chest or breast checked by the cancer doctor or specialist nurse. People with lymphoedema can be referred to a specialist, who can advise on exercise, massage and skin care.

    Breast Cancer Now says treatment can affect intimacy through pain, discomfort or loss of sensitivity. A hospital follow-up leaflet lists intimacy and relationship issues among the topics that can be discussed at follow-up.

    What is secondary breast cancer?

    Secondary breast cancer, also called metastatic or stage 4 breast cancer, is breast cancer that has spread to another part of the body. The most common places are the bones, liver, lungs and brain.

    Breast Cancer Now says metastatic breast cancer can be treated but cannot be cured. Cancer Research UK says treatment can hold the cancer back for some time and help to relieve symptoms. Breast Cancer Now describes the aims as slowing the spread, controlling the cancer, relieving symptoms and maintaining health and wellbeing. Chemotherapy, hormone therapy and targeted drugs are the usual options.

    Breast Cancer Now says that as treatments have improved, more and more people are living longer after a diagnosis. Supportive care can help with symptoms such as pain and fatigue at any stage, alongside treatment for the cancer.

    Frequently asked questions

    Are most breast lumps cancer?

    The NHS says most breast lumps are not cancer, but it advises having any new lump checked by a doctor. The NHS says the first symptom most women notice is a lump or an area of thickened tissue in the breast.

    Does breast pain mean breast cancer?

    The NHS says breast pain is not usually a symptom of breast cancer. Pain linked to the menstrual cycle usually affects both breasts and eases when a period ends. The NHS advises contacting a GP urgently if pain comes with a very high temperature, or if any part of the breast is red, hot or swollen, or if there is a hard lump that does not move.

    Is surgery always the first treatment?

    The NHS says surgery is usually the first type of treatment for breast cancer, but chemotherapy or hormone therapy will sometimes be the first treatment. Chemotherapy may be given before surgery to make the cancer smaller. Hormone therapy may be the only treatment if a person's general health prevents surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

    Can secondary breast cancer be treated?

    Secondary breast cancer is cancer that has spread to another part of the body. Breast Cancer Now says it can be treated but cannot be cured. Treatment aims to control and slow the spread, relieve symptoms and maintain health and wellbeing. Breast Cancer Now adds that more people are living longer as treatments improve, and that each person's situation is different.

    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.

    Imaging

    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.

    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.
