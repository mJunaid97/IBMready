    Heart attack (myocardial infarction)

    Also known as: heart attack, MI, STEMI, NSTEMI

    Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.

    When to seek care

    Call emergency services immediately for crushing chest pain, pain spreading to the arm or jaw, or chest discomfort with sweating, breathlessness or nausea. Do not drive yourself.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Overview

    Every minute of blockage kills more muscle, so a heart attack is treated as a race to reopen the artery. Survival and the amount of muscle saved depend on how quickly the person calls for help and reaches a hospital able to perform emergency angioplasty.

    Causes

    • Rupture of an atherosclerotic plaque with clot formation
    • Less often: coronary spasm, spontaneous artery dissection, severe anaemia or oxygen lack with a fixed narrowing

    Risk factors

    • Same as coronary artery disease: smoking, cholesterol, blood pressure, diabetes, family history, age
    • Cocaine and amphetamine use
    • Extreme physical or emotional stress

    Symptoms

    Chest painShortness of breathNauseaDizzinessFatigue

    What a clinician may find

    • Pale, sweaty, distressed
    • Fast or slow pulse, low or high blood pressure
    • Crackles in the lungs if the heart is failing

    Complications

    • Cardiac arrest from ventricular fibrillation
    • Heart failure
    • Rupture of the heart wall or a papillary muscle
    • Arrhythmias and heart block
    • Pericarditis

    Diagnosis

    An ECG within ten minutes of arrival shows ST elevation (STEMI) when a large artery is fully blocked; troponin in the blood rises over hours and confirms muscle damage in other cases. Echocardiography shows which wall has stopped moving.

    Treatment

    Aspirin immediately, then emergency angioplasty with a stent to reopen the artery (or clot-dissolving drugs if angioplasty is not reachable within about two hours). Afterwards: a second antiplatelet drug, a statin, a beta blocker and an ACE inhibitor, cardiac rehabilitation and risk factor control.

    Prevention

    The measures that prevent coronary artery disease; after a first attack, lifelong medication and rehabilitation halve the risk of another.

    What are the symptoms of a heart attack?

    The NHS says to call 999 for chest pain that feels tight or squeezing or that spreads to the arms, neck or jaw; other symptoms can include shortness of breath, feeling or being sick, sweating, and pale, blue or grey skin. A heart attack is a medical emergency.

    Not everyone has the classic chest pain. NHS 111 Wales notes that women are more likely to have other symptoms such as shortness of breath, feeling or being sick, and back or jaw pain. Symptoms vary from person to person, and some people, including older adults and people with diabetes, may have milder or atypical symptoms.

    The NHS advises that, while waiting for the ambulance, a person who is not allergic to aspirin may chew and swallow an aspirin tablet.

    What causes a heart attack?

    The NHS says a heart attack happens when the blood supply to part of the heart is suddenly cut off, so that the heart muscle begins to die. Coronary heart disease is the leading cause. In coronary heart disease, fatty plaques build up in the coronary arteries, and a plaque can rupture and trigger a clot that blocks the vessel. A shortage of oxygen in the blood, for example from carbon monoxide poisoning or lung problems, is a less common cause.

    The NHS lists smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, being overweight and a close relative who has had a heart attack among the risk factors. NHS Inform lists age and ethnicity among the risk factors for coronary heart disease.

    A heart attack is a problem with the blood supply to the heart muscle. Cardiac arrest is different: the NHS says that if a large part of the heart is damaged, the heart can stop beating altogether.

    How is a heart attack diagnosed?

    The NHS says the main tests for finding out whether someone is having a heart attack are an electrocardiogram (ECG) and blood tests.

    There are two main types. In a STEMI, the ECG shows a rise in a part of the tracing called the ST segment. In an NSTEMI, the ECG does not show the ST rise seen in a STEMI, and it can look normal or show other changes.

    Troponin, a marker of damage to the heart muscle, is measured in blood tests. Troponin is the preferred blood marker for diagnosing a heart attack, and a raised level with an ECG that lacks STEMI changes points to an NSTEMI. A BHF-funded trial in Edinburgh found that a high-sensitivity troponin test doubled the number of women diagnosed with a heart attack.

    How is a heart attack treated?

    The NHS says the choice of treatment depends on how severe the heart attack is. The NHS lists medicines to treat the blockage, angioplasty with a stent, and coronary artery bypass surgery. In angioplasty, a small plastic tube called a stent is used to open a blocked artery.

    For a STEMI, the usual approach is to offer a coronary angiogram, which images the arteries, followed by primary percutaneous coronary intervention (primary PCI), meaning emergency angioplasty, when symptoms began recently enough for it to help. NICE guideline NG185 covers the early management of acute coronary syndromes.

    The BHF says most people stay in hospital for about two to five days after a heart attack, and for two or three days after coronary angioplasty or stents.

    The NHS describes rupture of the heart's muscle, walls or valves as an extremely serious but relatively uncommon complication of a heart attack.

    What happens after a heart attack?

    People are commonly prescribed medicines after a heart attack, such as antiplatelets, beta blockers and statins, to lower the chance of another one. The BHF says antiplatelet drugs such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor are commonly prescribed for a year, and most people are prescribed aspirin for life. Beta blockers and statins are also common, and a glyceryl trinitrate spray can ease chest pain.

    The NHS says people are offered cardiac rehabilitation, which can be done in person or remotely. The BHF says cardiac rehabilitation usually starts around a month to six weeks after getting home from hospital, and typically covers topics such as driving, returning to work and holidays.

    The NHS says recovery after a heart attack can take several weeks or longer, and hospital guidance says when someone can return to work depends on their job, how severe the heart attack was and how quickly they recover. A GP can provide a fit note if someone is not fit for work.

    Feelings, anxiety and sex after a heart attack

    The BHF says rehabilitation should include support for wellbeing, including an assessment of anxiety or depression, tips for dealing with stress and information on other sources of help.

    Most people can return to having sex after a heart attack, and NHS 111 Wales, citing the BHF, gives about four to six weeks as a usual time.

    Chest pain that feels tight or squeezing, or that spreads to the arms, neck or jaw, is a reason to call 999 immediately, as described at the top of this article.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the difference between a heart attack and cardiac arrest?

    In a heart attack the blood supply to part of the heart muscle is suddenly cut off. In cardiac arrest the heart stops beating. The NHS says that if a large part of the heart is damaged in a heart attack, the heart can stop beating altogether.

    Do women have different heart attack symptoms?

    NHS 111 Wales notes women are more likely to have other symptoms too, such as breathlessness, feeling sick, and back or jaw pain.

    How long do people stay in hospital?

    The BHF says people usually stay in hospital for about two to five days after a heart attack. If they have had coronary angioplasty or stents, the stay is likely to be 2 or 3 days.

    Will I need heart medicines for life?

    The BHF says antiplatelets such as clopidogrel or ticagrelor are commonly prescribed for a year, and most people are prescribed aspirin for life. Beta blockers and statins are also common.

    When can I have sex again after a heart attack?

    Most people can return to having sex after a heart attack, and NHS 111 Wales, citing the BHF, gives about four to six weeks as a usual time.

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.

    Biomarkers

    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Clopidogrel An antiplatelet medicine that stops platelets clumping by blocking their P2Y12 receptor, taken to prevent clots after a heart attack, stent.
    • Ezetimibe Ezetimibe is a prescribed cholesterol-lowering medicine that works by reducing how much cholesterol the gut absorbs.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Antiplatelet drugs Medicines that stop platelets clumping into the clots that block arteries.
    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Cholesterol absorption inhibitors Cholesterol absorption inhibitors are medicines that lower cholesterol by partly blocking its absorption in the small intestine.
    • Nitrates Vasodilators that relieve angina within minutes by relaxing veins and coronary arteries.
    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • PDE5 inhibitors PDE5 inhibitors are medicines that include sildenafil and tadalafil, used mainly for erection problems.
    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
