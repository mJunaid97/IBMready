    Atrial fibrillation

    Also known as: AF, AFib, irregular heartbeat

    A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.

    When to seek care

    Urgent care for palpitations with chest pain, fainting or severe breathlessness, and emergency services for any stroke symptoms. NHLBI advises emergency care when palpitations are accompanied by dizziness, sudden shortness of breath, chest pain or fainting.

    Overview

    In atrial fibrillation the sinoatrial node loses control and hundreds of electrical impulses a minute swirl through the atria; the atrioventricular node lets an irregular selection through. Blood stagnates in the fibrillating left atrium and can clot, which is why AF causes one in five strokes. Many people have no symptoms and the rhythm is found at a routine pulse check or on an ECG done for another reason. When symptoms occur, NHLBI describes fatigue as the commonest, with palpitations felt in the chest, throat or neck, breathlessness on lying down or exercising, chest pain, light-headedness or fainting and low blood pressure. AF is not usually life-threatening in itself, but left untreated it can lead to stroke, heart failure, blood clots, sudden cardiac arrest and cognitive impairment or dementia, which is why detection and treatment matter even when the rhythm is painless.

    Causes

    • High blood pressure
    • Coronary artery disease and heart failure
    • Valve disease, especially mitral
    • Overactive thyroid
    • Alcohol binges
    • Lung disease and sleep apnoea
    • Often no single cause in older people

    Risk factors

    • Age over 65
    • Obesity
    • Diabetes
    • Family history
    • Sleep apnoea
    • Overweight

    Symptoms

    DizzinessShortness of breathFatigueChest pain

    What a clinician may find

    • Irregularly irregular pulse
    • Pulse deficit (heart rate higher than the wrist pulse)
    • Low blood pressure during a fast episode

    Complications

    • Stroke and systemic embolism
    • Heart failure from a persistently fast rate
    • Falls and fainting
    • Sudden cardiac arrest (rare)
    • Cognitive impairment and dementia

    Diagnosis

    An ECG shows no P waves and an irregularly irregular rhythm. The clinician asks about symptoms, lifestyle, other conditions and any family history of AF. Brief, paroxysmal episodes are caught with a Holter monitor or patch recording over 24 hours or longer, or increasingly with a wearable. Echocardiography looks for valve disease and atrial size; blood tests check thyroid function, kidney function, electrolytes and a blood count, with clotting studies if an anticoagulant is to be started. A chest X-ray is often done to look at the heart and lungs.

    Treatment

    Treatment has three goals: preventing clots, controlling the heart rate and, where appropriate, restoring normal rhythm. Rate control uses beta blockers, rate-limiting calcium-channel blockers or digoxin. Rhythm control, with electrical cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs or catheter ablation, is recommended for people who remain symptomatic or are not improving on rate-controlling medicines; surgical approaches are used in selected cases. Stroke prevention with an anticoagulant such as warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant is offered when the stroke risk score (which weighs age, high blood pressure, heart failure, diabetes and previous clots or stroke) justifies it. Underlying problems such as thyroid disease and sleep apnoea are treated, and lifestyle changes include aiming for a healthy weight and limiting alcohol and stimulants that raise the heart rate.

    Prevention

    Control blood pressure, weight, alcohol and sleep apnoea; keep active. Treating the conditions that provoke AF, including thyroid disease and sleep apnoea, and limiting alcohol and stimulants reduce the chance of episodes.

    What does atrial fibrillation feel like?

    The NHS describes atrial fibrillation (AF) as a common heart rhythm problem. It lists palpitations as the most common sign, along with tiredness, breathlessness, dizziness and chest discomfort.

    Sometimes there are no symptoms at all. The NHS says the condition may then be found at a routine check-up or when tests are done for something else. NICE guidance advises a manual pulse check to look for an irregular pulse when AF is suspected.

    NHS inform describes atrial fibrillation as generally not dangerous in itself, and says many people live normal, healthy lives with it. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) says there is no cure, but people with AF can lead healthy lives.

    What are the different types of atrial fibrillation?

    NHS inform sorts atrial fibrillation into types by how long episodes last. Paroxysmal, or intermittent, AF comes and goes, with episodes lasting from minutes to hours. Persistent AF is when episodes last for more than seven days. Long-term persistent AF is continuous for over a year.

    The BHF describes permanent AF as continuous, and says people with it may always have symptoms.

    NHS inform adds that some people with atrial fibrillation will still need treatment, or be advised to have it, even though the condition is generally not dangerous in itself.

    Some people have episodes that come and go. The NHS says a portable ECG recorder can help diagnose paroxysmal AF, because it can be worn for 24 hours or longer.

    How is atrial fibrillation diagnosed?

    The NHS advises seeing a GP if a person thinks they may have symptoms of atrial fibrillation. It also advises contacting the GP if palpitations keep happening or get worse, or if treatment is not controlling the symptoms. A GP may refer a person to a cardiologist, a heart specialist, who checks the heart rate and symptoms.

    NICE guidance recommends a manual pulse check to look for an irregular pulse when AF is suspected. It says a 12-lead ECG, a recording of the heart's electrical activity, should be used to confirm the diagnosis. The NHS says an echocardiogram, a chest X-ray or blood tests may also be arranged.

    NICE says that when paroxysmal AF is suspected but not found on an ECG, an ambulatory ECG monitor is used, and the choice of monitor depends on how far apart the episodes are.

    What treatments are there for atrial fibrillation?

    The NHS says there is currently no cure for atrial fibrillation, but treatment should help manage the symptoms and lower the risk of complications such as blood clots, stroke and heart failure. NHLBI says treatment uses lifestyle changes, medicines and procedures, including surgery, to help prevent blood clots, slow the heartbeat or restore the heart's normal rhythm.

    NHS sources list beta blockers and anti-arrhythmic drugs among the medicines for controlling heart rate and rhythm. Anticoagulants are blood-thinning medicines. An NHS trust leaflet says they do not treat the symptoms of AF but do reduce the risk of stroke. Local NHS guidance lists direct-acting oral anticoagulants first, with warfarin for people who cannot take them.

    NHS inform lists cardioversion, ablation and a pacemaker among the other treatments. NICE says catheter ablation is usually reserved for people in whom antiarrhythmic drugs have failed. NHLBI describes rhythm control as recommended for people who continue to have symptoms or are not improving on medicines that control heart rate.

    How can people live with atrial fibrillation?

    The BHF says working out what triggers episodes is an important part of self-management. NHS 111 Wales lists caffeine, spicy food, stress, dehydration and lack of sleep as possible triggers.

    The BHF says people with permanent AF can generally exercise if they feel well, while people with intermittent AF are advised to exercise only when the heart is in a normal rhythm.

    NHLBI says combining weight loss with physical activity and managing other risk factors, such as high blood pressure, diabetes, alcohol use and smoking, can improve symptoms more than weight loss alone. NHLBI suggests discussing activity levels, weight control and alcohol use with a provider. It also notes that blood thinners carry risks, including the possibility of severe bleeding in the brain.

    Is atrial fibrillation serious, and when is it an emergency?

    NHLBI says atrial fibrillation that goes undiagnosed or is left untreated can cause serious and even life-threatening complications, including stroke and heart failure. It also lists blood clots, cognitive decline and dementia. The Stroke Association says AF raises the risk of stroke, and that strokes linked to AF tend to cause more brain damage. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) says AFib causes about 1 in 7 strokes in the United States.

    The NHS says to call 999 for a fast or irregular heartbeat together with chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting or dizziness, or stroke-like signs such as weakness on one side, confusion or difficulty speaking. It advises against driving yourself to A&E. The Stroke Association says to call 999 straight away for any sign of stroke. For symptoms that are not an emergency, the NHS advises seeing a GP.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can atrial fibrillation be cured?

    The NHS says there is currently no cure for atrial fibrillation. Treatment should help manage the symptoms and lower the risk of complications. NHLBI says treatment aims to prevent blood clots, slow the heartbeat or restore the heart's normal rhythm. The options include lifestyle changes, medicines and procedures, such as cardioversion or ablation.

    Is atrial fibrillation dangerous?

    NHS inform describes atrial fibrillation as generally not dangerous in itself, and says many people live normal, healthy lives with it. However, NHLBI says it can cause serious complications if it is undiagnosed or left untreated. The Stroke Association says it raises the risk of stroke.

    Can you have atrial fibrillation without symptoms?

    Yes. The NHS says sometimes there are no symptoms. The condition may then be found at a routine check-up or when tests are done for something else. NICE guidance advises a manual pulse check to look for an irregular pulse when AF is suspected, and a 12-lead ECG is used to confirm the diagnosis.

    What is the difference between paroxysmal and permanent AF?

    NHS inform groups atrial fibrillation by how long episodes last. Paroxysmal AF comes and goes, with episodes lasting from minutes to hours. The BHF describes permanent AF as continuous, with people possibly always having symptoms. Persistent AF lasts for more than seven days, and long-term persistent AF is continuous for over a year.

    What can people do to help with atrial fibrillation day to day?

    NHLBI says combining weight loss with physical activity and managing risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes, alcohol use and smoking can improve symptoms more than weight loss alone. The BHF says working out what triggers episodes is an important part of self-management.

    When should someone call 999 about a fast or irregular heartbeat?

    The NHS says to call 999 for a fast or irregular heartbeat together with chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting or dizziness, or stroke-like signs. It advises against driving yourself to A&E. For palpitations that keep happening or get worse, or if treatment is not controlling symptoms, the NHS advises seeing a GP.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.

    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • INR The prothrombin time expressed as a standardised ratio, the number used to dose warfarin and to gauge the liver's production of clotting factors.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.

    • Warfarin The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
