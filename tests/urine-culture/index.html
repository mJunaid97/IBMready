    Urine culture

    Also known as: midstream urine, urine microscopy, culture and sensitivity

    A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them. It turns a dipstick suspicion into a proven infection with a targeted treatment. The laboratory first examines the urine under the microscope for white blood cells and bacteria, then plates a measured volume on nutrient agar and incubates it overnight; the colonies that grow are counted, identified and exposed to a panel of antibiotics (microscopy, culture and sensitivity, MC&S). Because the urethra and surrounding skin carry their own bacteria, how the sample is collected matters as much as what the laboratory does with it: a clean-catch midstream sample reduces contamination, and a catheter or, rarely, a needle sample through the lower abdomen is used when a clean sample cannot be obtained. Culture is the reference standard for diagnosing urinary tract infection, but it is often unnecessary when the diagnosis is already clear, and in people without symptoms a positive culture usually should not be treated.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Colony count100,000 or more colony-forming units per mL of a single organism is significant; lower counts count in symptomatic patients and catheter samples. The count is estimated from the number of colonies that grow from the measured volume plated; a low count of a single organism in a symptomatic person can still be a true infection.
    OrganismE. coli causes most infections; Proteus suggests stones; Staphylococcus saprophyticus in young women; mixed growth usually means contamination. Yeasts are also reported and interpreted with symptoms and risk factors.
    SensitivitiesWhich antibiotics the organism is sensitive or resistant to, so that empirical treatment can be confirmed, narrowed or changed.
    MicroscopyWhite cells and bacteria seen under the microscope support infection and decide whether the sample goes on to culture.

    Why it is ordered

    • Infection in men, children, pregnancy and anyone with a catheter or kidney disease
    • Infections that keep coming back or fail treatment
    • Suspected kidney infection or sepsis from a urinary source
    • Before urological surgery
    • Symptoms of cystitis with a dipstick result suggesting infection, when the result will change treatment
    • Confirming clearance after treatment in pregnancy or before a urological procedure

    How it is done

    Midstream sample in a sterile pot (or from a fresh catheter); refrigerate if it cannot reach the lab within hours. For a clean-catch sample the area is cleaned, the first part of the stream is passed into the toilet and the middle portion is caught in the sterile container without touching its inside; kits for collection at home are available. A catheter sample is taken from a freshly placed or freshly changed catheter, and the person may be asked not to pass urine for an hour or so beforehand (or to drink water) so that enough urine is present. Culture results take one to two days; microscopy can be reported sooner. A repeat culture is sometimes taken after treatment to confirm the bacteria have cleared, particularly in pregnancy or before urological procedures.

    Preparation. Collect before starting antibiotics whenever possible. The laboratory form records symptoms, pregnancy, catheter status and any antibiotics already taken, because these change how the colony count is interpreted.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Pure growth of E. coli, sensitive to nitrofurantoinConfirmed infection; treat with the narrowest effective antibiotic
    No growthNo bacterial infection detected; if symptoms persist, consider antibiotics taken before sampling, organisms that do not grow on standard culture, or a non-infective cause of the symptoms
    Mixed growthProbably contaminated; repeat with careful collection
    Growth without symptomsAsymptomatic bacteriuria: treat only in pregnancy or before urological procedures
    Resistant organismThe antibiotic already started is changed to one the organism is sensitive to; recurrent resistant infections prompt a review of the urinary tract and of antibiotic use
    Yeast grownReported alongside bacteria; its significance is judged from symptoms and risk factors rather than from the culture alone

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Results take two days, so treatment often starts empirically. Antibiotics taken beforehand can sterilise the sample. Contamination is common, especially in primary care where a large share of samples grow mixed skin or genital flora, which wastes a culture and can lead to treatment of a non-existent infection. Standard culture does not detect anaerobes, mycobacteria or some fastidious organisms, and yeasts may grow but are not always reliably detected, and in a large proportion of people with asymptomatic bacteriuria, including older adults and people with catheters, a positive culture is not an infection to treat. Culture confirms bacteria in the urine but not where in the urinary tract the infection sits or whether it has reached the kidneys or the bloodstream; that needs the clinical picture, blood tests and sometimes imaging.

    Conditions

    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) A kidney infection, also called pyelonephritis, is a urinary tract infection that affects one or both kidneys.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Procedures

    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Guides and news

    • Blood in urine: causes and what happens next Blood in the urine is not usually serious, but the NHS advises seeing a GP every time, even if the amount is small.
    • UTI and cystitis: what they are A urinary tract infection (UTI) can affect the bladder, urethra or kidneys; cystitis is a UTI of the bladder, and antibiotics are not always needed.
