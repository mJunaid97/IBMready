    Blood glucose

    Also known as: blood sugar, plasma glucose

    The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick. Fasting, random and after-a-sugar-drink (oral glucose tolerance) versions answer different questions.

    Open in the 3D explorerPancreas anatomy
    Kind
    Laboratory test
    Categories
    Diabetes & glucose, Chemistry & metabolic
    Specimen
    Serum or plasma, Capillary (fingerprick) blood
    Method
    Chemistry analyser, Point-of-care device
    Terminology
    LOINC 2345-7, 1558-6

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Fasting glucoseAfter at least 8 hours without food. A fasting level of 7.0 mmol/L or above indicates diabetes and, without symptoms, is confirmed by a second test on another day. Normal fasting values differ slightly between countries and laboratories, so the report gives the range used.
    Random glucoseAny time of day; 11.1 mmol/L or above with symptoms suggests diabetes.
    Oral glucose toleranceGlucose two hours after a 75 g drink; used in pregnancy and borderline cases.

    Why it is ordered

    • Thirst, passing lots of urine, weight loss
    • Screening for diabetes in people at risk
    • Confusion, sweating or collapse (hypoglycaemia)
    • Monitoring insulin and other diabetes treatment

    How it is done

    Venous sample in a fluoride tube, or a capillary fingerprick on a meter; continuous glucose monitors sample fluid under the skin.

    Preparation. Fasting tests need at least 8 hours without food, water allowed.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Fasting 6.1-6.9 mmol/LImpaired fasting glucose on the World Health Organization definition, a form of prediabetes. NICE flags a fasting level of 5.5 to 6.9 mmol/L as high risk of type 2 diabetes, and US sources start at 5.6, so the lower limit depends on the guideline used
    Fasting 7.0 or aboveDiabetes if confirmed on a second test, or if symptoms are present
    Below 4.0 mmol/LHypoglycaemia in people with diabetes, usually from insulin or a sulfonylurea; mild episodes are treated with fast-acting carbohydrate as set out in the care plan, and confusion, drowsiness or a seizure is an emergency (call 999)

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    A single value is a snapshot; HbA1c gives the three-month average. Stress and illness raise glucose temporarily.

    Conditions

    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Gestational diabetes Gestational diabetes is a form of diabetes that starts during pregnancy and typically clears up after the baby is born.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.

    Biomarkers

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.

    Procedures

    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.

    Medications

    • Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin is a prescription tablet from the SGLT2 inhibitor group, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Empagliflozin Empagliflozin is a prescription medicine used to treat type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Fluoxetine Fluoxetine is an SSRI antidepressant, available only on prescription, that the NHS says is used for depression.
    • Gliclazide Gliclazide is a sulfonylurea tablet prescribed for type 2 diabetes that prompts the pancreas to make more insulin.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) Semaglutide is a medicine used to manage type 2 diabetes (as Ozempic and Rybelsus) or to treat obesity (as Wegovy).

    Guides and news

    • Preparing for a blood test: fasting explained Some blood tests need fasting or a change to what you eat, drink or take beforehand; the person who arranges the test should say which applies.
    • Dizziness and vertigo: causes and warning signs Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure.
    • Eating well with type 2 diabetes Diabetes UK says there is no special diet only for type 2 diabetes, and NICE advises the same healthy balanced eating as for everyone.
    • HbA1c test: what it shows and why it matters The HbA1c test shows average blood glucose over the past two to three months and is used to diagnose and monitor diabetes.
    • Metformin: what to expect when starting The NHS describes metformin as usually the first medicine for type 2 diabetes.
    • Numbness and tingling: causes and when to act Pins and needles are usually brief and harmless, but numbness or tingling that lasts, keeps returning or comes with weakness needs checking.
    • Type 1 and type 2 diabetes compared Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition treated with insulin; type 2 develops when the body makes too little insulin or it works poorly.
