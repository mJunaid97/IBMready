    ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate)

    Also known as: sed rate, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, sedimentation rate

    An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour. Inflammatory proteins such as fibrinogen and immunoglobulins make red cells stack and sink faster, so the ESR rises with inflammation, infection and some blood disorders, more slowly and less specifically than CRP. The mechanism is physical rather than chemical: changes in the composition of plasma reduce the electrical charge that normally keeps red cells apart, so they aggregate into stacks (rouleaux) that are heavier than single cells and fall faster through the plasma. The classical Westergren method, in which anticoagulated blood stands in an upright graduated tube, remains the reference standard, and modern automated analysers are calibrated against it. The ESR is a non-specific indicator: it shows that inflammation is probably present and how it is changing over weeks, but it cannot say which disease is responsible, so it is always read alongside the history, examination, CRP and other tests.

    Why is it used?

    • Suspected temporal (giant cell) arteritis and polymyalgia rheumatica, where it is part of the diagnostic picture
    • Monitoring rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and other long-term inflammatory diseases
    • Suspected myeloma and other conditions that raise immunoglobulins
    • Unexplained symptoms where inflammation is one possibility, alongside CRP
    • Monitoring vasculitis and other long-term inflammatory conditions over months, alongside CRP and symptoms

    What does it measure?

    ComponentWhat it measuresA higher value may be seen inA lower value may be seen in
    Sedimentation rate ESRRed-cell settling distance in one hourInflammation, infection, anaemia, pregnancy, myeloma, older age, kidney diseasePolycythaemia, sickle cell disease, very high white-cell counts (low or falsely low)

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A venous blood sample in an anticoagulant tube, tested within a few hours of collection because standing blood changes the result. In the Westergren method the blood is drawn up a long, narrow graduated tube that is left upright and undisturbed for one hour, and the height of clear plasma above the settled red cells is read in millimetres. Most laboratories now use automated analysers that reproduce the method on smaller volumes and report in the same units; these are validated against the manual Westergren test. The tube must be vertical and undisturbed, because tilting or vibration speeds settling and falsely raises the value.

    Specimen. Venous blood in an EDTA or citrate tube

    Do I need to prepare?

    None.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    Markedly raised ESR (over 100 mm/h)Giant cell arteritis, myeloma, serious infection or widespread cancer are among the causes considered
    Raised ESR with raised CRPActive inflammation or infection; the CRP will have risen first and will fall first as the problem settles
    Raised ESR with normal CRPAnaemia, pregnancy, kidney disease, lupus, myeloma or simply older age
    Normal ESRMakes active inflammatory disease less likely but does not exclude it; the ESR is slow to rise and can be held down by red-cell abnormalities
    ESR falling over weeks of treatmentThe inflammatory disease is coming under control; used to track rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis and other long-term conditions alongside symptoms
    Unexpectedly low ESRPolycythaemia, abnormally shaped red cells such as sickle cells, or a very high white-cell count interfere with settling and can mask inflammation
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. Reference limits depend on age and sex and rise in older adults; laboratories publish their own. No ESR value diagnoses a disease on its own.

    What can affect the result?

    • Anaemia and red-cell shape (sickle cells settle slowly)
    • Pregnancy, age and sex
    • Time between sampling and testing
    • High immunoglobulin or fibrinogen levels from any cause
    • Tube angle, temperature and vibration during the test (technical factors that falsely raise or lower the value)

    Medicines. Corticosteroids and anti-inflammatory biologics lower it; oral contraceptives and some intravenous immunoglobulin preparations raise it.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • What is causing the inflammation
    • How recently the inflammation began (it lags by days)
    • Whether a normal result excludes disease

    The ESR is influenced by many things other than inflammation, including anaemia, red-cell shape, pregnancy, age, sex and immunoglobulin levels, and it changes slowly, lagging days behind the onset of inflammation and weeks behind its resolution. It is not specific to any disease: it indicates that an inflammatory process is probably present but cannot identify the cause, so an isolated raised ESR in a well person is a prompt for a careful history and examination rather than a diagnosis. Technical factors such as tube angle, temperature and delay before testing alter the result. CRP is preferred for tracking acute infection because it rises and falls faster; the ESR keeps a place in a few conditions and in long-term monitoring.

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate).

    Conditions

    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) A long-term digestive condition with repeated abdominal pain and changes in bowel habit, but no visible damage or disease in the digestive tract.

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.

    Guides and news

    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.
