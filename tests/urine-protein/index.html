    Urine protein

    Also known as: albumin:creatinine ratio, protein:creatinine ratio, microalbumin, proteinuria test

    Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein. Measuring albumin leaking into urine, corrected for how dilute the sample is, detects kidney damage years before blood tests change, particularly in diabetes and high blood pressure. Albumin is the main protein in blood, and healthy kidneys let only a trace of it through. Because early kidney disease usually causes no symptoms, the test is used above all as a screen in people at higher risk: those with diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease or a family history of kidney disease, and smokers and people with obesity. UK guidance recommends the albumin:creatinine ratio in preference to other tests of proteinuria, and reads it alongside the eGFR blood test: the two measure different things, kidney damage and kidney filtration, and together they diagnose and classify chronic kidney disease. A raised ratio matters at any eGFR, because albumin in the urine is an independent marker of risk both for progression to kidney failure and for cardiovascular disease.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR)The amount of albumin in a single urine sample divided by the creatinine in the same sample. Creatinine leaves the body at a fairly steady rate, so dividing by it corrects for how concentrated or dilute the urine is, which depends on how much the person has drunk. Below 3 mg/mmol normal; 3-30 moderately raised (formerly microalbuminuria); above 30 severe. Combined with eGFR to stage kidney disease, and used to follow how well treatment is protecting the kidneys.
    Protein:creatinine ratioAll proteins, not just albumin, used when large amounts leak (nephrotic range above 300 mg/mmol) and when non-albumin proteins are suspected; the ACR is the more sensitive test for the small leaks of early disease.
    24-hour collectionThe old gold standard; a random or early-morning ACR performs as well and is far more convenient, so timed collections are now rarely needed.
    Dipstick proteinA quick bedside screen that detects only larger amounts of protein; it is not sensitive enough to pick up the moderately raised albumin of early kidney damage, so a negative dipstick does not replace a laboratory ACR in people at risk.

    Why it is ordered

    • Yearly in diabetes
    • High blood pressure, kidney disease, lupus
    • Frothy urine or unexplained swelling
    • Pregnancy with high blood pressure (pre-eclampsia)
    • Staging chronic kidney disease and deciding on ACE inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors
    • Heart disease, a family history of kidney disease, smoking or obesity, where early kidney damage is more likely
    • Monitoring known kidney disease and checking whether treatment is reducing the leak
    • Confirming or excluding kidney damage when the eGFR is reduced, or detecting it when the eGFR is still normal

    How it is done

    A single urine sample is passed into a clean container; the first sample can be collected at any time of day, but an early-morning sample is preferred to confirm a raised result because it is less affected by the day's activity and fluid intake. The laboratory measures albumin, usually by immunoassay, and creatinine on a chemistry analyser, and reports the ratio as a single number. No blood test is needed for the ratio itself, though it is usually requested together with creatinine and eGFR. Semi-quantitative point-of-care devices exist for settings where laboratory testing is hard to access, at the cost of precision.

    Preparation. Avoid hard exercise the day before; repeat if there is infection or fever. Heavy exertion, fever and infection can raise albumin temporarily, so a raised result is usually repeated, ideally on an early-morning specimen, to show it persists and is not explained by something else.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    ACR persistently 3 or moreKidney damage: the treating team uses the ACR together with blood pressure and diabetes status to decide on kidney-protecting medicines
    Nephrotic-range protein with low albumin and swellingNephrotic syndrome, needs a kidney biopsy in adults
    Protein with bloodGlomerulonephritis; urgent specialist referral
    Raised ACR with a normal eGFREarly kidney damage can be present while filtration is still normal; if the leak persists on repeat testing it is itself enough to diagnose chronic kidney disease, and it raises cardiovascular risk
    Reduced eGFR with a normal ACRKidney disease without albumin leak; the two results are classified together, and risk rises as either worsens
    A single raised resultNot diagnostic on its own: repeated to exclude exercise, fever, infection or other temporary causes, and chronic kidney disease is diagnosed only when the abnormality persists over months
    Negative dipstick in someone at riskDoes not exclude moderately raised albumin; a laboratory ACR is still needed
    Higher ACR categoryThe more albumin leaks, the greater the risk of progression to kidney failure and of cardiovascular events, independently of eGFR

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Exercise, fever, infection and standing for long periods raise protein transiently; two out of three samples must be positive to confirm. The ratio shows that the kidney is leaking albumin but not why, so the cause is sought from the history, blood pressure, glucose, blood tests, imaging and sometimes a biopsy. It measures albumin only, so a protein:creatinine ratio is needed when other proteins may be leaking. A dipstick is not a substitute for the laboratory ratio because it misses small leaks. Laboratories in different countries report the ratio in different units, so a result is read against the units and reference information of the laboratory that produced it.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Lupus A long-term autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks healthy tissue, most often causing joint aches, skin rashes and tiredness.
    • Pre-eclampsia Pre-eclampsia is a condition that can develop in pregnancy, with high blood pressure and protein in the urine as its early signs.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.

    Guides and news

    • eGFR and creatinine: kidney test results Creatinine and eGFR show how well the kidneys filter blood, and kidney disease is confirmed only when abnormal results persist.
