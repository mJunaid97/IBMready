    TSH test

    Also known as: thyroid-stimulating hormone test, thyrotropin test, thyroid screening test

    The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is overactive, often before the thyroid hormones themselves leave their reference range. It is the first-line screening test and the value used to adjust levothyroxine.

    Why is it used?

    • Tiredness, weight change, feeling cold or hot, palpitations, anxiety, constipation or low mood
    • A goitre, neck lump or family history of thyroid disease
    • Atrial fibrillation, high cholesterol, depression, infertility or menstrual change
    • Monitoring levothyroxine (6-8 weeks after a dose change, then yearly) or antithyroid drugs
    • Before and during pregnancy in women with thyroid disease; on amiodarone or lithium

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A venous blood sample at any time of day; people on levothyroxine usually take the dose after the sample.

    Specimen. Venous blood (serum)

    Do I need to prepare?

    None; tell the laboratory about biotin supplements, which can interfere with the assay and are paused for two days before the test.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    High TSH with low free T4Primary hypothyroidism (the thyroid itself is failing)
    High TSH with normal free T4Subclinical hypothyroidism: often repeated after three months; treatment considered if TSH stays above 10 mU/L or with symptoms, pregnancy or planning pregnancy
    Low TSH with high free T4 or T3Hyperthyroidism: Graves disease, toxic nodules or thyroiditis
    Low TSH with low free T4Pituitary (secondary) hypothyroidism, severe non-thyroidal illness or recent over-treatment; TSH alone cannot be trusted here
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. The TSH reference interval is set by the laboratory and its assay and widens with age; trimester-specific intervals apply in pregnancy. Treatment decisions in subclinical hypothyroidism use a guideline threshold, not the reference limit.

    What can affect the result?

    • Time of day (TSH peaks overnight) and acute illness
    • Biotin supplements and some antibodies interfere with immunoassays
    • Levothyroxine timing, adherence and absorption (iron, calcium, proton pump inhibitors reduce absorption)
    • Pregnancy, older age, amiodarone, lithium, high-dose steroids and dopamine

    Medicines. Levothyroxine and antithyroid drugs are the intended influences; amiodarone, lithium, interferon and immune checkpoint inhibitors can cause thyroid dysfunction; biotin interferes with the assay itself.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • The cause of an over- or underactive thyroid (antibodies, scans or ultrasound are needed)
    • Thyroid status when the pituitary itself is diseased
    • Whether a nodule or goitre is present

    TSH is unreliable when the pituitary is diseased, during and shortly after severe illness ('sick euthyroid'), in the first trimester of pregnancy and with some medicines; it takes about six weeks to settle after a levothyroxine dose change, so testing sooner misleads. A normal TSH does not exclude thyroid problems that involve antibodies or nodules.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.
    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Guides and news

    • Levothyroxine: timing and other medicines The NHS says levothyroxine replaces a missing thyroid hormone and is taken at the same time each day on an empty stomach.
    • Thyroid blood tests: TSH and T4 explained Thyroid tests read TSH and free T4 together, because free T4 helps to interpret an abnormal TSH.
    • Underactive and overactive thyroid An underactive thyroid does not make enough thyroid hormone, while an overactive thyroid makes too much; the symptoms, causes and treatments differ.
    Educational content. Reference ranges differ between laboratories, methods, ages and sexes, and results are always read against the person, the question asked and other findings. This page cannot interpret an individual result.
