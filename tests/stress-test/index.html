    Cardiac stress test

    Also known as: exercise ECG, exercise tolerance test, treadmill test, stress echo, myocardial perfusion scan

    The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity, images or blood flow are recorded. A narrowed coronary artery that copes at rest fails under load, revealing itself as ECG change, a segment of muscle that stops moving, or a patch that does not take up tracer.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    ECG changes with exerciseST depression, arrhythmia or a fall in blood pressure signal ischaemia.
    Exercise capacityMinutes achieved and heart rate reached; a strong predictor of prognosis in itself.
    Stress echo wall motionA segment that moves at rest but not under stress marks a significant narrowing.
    Perfusion (nuclear or MRI)Reduced tracer uptake under stress that recovers at rest = reversible ischaemia; fixed defect = scar.

    Why it is ordered

    • Chest pain on exertion with an intermediate probability of coronary disease
    • Assessing how much a known narrowing matters
    • After a heart attack or angioplasty, before cardiac rehabilitation
    • Exercise-induced palpitations or blackouts

    How it is done

    Electrodes and blood pressure cuff on; the treadmill speeds up every three minutes until target heart rate, symptoms or ECG change. Imaging versions add ultrasound or a radioactive tracer.

    Preparation. Light meal only; comfortable shoes; some heart drugs (beta blockers) are paused on instruction.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Marked ST depression early in exerciseHigh-risk coronary disease; proceed to angiography
    Good exercise capacity with no changesLow risk; significant disease unlikely
    Reversible perfusion defectViable muscle with restricted supply, likely to benefit from revascularisation

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Exercise ECG alone misses a fifth of disease and gives false positives, especially in women and with abnormal resting ECGs; CT coronary angiography is now the first test in many guidelines.

    Educational content. Reference ranges differ between laboratories, methods, ages and sexes, and results are always read against the person, the question asked and other findings. This page cannot interpret an individual result.
