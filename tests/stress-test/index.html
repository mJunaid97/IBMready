    Cardiac stress test

    Also known as: exercise ECG, exercise tolerance test, treadmill test, cardiac stress test

    The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity, images or blood flow are recorded. A narrowed coronary artery that copes at rest fails under load, revealing itself as ECG change, a segment of muscle that stops moving, or a patch that does not take up tracer. The exercise ECG (exercise tolerance test) is the simplest version: it shows whether the heart muscle receives enough blood through the coronary arteries during physical activity and is used to investigate chest pain, palpitations, dizziness and breathlessness on exertion, to judge prognosis after a heart attack or angioplasty and to set a safe exercise level for cardiac rehabilitation. When a person cannot exercise adequately, a drug such as dobutamine or adenosine stresses the heart instead, and imaging with echocardiography, a radioactive tracer or MRI is added when a more accurate answer is needed than the ECG alone can give.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    ECG changes with exerciseST depression, arrhythmia or a fall in blood pressure signal ischaemia. The ECG is watched continuously during exercise and for a recovery period afterwards, because changes can appear only as the heart rate comes down.
    Exercise capacityMinutes achieved and heart rate reached; a strong predictor of prognosis in itself. The test aims for a target heart rate calculated from age, and whether that target is reached determines how much a negative test can be trusted.
    Symptoms and blood pressure responseChest pain, breathlessness, leg fatigue and the way blood pressure rises or falls with increasing work are recorded at each stage; a falling blood pressure with exertion is a warning sign.
    Stress echo wall motionA segment that moves at rest but not under stress marks a significant narrowing.
    Perfusion (nuclear or MRI)Reduced tracer uptake under stress that recovers at rest = reversible ischaemia; fixed defect = scar.

    Why it is ordered

    • Chest pain on exertion with an intermediate probability of coronary disease
    • Assessing how much a known narrowing matters
    • After a heart attack or angioplasty, before cardiac rehabilitation
    • Exercise-induced palpitations or blackouts
    • Setting a safe exercise level during cardiac rehabilitation after a heart attack or heart surgery
    • Checking blood flow to the heart after coronary angioplasty

    How it is done

    Electrodes and blood pressure cuff on; the treadmill speeds up every three minutes until target heart rate, symptoms or ECG change. Imaging versions add ultrasound or a radioactive tracer. Electrodes are attached to the chest and shoulders and a resting ECG and blood pressure are taken first. The person then walks on the treadmill or pedals a static bicycle while the speed, incline or pedal resistance increases in stages; the blood pressure cuff inflates every few minutes and feels tight. Staff stop the test when the target heart rate is reached, if chest pain, worrying blood pressure changes or ECG signs of the heart muscle lacking oxygen appear, or if the person is too tired or has symptoms such as leg pain. The exercise itself usually lasts around a quarter of an hour; monitoring continues during recovery until the heart rate has returned towards baseline, and the whole appointment takes about an hour. The test is supervised by trained staff, and serious complications are rare, the most frequent being transient extra beats.

    Preparation. Light meal only; comfortable shoes; some heart drugs (beta blockers) are paused on instruction. Loose clothing and trainers are worn, a heavy meal and caffeine are avoided for a few hours beforehand, and the person brings a list of their medicines; beta blockers may be stopped for a day or two before the test only if the doctor has said so, because they blunt the heart-rate response. Anyone who has taken a medicine for erectile dysfunction (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) in the previous day or two tells the team beforehand.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Marked ST depression early in exerciseHigh-risk coronary disease; proceed to angiography
    Good exercise capacity with no changesLow risk; significant disease unlikely
    Test stopped before target heart rate without ECG changeInconclusive rather than negative; a pharmacological or imaging stress test, or CT coronary angiography, is considered
    Symptoms reproduced with ECG changeSupports angina as the cause of the person's exertional symptoms and guides anti-anginal treatment and further imaging
    Fall in blood pressure or sustained arrhythmia with exerciseA high-risk response; the test is stopped and early specialist assessment follows
    Reversible perfusion defectViable muscle with restricted supply, likely to benefit from revascularisation

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Exercise ECG alone misses a fifth of disease and gives false positives, especially in women and with abnormal resting ECGs; CT coronary angiography is now the first test in many guidelines. Its sensitivity and specificity are only moderate, so it works best in people with an intermediate likelihood of coronary disease: in those at low risk a positive result is more often false than true, and in those at high risk a negative result does not exclude disease. People who cannot walk or cycle hard enough to reach the target heart rate, because of arthritis, lung disease, frailty or beta blockers, produce inconclusive tests and need pharmacological stress instead. Heart size and abnormalities on the resting ECG also reduce the accuracy of the ECG changes. The test shows the functional consequence of narrowing, not the anatomy of the arteries, which needs CT or invasive angiography.

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
