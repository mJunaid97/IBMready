    Ambulatory ECG monitoring

    Also known as: 24-hour ECG, ambulatory ECG, event recorder, loop recorder, patch monitor

    A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during normal life, catching the intermittent arrhythmias a ten-second ECG misses. Holter and event monitors are small, battery-powered electrocardiogram devices that record the heart's electrical activity continuously, or when triggered, while the person goes about their usual activities. The recording is used to diagnose an arrhythmia (an irregular heartbeat), to match symptoms such as palpitations, dizziness or blackouts to what the heart was doing at that moment, and to check whether treatment for a rhythm problem is working. A Holter monitor records every beat for one or more days; an event monitor is worn for longer and records when the wearer presses a button or when the device detects an abnormal rhythm; a loop recorder keeps overwriting its memory and saves the stretch around an event; and an implantable loop recorder sits under the skin of the chest for long-term monitoring.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Rhythm over timeEvery beat is recorded and analysed; runs of atrial fibrillation, pauses and extra beats are counted and timed, and the report tabulates how often each abnormality occurred over the whole recording.
    Symptom correlationThe wearer presses a button or keeps a diary of activities and symptoms so that each symptom can be matched to the tracing at that moment; a symptom with a normal tracing is as informative as one with an arrhythmia.
    Heart rate rangeSlowest and fastest rates, including during sleep and exercise, and how quickly the rate responds to activity.
    Treatment responseWhether a rate-controlling drug, an ablation or a pacemaker is keeping the rhythm as intended, judged by how much arrhythmia remains on the recording.

    Why it is ordered

    • Palpitations that come and go
    • Unexplained blackouts or falls
    • Looking for atrial fibrillation after a stroke
    • Checking a pacemaker or drug treatment is controlling a rhythm
    • Suspected heart block
    • Matching intermittent symptoms such as dizziness or breathlessness to the heart rhythm at that moment
    • Measuring how much of the day a known arrhythmia is present (its burden) before and after treatment

    How it is done

    Three to five chest electrodes lead to a small recorder on a belt, or a single adhesive patch; normal activities continue except bathing. Small sticky electrode patches are placed on the chest and connected by thin wires to a battery-powered recorder worn in a pocket or in a pouch around the neck or waist. The wearer keeps a diary of what they were doing and how they felt, and presses the event button when symptoms occur. At the end of the period the device is returned to the clinic, where clinical staff analyse the recording, tabulate the rhythm findings and correlate any irregular activity with the diary entries. Event monitors and patch monitors are worn for days to weeks and send or store recordings when triggered. Implantable loop recorders are injected under the skin of the chest.

    Preparation. None; keep a symptom diary of activities and how the person felt, and keep the recorder dry.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Palpitations coincide with atrial fibrillationDiagnosis made; consider anticoagulation and rate or rhythm control
    Pause of more than 3 seconds with dizzinessSymptomatic bradycardia; likely pacemaker
    Symptoms with normal rhythmPalpitations are not due to an arrhythmia; reassuring
    Arrhythmia without symptomsSilent atrial fibrillation or frequent extra beats found incidentally; significance depends on the burden and the person's other risks, and after a stroke it changes treatment
    No symptoms and no arrhythmia during the recordingNon-diagnostic rather than normal; if symptoms are infrequent a longer event or patch monitor, or an implantable recorder, is considered
    Arrhythmia persisting on treatmentThe drug, ablation or device is not controlling the rhythm as intended; treatment is adjusted

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Only useful if symptoms occur during the recording; rare events need longer monitoring or an implanted device. A one- or two-day Holter recording detects only a small proportion of the intermittent atrial fibrillation that longer monitoring eventually finds, which is why extended event, patch or implantable monitoring is used after an unexplained stroke. With a conventional Holter recorder the analysis is retrospective, so the device itself does not warn the wearer or the clinic of a dangerous rhythm as it happens. Electrodes can irritate the skin and come loose, movement produces artefact that mimics arrhythmia, and the recorder must be kept dry, which limits bathing and swimming. The recording shows rhythm only: it does not measure blood pressure, oxygen or how the heart muscle is pumping.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Symptoms

    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
