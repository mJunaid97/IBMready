    Pregnancy test

    Also known as: hCG test, beta-hCG, urine pregnancy test

    Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb. Urine tests give a yes/no answer; a blood test gives a number that normally rises steeply in a healthy early pregnancy. hCG appears in blood and urine within days of the embryo implanting and rises steeply through early pregnancy, which is why home tests become reliable from around the first day of a missed period and some sensitive tests can turn positive a little earlier. The urine test is a widely used point-of-care test: a strip or stick is dipped into urine or held in the stream and shows a line, symbol or word when hCG is present. In hospital, a urine test is done routinely in women of reproductive age with abdominal pain, bleeding or collapse, and before X-rays, operations and certain medicines, because an unrecognised pregnancy, and above all an ectopic pregnancy, changes what happens next.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Urine hCGMost tests can be used from the first day of a missed period; some very sensitive tests can be used earlier. A qualitative result: it says whether hCG is present above the test's detection limit, not how much. First-morning urine is the most concentrated and gives the clearest result when testing early, although most tests work at any time of day once the period is late.
    Serum hCGQuantitative; used to track ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage and molar pregnancy, and some tumours. A laboratory blood test can be qualitative (present or absent) or quantitative (the concentration), and serial quantitative results show whether a pregnancy is progressing normally, failing or abnormally sited; results take from a few hours to more than a day.

    Why it is ordered

    • A missed period, nausea, breast tenderness
    • Abdominal pain or bleeding in any woman of reproductive age (to exclude ectopic pregnancy)
    • Before X-rays, CT, surgery and many drugs
    • Monitoring after ectopic pregnancy or molar pregnancy treatment
    • Confirming pregnancy before booking antenatal care
    • Investigating a late period when the date of the expected period is uncertain

    How it is done

    A drop of urine on a test strip, ideally first-morning; or a venous blood sample. For a home test the strip is dipped into a collected sample or passed through the urine stream, and read within the time stated in the instructions. The instructions matter: reading too early or too late, using too little urine or testing before the period is due are the usual reasons for a misleading result. A blood sample for quantitative hCG is sent to the laboratory and is usually repeated after an interval so the trend can be judged.

    Preparation. None; testing before the missed period risks a false negative. If the date of the expected period is unknown, NHS advice is to test at least 21 days (three weeks) after the last unprotected sex. Drinking large volumes beforehand dilutes the urine and can push an early positive below the detection limit.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Positive urine testPregnant, almost always, provided the instructions were followed; an ultrasound scan at about 6 to 7 weeks can confirm where the pregnancy is
    Negative urine test with a period still overduePossibly tested too early or with dilute urine; repeat after a few days, and seek advice if a second test is negative and the period has still not started
    Positive test with pain or bleedingEctopic pregnancy must be excluded urgently
    Serum hCG rising slowlyFailing or ectopic pregnancy
    Serum hCG unusually high for datesTwins or a higher multiple, a molar pregnancy or wrong dates; ultrasound clarifies
    Serum hCG falling after treatmentResolution of a miscarriage, ectopic or molar pregnancy; followed until it is undetectable

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    False negatives when tested too early or with very dilute urine; rare false positives from fertility drugs, some tumours and recent pregnancy loss. A positive result is more reliable than a negative one. Very rarely, extremely high hCG levels, as in a molar pregnancy or a multiple pregnancy, overwhelm a urine test and give a false negative. Some medicines can affect the result. The test confirms that hCG is present but cannot say where the pregnancy is or whether it is viable; that needs ultrasound and, where there is doubt, serial blood hCG.

    Conditions

    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Cervical screening (smear test) A sample of cells brushed from the transformation zone of the cervix.

    Biomarkers

    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.

    Guides and news

    • NHS antenatal care: appointments and scans The NHS offers antenatal appointments with a midwife, two ultrasound scans and optional screening tests.
    • Early pregnancy signs and contacting a midwife The NHS says a missed period is the earliest sign of pregnancy, and many areas let people refer themselves to a midwife.
    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
    • Pregnancy scans and tests on the NHS NHS pregnancy care includes booking blood tests, a dating scan and a mid-pregnancy anomaly scan, plus optional screening for Down's.
