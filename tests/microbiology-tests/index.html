    Microbiology tests

    Also known as: infection tests, cultures and swabs

    Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests, or the antibodies the body makes against it. Culture also reports which antibiotics work. A culture test takes a sample from the site of suspected infection (blood, urine, sputum, a wound or throat swab, stool, spinal fluid or tissue) and places it in or on a nutrient medium that lets bacteria multiply until there are enough to identify. Once an organism grows, it is identified and exposed to a panel of antibiotics to see which stop it (susceptibility or sensitivity testing), which is what turns an empirical antibiotic choice into a targeted one. Molecular and antigen tests detect the organism directly, in hours rather than days, and serology shows the immune response to it. Microbiology results are read alongside the clinical picture, inflammatory markers and imaging, because the same organism may be a harmless coloniser in one person and the cause of sepsis in another.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Blood culturesTwo bottles from separate sites before antibiotics; growth is the first sign of possible bloodstream infection; the organism, growth in more than one set and the clinical picture decide whether it is real. The skin is cleaned with an antiseptic such as chlorhexidine before the needle goes in, because skin bacteria that fall into the bottle produce a false-positive result. Bacteria may be present in the blood only intermittently, so more than one set is often taken.
    Swabs and sputumThroat, wound, genital and sputum samples cultured or tested by PCR; a Gram stain of the sample under the microscope gives an early clue to the type of bacteria before culture results are back.
    PCR and antigen testsFast detection of viruses (COVID-19, influenza) and hard-to-grow bacteria; molecular panels can also identify the organism and some resistance genes directly from a positive blood-culture bottle within hours, well before conventional identification is complete.
    SerologyAntibodies show past or current infection (hepatitis, HIV, glandular fever) and immunity after vaccination.
    Stool testsBacteria, parasites and C. difficile toxin; faecal immunochemical test screens for bowel cancer.
    Antimicrobial susceptibilityFor each organism grown, which antibiotics it is sensitive or resistant to, so that treatment can be narrowed to the most effective, least broad agent.

    Why it is ordered

    • Fever with no obvious source, rigors, suspected sepsis
    • Pneumonia, urinary infection, wound infection, meningitis
    • Diarrhoea lasting more than a few days or after travel
    • Screening for HIV, hepatitis and sexually transmitted infections
    • Outbreak control and infection prevention (MRSA screening)
    • Choosing or narrowing an antibiotic once the organism and its sensitivities are known
    • Checking that an infection has cleared after treatment

    How it is done

    Samples taken with sterile technique, ideally before antibiotics; results from hours (PCR) to days (culture) or weeks (TB). In the laboratory the sample is placed in a dish or bottle containing a nutrient medium and incubated; most disease-causing bacteria grow enough to be tested within one to two days, but some species take much longer. After growth is detected, identifying the species and running antibiotic susceptibility tests adds a day or more, so a culture report usually arrives in stages: a Gram stain or early positive first, then the organism, then its sensitivities. Rapid molecular tests on positive blood-culture broth shorten this to hours in some laboratories. PCR and antigen tests are run on swabs or fluids and reported the same day.

    Preparation. None for most; clean-catch technique for urine, deep cough for sputum. Blood cultures are drawn with careful skin antisepsis, and the laboratory is told which antibiotics, if any, have already been given because they can stop organisms growing.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Two blood cultures grow the same organismTrue bacteraemia; find the source and treat for the right duration
    Skin organism in one bottle onlyProbably contamination introduced at the time of sampling rather than infection
    PCR positive for influenzaAntiviral treatment if early and at risk; isolation
    Resistant organismAntibiotic changed to a sensitive agent; infection control alerted
    No growthDoes not exclude infection, especially if antibiotics were given first, the organism is slow-growing or hard to culture, or the sample came from the wrong site; the clinical picture decides whether to continue treatment
    Growth of a usual coloniser from a non-sterile siteSkin, throat or gut flora on a swab may simply reflect normal carriage; treated only if the clinical picture indicates infection

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Cultures are negative in a third of sepsis cases, especially after antibiotics; a positive result can mean colonisation rather than infection, and contamination of blood cultures by skin bacteria is a recognised problem that leads to unnecessary treatment unless the pattern of growth is read carefully. Conventional culture is slow, so initial treatment is almost always empirical and adjusted when results arrive. Some organisms (anaerobes, fungi, mycobacteria, many viruses) grow poorly or not at all in standard culture and need special media, prolonged incubation or molecular tests.

    Conditions

    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Cellulitis Cellulitis is an infection of the deeper layers of the skin caused by bacteria.
    • Chlamydia A sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis.
    • Genital herpes Genital herpes is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the herpes simplex virus.
    • Gonorrhoea A bacterial sexually transmitted infection caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae that can affect the genitals, rectum, throat and eyes.
    • Flu (influenza) Flu is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Cervical screening (smear test) A sample of cells brushed from the transformation zone of the cervix.
    • HIV test A blood test that looks for the human immunodeficiency virus.

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Guides and news

    • Fever in adults: causes and when to get help A high temperature in adults is usually a sign of infection and often settles at home.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
