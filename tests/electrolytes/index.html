    Electrolytes

    Also known as: sodium and potassium, serum electrolytes

    The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium, magnesium and phosphate. An electrolyte panel is a blood test that checks for imbalances of these salts, of body water and of acid-base (pH) status. Sodium, which sits mostly in the fluid outside cells, governs how much water the body holds and keeps nerves and muscles working; potassium, which sits mostly inside cells, sets the excitability of the heart and muscle, so even small shifts in the blood level matter; chloride helps maintain blood volume and blood pressure; and bicarbonate is the main buffer against acid. From sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate the laboratory also calculates the anion gap, a derived figure that helps classify an acidosis. The panel is one of the most frequently requested hospital tests and is almost always read together with kidney function.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    SodiumGoverns water balance; low sodium is the commonest electrolyte disorder in hospital. Because most sodium is in the fluid outside cells, the blood level mainly reflects how much water the body is holding relative to salt. Symptoms of a low sodium depend on how fast it falls: a rapid drop causes weakness, tiredness and, when severe, confusion or coma, whereas a slow fall may cause nothing at all.
    PotassiumHigh or low levels cause dangerous heart rhythms. Most potassium is inside cells, so a small change in the plasma concentration can alter the heart's rhythm and its ability to contract, which is why it is checked so often in kidney disease, heart disease and in people taking diuretics or drugs that act on the kidney.
    ChlorideHelps maintain blood volume and blood pressure. High levels accompany dehydration, kidney disease and metabolic acidosis; low levels are seen with heart failure and lung disease.
    BicarbonateThe blood's main buffer; low in acidosis, high in alkalosis. Read with the anion gap, it helps separate acidosis caused by acid accumulation (diabetic ketoacidosis, lactic acidosis, kidney failure) from acidosis caused by bicarbonate loss.
    CalciumCorrected for albumin; high in parathyroid overactivity and cancer, low after thyroid surgery and in vitamin D deficiency.
    Magnesium and phosphateFall with malnutrition, alcohol and refeeding; matter for heart rhythm and muscle. Not every laboratory includes them in the basic panel, so they may need to be requested separately.

    Why it is ordered

    • Confusion, weakness, seizures, palpitations
    • Vomiting, diarrhoea, dehydration
    • Kidney disease and diuretic treatment
    • Before and after surgery
    • Endocrine disease (adrenal, parathyroid)
    • Monitoring treatment with intravenous fluids
    • High blood pressure, heart failure, liver disease and the medicines used for them
    • Suspected acid-base disturbance (diabetic ketoacidosis, shock, severe diarrhoea)

    How it is done

    Venous blood, analysed on an automated chemistry analyser; the panel is commonly run as part of a basic or comprehensive metabolic panel alongside kidney function and glucose. Point-of-care blood gas analysers in emergency departments and intensive care report sodium and potassium within minutes, but their values can differ from the central laboratory's and are confirmed with a laboratory sample when a decision rests on them. Potassium is falsely high if the sample is squeezed, haemolysed or delayed before separation.

    Preparation. None for a routine panel. The person's medicines, fluid intake and any vomiting or diarrhoea are noted because they strongly affect how the result is read.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Low sodiumOften too much water relative to salt: diuretics, heart or liver failure, SIADH, excess drinking; also sodium loss through vomiting, diarrhoea or the kidneys, or fluid accumulating as oedema. The speed of the fall determines the symptoms
    High sodiumWater deficit from dehydration or inadequate intake, or less commonly a hormone or kidney disorder that wastes water
    High potassiumKidney failure, ACE inhibitors and spironolactone, tissue breakdown; needs an ECG. A haemolysed or delayed sample is excluded first by repeating it
    Low potassiumVomiting, diarrhoea, loop diuretics, steroid excess
    High chlorideDehydration, kidney disease or a metabolic acidosis in which bicarbonate has been lost
    Low chlorideHeart failure or lung disease
    Low bicarbonate with a raised anion gapAcid accumulation: diabetic ketoacidosis, shock or kidney failure
    High calciumPrimary hyperparathyroidism or malignancy in most adults

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Blood levels do not always reflect total body stores (potassium is mostly inside cells, so a normal level can hide a large deficit or excess). Haemolysed, squeezed or delayed samples give spurious potassium. Each electrolyte is read against the reference interval of the laboratory that ran it, and intervals differ between laboratories and methods, so results from a blood gas analyser and the central laboratory are not interchangeable. An abnormal result identifies a disturbance but not its cause, which needs the history, medicines, kidney function and sometimes urine electrolytes and hormone tests.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Ambulatory ECG monitoring A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during…
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    Educational content. Reference ranges differ between laboratories, methods, ages and sexes, and results are always read against the person, the question asked and other findings. This page cannot interpret an individual result.
