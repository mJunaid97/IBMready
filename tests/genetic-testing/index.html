    Genetic testing

    Also known as: DNA test, gene panel, genomic testing

    Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk, or predict which drug will work. Modern sequencing panels test hundreds of genes at once. In the NHS, genetic (or genomic) testing is used mainly to find gene changes behind rare inherited conditions and some cancers: to diagnose a rare condition in a child, to show whether someone has a higher risk of certain conditions, and to guide the choice of treatment or medicine. Tests range from a chromosome study that checks the number and structure of whole chromosomes, through a single-gene test when a particular disorder is suspected, to whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing when it is unclear which gene might be responsible. Testing is free on the NHS when it is requested by a hospital specialist, and it is offered with genetic counselling because the result can affect relatives as well as the person tested.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Diagnostic testingConfirms a suspected inherited condition (cystic fibrosis, Huntington disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia). A single gene is sequenced when the diagnosis is clear from the symptoms; a panel or whole-genome test is used when several genes could be responsible.
    Predictive testingFinds high-risk variants before disease appears (BRCA1/2 for breast and ovarian cancer, Lynch syndrome for bowel cancer). A positive predictive result shows raised risk but usually cannot say exactly how likely, or when, the disease will develop.
    Tumour profilingMutations in the cancer itself (EGFR, ALK, KRAS in lung cancer) that match targeted drugs. These are acquired changes in the tumour, not inherited ones, and are tested on the existing biopsy.
    PharmacogenomicsVariants that change how drugs are handled (DPYD before fluorouracil, HLA-B*57:01 before abacavir), so that a dose can be adjusted or a drug avoided.
    Carrier, prenatal and newborn testingCarrier testing shows whether a healthy person carries a variant that could be passed to children; prenatal testing looks for genetic conditions in a fetus; newborn screening picks up treatable inherited conditions soon after birth.

    Why it is ordered

    • A family history of cancer at young ages or in several relatives
    • A child with developmental problems or an unexplained syndrome
    • Choosing targeted cancer treatment
    • Planning a pregnancy in carriers of recessive conditions
    • Sudden cardiac death in a young relative
    • Checking whether a healthy person carries a variant that could be passed on (carrier testing), and newborn screening for treatable inherited conditions
    • Confirming a genetic cause when a condition is already suspected clinically, so that the right screening and treatment can be planned

    How it is done

    A routine blood sample is usual; saliva or a cheek swab (buccal smear), hair, skin, amniotic fluid or other tissue can also be used, and tumour testing uses the existing biopsy. The laboratory looks for specific changes in chromosomes, DNA or proteins according to the question asked: sequencing reads the letters of a gene or of the whole genome, chromosome tests look for large gains, losses or rearrangements, and protein or enzyme tests infer a gene change from its effect. Because many thousands of variants must be interpreted, results take weeks and sometimes months; NHS laboratories quote a range of several weeks to a few months for most tests, with longer waits for complex genome analyses. The written report goes to the referring clinician, who explains it to the person; stored data can be reanalysed later if new gene-disease links are discovered. Direct-to-consumer kits that use a saliva sample are also sold; a worrying result from one of these is usually discussed with a doctor or genetic counsellor, who may arrange confirmatory testing.

    Preparation. Counselling before predictive tests, because results affect relatives and insurance in some countries. A genetic counsellor or clinician explains what the test can and cannot show, the chance of an uncertain result, how the information will be shared with family members and that the result may affect life or medical insurance; a consent form records the decision. No fasting or other physical preparation is needed for a blood or saliva sample.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant foundDiagnosis confirmed or high risk established; usually, though not always, this explains the condition. Cascade testing is offered to relatives and the result may guide screening, treatment or family planning
    Variant of uncertain significanceA DNA change not yet known to cause or not cause disease; cannot be used for diagnosis or to change management, but may be reclassified as evidence accumulates
    No variant foundLowers but does not abolish the chance of a genetic cause; the test only reads the genes on the panel, and other genomic tests or reanalysis may be possible in future. Relatives may still carry risk
    Positive predictive result in a healthy personRaised lifetime risk of the condition, but the test usually cannot give the exact risk or the age at which disease might start; surveillance or risk-reducing options are discussed
    Carrier resultThe person is healthy but could pass the variant on; testing of a partner and reproductive options are discussed

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Most common diseases are polygenic and environmental, so a single gene test says little; incidental findings and implications for family members need careful counselling. Everyone carries many harmless gene changes, so the difficulty lies in interpretation: variants of uncertain significance are common and cannot be acted on, a negative result does not exclude a genetic cause because only the genes tested are read, and even a confirmed diagnosis may say little about how severe the condition will be. Results take weeks to months. In some countries a result can affect insurance, and knowledge of risk cannot be undone, which is why counselling precedes predictive testing.

    Conditions

    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.
    • Ovarian cancer A malignant tumour of the ovary or, as is now recognised for many cases of the commonest type, of the adjacent fallopian tube.
    • Prostate cancer Prostate cancer is a cancer that starts in the prostate, a small gland in the male reproductive system that sits just below the bladder.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Imaging

    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.

    Guides and news

    • FDA approves imlunestrant with abemaciclib The US FDA approved imlunestrant (Inluriyo) with abemaciclib on 18 September 2026 for ER-positive, HER2-negative, ESR1-mutated advanced breast cancer.
