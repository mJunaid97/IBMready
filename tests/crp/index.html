    CRP test

    Also known as: C-reactive protein, C-reactive protein test, inflammatory marker

    A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury. It measures the size and course of an inflammatory response and how it changes with treatment, but not its cause. CRP is a general marker: it is often the first laboratory evidence that something inflammatory is happening, with the level rising before pain, fever or other signs are obvious, and its concentration can climb many hundred-fold during a severe infection. Because it falls quickly once the trigger settles, repeated measurements are more informative than a single value, and clinicians read it alongside the symptoms, the examination and other tests.

    Why is it used?

    • Suspected infection or sepsis, and monitoring the response to antibiotics
    • Distinguishing inflammatory joint disease from wear-and-tear pain
    • Unexplained fever, weight loss or fatigue
    • Monitoring rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, polymyalgia and vasculitis
    • After surgery, where a rising level can signal a complication
    • Deciding whether a respiratory infection in primary care is likely to need antibiotics (point-of-care CRP)
    • Estimating cardiovascular risk with a high-sensitivity assay in selected people

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A venous blood sample, measured in the laboratory by an immunoassay such as nephelometry or turbidimetry. Point-of-care fingerprick CRP, read on a small device in the consulting room, is used in some primary-care settings to help decide whether an infection is likely to be bacterial and whether antibiotics are needed.

    Specimen. Venous blood (serum or plasma); fingerprick for point-of-care tests

    Do I need to prepare?

    None.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    Markedly raised CRPSignificant bacterial infection or major inflammation until proven otherwise; the source is sought with examination, imaging and cultures
    Mildly raised CRP (roughly 5-20 mg/L)Minor inflammation, viral illness, obesity, smoking or recent exertion; interpreted with the clinical picture and often repeated rather than acted on
    Normal CRP with a high ESRConsider conditions that raise ESR without much CRP: myeloma, lupus, anaemia, pregnancy
    Falling CRP on treatmentThe inflammation is settling or the antibiotic is working; a persistently rising value after surgery prompts a search for infection or a leak
    Raised CRP in a long-term inflammatory diseaseA flare of rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis, lupus or inflammatory bowel disease, or an intercurrent infection; a fall after a change of treatment shows the anti-inflammatory therapy is taking effect
    Normal CRP very early in an illnessDoes not exclude infection, because the level takes hours to rise; a repeat sample the next day is often more informative
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. Laboratories report an upper reference limit for CRP that differs between methods; high-sensitivity assays used for cardiovascular risk have their own bands. There is no diagnostic threshold: a raised CRP is a prompt to look for the cause, not a diagnosis.

    What can affect the result?

    • Time since the illness began (the first hours can be normal)
    • Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants blunt the rise
    • Obesity, smoking, pregnancy and oestrogens raise the baseline
    • Strenuous exercise in the previous day
    • Recent heart attack or surgery, which raise CRP even without infection
    • The method and units used by the laboratory or point-of-care device

    Medicines. Corticosteroids, biologic anti-inflammatory drugs (particularly IL-6 inhibitors) and other immunosuppressants can keep CRP low despite active infection.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • Where the inflammation is or what is causing it
    • Whether an infection is bacterial or viral with certainty
    • Whether a person with a normal CRP has no serious illness
    • Which organ or tissue is inflamed
    • Whether a long-term condition is active or an unrelated infection has occurred, without the clinical picture

    Non-specific: it cannot say where the inflammation is or what is causing it, and it is not diagnostic of any single disease. A normal CRP early in an illness does not exclude infection, and some infections (viral, some fungal) and lupus flares barely raise it. Steroids and immunosuppressants blunt the rise. Although CRP is often used to help separate bacterial from viral infection, the overlap between the two is considerable, so it supports rather than replaces clinical judgement. Different laboratories and point-of-care devices use different methods and units, so results from different sources are not directly interchangeable.

    Conditions

    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Diverticular disease and diverticulitis Diverticular disease is a condition in which small pouches in the wall of the large intestine cause symptoms.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) A long-term digestive condition with repeated abdominal pain and changes in bowel habit, but no visible damage or disease in the digestive tract.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.

    Medical tests

    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Knee replacement The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when…

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.

    Guides and news

    • Fever in adults: causes and when to get help A high temperature in adults is usually a sign of infection and often settles at home.
    • High CRP: what it means and what happens next A high CRP means there is inflammation or infection somewhere in the body, but not where or why, so doctors read it with symptoms and other tests.
    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
