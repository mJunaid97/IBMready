    CRP test

    Also known as: C-reactive protein, C-reactive protein test, hs-CRP, inflammatory markers

    A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury. It measures the size and course of an inflammatory response and how it changes with treatment, but not its cause.

    Type
    Blood test
    Sample
    Blood from a vein
    Used for
    Infection, inflammation, monitoring treatment
    Measures
    C-reactive protein, an acute-phase protein made by the liver
    Result
    Numeric; a laboratory reference interval, usually with an upper limit only

    Why is it used?

    • Suspected infection or sepsis, and monitoring the response to antibiotics
    • Distinguishing inflammatory joint disease from wear-and-tear pain
    • Unexplained fever, weight loss or fatigue
    • Monitoring rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, polymyalgia and vasculitis
    • After surgery, where a rising level can signal a complication

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A venous blood sample; point-of-care fingerprick CRP is used in some primary-care settings to guide antibiotic decisions.

    Specimen. Venous blood (serum or plasma); fingerprick for point-of-care tests

    Do I need to prepare?

    None.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    CRP above 100 mg/LSignificant bacterial infection or major inflammation until proven otherwise
    Mildly raised CRP (roughly 5-20 mg/L)Minor inflammation, viral illness, obesity, smoking or recent exertion; interpreted with the clinical picture
    Normal CRP with a high ESRConsider conditions that raise ESR without much CRP: myeloma, lupus, anaemia, pregnancy
    Falling CRP on treatmentThe inflammation is settling; a persistently rising value after surgery prompts a search for infection or a leak
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. Laboratories report an upper reference limit for CRP that differs between methods; high-sensitivity assays used for cardiovascular risk have their own bands. There is no diagnostic threshold: a raised CRP is a prompt to look for the cause, not a diagnosis.

    What can affect the result?

    • Time since the illness began (the first hours can be normal)
    • Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants blunt the rise
    • Obesity, smoking, pregnancy and oestrogens raise the baseline
    • Strenuous exercise in the previous day

    Medicines. Corticosteroids, biologic anti-inflammatory drugs (particularly IL-6 inhibitors) and other immunosuppressants can keep CRP low despite active infection.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • Where the inflammation is or what is causing it
    • Whether an infection is bacterial or viral with certainty
    • Whether a person with a normal CRP has no serious illness

    Non-specific: it cannot say where the inflammation is or what is causing it. A normal CRP early in an illness does not exclude infection, and some infections (viral, some fungal) and lupus flares barely raise it. Steroids and immunosuppressants blunt the rise.
