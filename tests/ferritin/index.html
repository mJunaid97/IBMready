    Ferritin test

    Also known as: serum ferritin, iron stores test, ferritin blood test

    A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells. It is the best single test for iron deficiency, the commonest cause of anaemia, and it also detects iron overload; because ferritin rises with inflammation, it is often read alongside CRP.

    Why is it used?

    • Anaemia, especially with small red cells (low MCV)
    • Tiredness, restless legs, hair loss or brittle nails where iron deficiency is suspected
    • Heavy periods, pregnancy, vegetarian or vegan diets, coeliac disease, after gastric surgery
    • Suspected haemochromatosis or iron overload from repeated transfusions
    • Monitoring iron treatment and, in chronic kidney disease and heart failure, iron status before intravenous iron

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A venous blood sample, often with a full blood count and sometimes transferrin saturation.

    Specimen. Venous blood (serum)

    Do I need to prepare?

    None; a recent iron infusion or blood transfusion should be mentioned.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    Ferritin below about 30 µg/L in an adultIron stores are depleted (iron deficiency), whether or not anaemia is present yet; the cause, often blood loss, is then looked for
    Normal ferritin with raised CRP and anaemiaInflammation may be masking iron deficiency; transferrin saturation or a trial of iron helps decide
    Very high ferritinIron overload (haemochromatosis, transfusions) or severe inflammation, liver disease, cancer or a haemophagocytic syndrome
    Ferritin rising on iron treatmentStores are being replenished; treatment usually continues for months after haemoglobin normalises
    Read against guideline thresholds, not a laboratory range. Laboratories publish reference intervals that differ by sex and age, but the clinical question 'are iron stores depleted?' is answered with a guideline cut-off rather than the lower reference limit, and the cut-off is raised when inflammation is present.

    Guideline thresholds

    ThresholdValueUnitApplies toCountry · source
    Iron deficiencybelow 30µg/LAdults; ferritin below this level confirms depleted iron stores. With coexisting inflammation a higher cut-off is usedUK UK

    Clinical decision thresholds come from the named guideline and country; they are not laboratory reference intervals, and they change when guidance changes.

    What can affect the result?

    • Inflammation and infection raise ferritin regardless of iron stores
    • Liver disease, alcohol, obesity and metabolic syndrome
    • Recent iron infusion or transfusion
    • Time of day and assay differences between laboratories

    Medicines. Oral and intravenous iron raise it; regular aspirin or NSAID use can cause the gut blood loss that lowers it; proton pump inhibitors reduce iron absorption.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • Why iron is low (the source of blood loss must be sought separately)
    • Iron status reliably during active inflammation without other tests
    • Whether anaemia is due to something other than iron, such as B12 or folate deficiency

    Ferritin is an acute-phase protein: any inflammation, infection, liver disease or alcohol excess raises it and can hide a deficiency. It does not show why iron is low; in adults iron deficiency is a reason to look for a source of blood loss, particularly in the gut.
