    Hormone tests

    Also known as: endocrine tests, hormone blood tests

    Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day, the cycle and the level of the controlling hormone from the pituitary. Includes cortisol, sex hormones, prolactin, growth hormone, parathyroid hormone, vitamin D and, by convention on this site, uric acid. Most hormones are released in pulses, follow a daily rhythm or vary with the menstrual cycle, so the laboratory value only makes sense when the time and circumstances of the sample are known. The usual logic is to measure a gland's hormone together with the pituitary hormone that drives it: a low gland hormone with a high pituitary signal points to failure of the gland itself, whereas a low gland hormone with a low or inappropriately normal pituitary signal points to the pituitary or hypothalamus. Where a single level cannot settle the question, dynamic tests are used: the gland is deliberately stimulated (for example with synthetic ACTH, Synacthen, to test the adrenal cortex) or suppressed (for example with dexamethasone to test whether cortisol production can be switched off) and the hormone is measured before and after.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    CortisolThe stress hormone from the adrenal cortex; highest at 9 am. Low in Addison disease, high in Cushing syndrome. Measured in blood, in a 24-hour urine collection (which averages the day's output and may need repeating because production varies) or in late-night saliva, and often paired with ACTH to work out whether an abnormality arises in the adrenal gland or the pituitary. Severe stress, acute illness and severe depression also raise it.
    Testosterone and oestradiolSex hormones; measured with LH and FSH from the pituitary to tell gland failure from pituitary failure. In men, testosterone with sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) helps investigate infertility, low libido and erectile dysfunction; prolactin, oestradiol and LH are often measured alongside to see the whole hormonal balance.
    ProlactinRaised by pituitary tumours, stress, many drugs and pregnancy; causes milk production and lost periods. Checked for unexplained milk production, irregular periods, fertility problems and persistent headaches or visual change; in men a raised prolactin often causes a low testosterone, so it is tested when testosterone is found to be low. An underactive thyroid is a common and easily treated cause of a raised prolactin.
    Parathyroid hormone and vitamin DControl calcium and bone; a high PTH with high calcium is primary hyperparathyroidism.
    Uric acid (urate)The waste product that crystallises in gout; NICE defines hyperuricaemia as a serum urate of 360 micromol/L or more. Its general treatment target in gout is below 360 micromol/L, with below 300 micromol/L suggested for people with tophi, chronic gouty arthritis or ongoing frequent flares despite a level below 360; the British Society for Rheumatology recommends an initial target below 300 micromol/L for everyone starting urate-lowering treatment.

    Why it is ordered

    • Suspected adrenal, pituitary or gonadal disease
    • Osteoporosis and calcium problems
    • Gout diagnosis and treatment targets
    • Infertility, missed periods, erectile dysfunction, hair changes
    • Assessing pituitary function after tumours or surgery
    • Monitoring gender-affirming hormone therapy (oestradiol or testosterone with the treating team's targets)
    • Suspected Cushing syndrome (weight gain with thin limbs, a rounded face, easy bruising) or suspected adrenal insufficiency (fatigue, weight loss, low blood pressure, low sodium with high potassium)
    • Unexplained milk production, irregular or absent periods, or headaches with visual change (prolactin)

    How it is done

    Venous blood, often at a set time (9 am cortisol, early morning testosterone) or after a stimulation or suppression drug. For a short Synacthen test, blood is taken for cortisol, a synthetic ACTH injection is given into the arm and further samples are drawn at set intervals afterwards to see whether the adrenal glands respond; a morning cortisol is checked first and the dynamic test is reserved for results that are neither clearly normal nor clearly low. A dexamethasone suppression test gives a dose of the synthetic steroid and then measures whether cortisol production has been switched off. Cortisol can also be measured in a 24-hour urine collection or a late-night saliva sample. Hormones are measured by immunoassay, and the laboratory's reference interval for the time of day, sex and, where relevant, phase of the cycle is printed on the report.

    Preparation. Depends on the test; some need fasting, timing to the menstrual cycle, and biotin supplements can affect some results; tell the team about every medicine you take, including steroids, and never stop or change one unless the prescriber tells you to. Cortisol is sampled early in the morning because it peaks then, and the person notes any steroid medicines (tablets, inhalers, creams or injections), which suppress the body's own production and invalidate the result. Prolactin is taken when the person is rested, because stress raises it. Medicines are reviewed before a prolactin test because many commonly used drugs increase it.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Low 9 am cortisolAdrenal insufficiency; confirmed with a synacthen stimulation test. Low sodium and high potassium on the electrolyte panel support the diagnosis of Addison disease
    Poor cortisol response to Synacthen with high ACTHPrimary adrenal failure (Addison disease), usually with low aldosterone; a poor response can also follow long-standing lack of ACTH from the pituitary, so further tests and endocrine referral separate the two
    Normal cortisol response to SynacthenMakes primary adrenal failure unlikely but does not exclude recent-onset ACTH deficiency from the pituitary, which needs specialist assessment if suspected
    High cortisol that does not suppress with dexamethasoneCushing syndrome from a pituitary or adrenal tumour, or from ACTH produced by a tumour elsewhere in the body; severe stress, illness and depression can mimic it, so results are confirmed with repeat and further tests
    High PTH and high calciumPrimary hyperparathyroidism, usually a single adenoma
    Low testosterone with high LHTesticular failure; low LH points to the pituitary instead
    Persistently raised prolactinMacroprolactin (a harmless large form) is excluded, thyroid function and medicines are reviewed, and an MRI of the pituitary is considered
    Serum urate 360 micromol/L or aboveSupports gout, but the level can be normal during an attack, so it is often repeated at least two weeks after the attack settles

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Hormones pulse and cycle; a single random level is often uninterpretable. Dynamic tests (stimulate or suppress the gland, then measure) are needed to prove most diagnoses, and even these have grey zones: a normal Synacthen response does not exclude early pituitary disease, and a failed response does not by itself say whether the adrenal gland or the pituitary is at fault. Stress, acute illness, depression, pregnancy, the contraceptive pill and many medicines shift the values. Reference intervals depend on time of day, sex, age and cycle phase and differ between laboratories. In a suspected adrenal crisis there is no time for dynamic testing: a blood sample is taken and treatment starts before the result is known.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.
    • Gender dysphoria (gender incongruence) The distress that can arise when a person's gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth.
    • Menopause and perimenopause Menopause is when periods stop altogether, and perimenopause is the stage before this when periods change and symptoms begin.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Vaginoplasty Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people.

    Medications

    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
