    Full pulmonary function tests

    Also known as: lung function tests, gas transfer, lung volumes, body plethysmography

    Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out, and how easily gas crosses from the air sacs into blood (gas transfer, DLCO). Together they classify almost any lung disease. Spirometry shows how much air can be moved and how fast, but it cannot see the air left in the chest after a full breath out, and it says nothing about whether oxygen is getting from the alveoli into the blood. Full pulmonary function tests add a lung-volume measurement, by body plethysmography in a sealed cabin or by breathing a harmless tracer gas that is diluted by the air in the lungs, and a gas-transfer test in which a very low concentration of carbon monoxide is inhaled, held for a few seconds and exhaled so the amount taken up by the blood can be calculated. The results are compared with values predicted for the person's age, sex and height, and the pattern across all three parts (airflow, volumes and transfer) distinguishes airway disease from disease of the lung tissue, the blood vessels, the chest wall or the breathing muscles.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Total lung capacity and residual volumeMeasured in a sealed booth (plethysmography) or by gas dilution; high in emphysema (air trapping), low in fibrosis. Total lung capacity is the volume in the lungs after the fullest possible breath in; residual volume is what remains after the fullest breath out and can only be measured indirectly, which is why spirometry alone cannot confirm a restrictive defect.
    Gas transfer (DLCO)How much of a trace of carbon monoxide crosses into blood in one breath; reduced in emphysema, fibrosis and pulmonary vascular disease, raised in lung bleeding. Carbon monoxide is used because it diffuses rapidly and binds tightly to haemoglobin, so the amount that disappears from the exhaled breath reflects the surface area and thickness of the alveolar-capillary membrane and the blood available to take it up.
    Flow-volume loopThe shape reveals upper airway obstruction and the site of narrowing.
    Respiratory muscle pressuresFor suspected neuromuscular weakness.
    Exercise capacityA six-minute walk test or similar measures how far the person can walk and how their oxygen saturation and breathlessness change with effort, adding a functional measure to the laboratory numbers.

    Why it is ordered

    • Unexplained breathlessness with normal spirometry
    • Interstitial lung disease and sarcoidosis
    • Assessing fitness for lung resection or chemotherapy (bleomycin)
    • Neuromuscular disease affecting breathing
    • Grading emphysema
    • Separating airway disease from disease of the lung tissue, chest wall or breathing muscles when spirometry shows a restrictive pattern
    • Monitoring the response of a lung disease to treatment over time

    How it is done

    Sitting in a clear booth breathing through a mouthpiece with a nose clip through a series of manoeuvres, about an hour. For lung volumes by plethysmography the person sits in a sealed, transparent cabin resembling a telephone box and breathes against a mouthpiece; as the chest moves, the pressure changes in the cabin and at the mouth are used to calculate the volume of gas in the chest. The dilution alternative has the person breathe a known quantity of an inert tracer gas from the machine until it mixes evenly with the air in the lungs. For gas transfer the person breathes out fully, takes a single deep breath of the test gas containing a very low concentration of carbon monoxide, holds it for a few seconds and breathes out; the exhaled gas is analysed and compared with what was inhaled. Spirometry, often with a bronchodilator, is repeated in the same session, and the operator coaches each manoeuvre and repeats it until the results are consistent.

    Preparation. As for spirometry; avoid smoking for 24 hours because carbon monoxide affects gas transfer. Inhalers, a heavy meal, alcohol and vigorous exercise are avoided beforehand as instructed, and a recent haemoglobin result is helpful because anaemia lowers gas transfer.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Obstruction with high residual volume and low DLCOEmphysema
    Obstruction with normal DLCOAsthma or chronic bronchitis without emphysema; the airways are narrowed but the gas-exchanging tissue is intact
    Restriction with low DLCOPulmonary fibrosis or other interstitial disease
    Restriction with normal DLCOChest wall, obesity or muscle weakness rather than lung parenchyma
    Low DLCO with normal volumesPulmonary vascular disease, early fibrosis, anaemia
    Normal full lung function with persistent breathlessnessPoints away from the lungs towards the heart, deconditioning, anaemia or other causes; exercise testing may follow

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Anaemia lowers DLCO; results are compared with predicted values that depend on age, sex, height and ethnicity. Lung function results alone cannot name the disease: they describe a pattern (obstructive, restrictive, mixed, impaired transfer) that must be combined with the history, examination and imaging. All the manoeuvres depend on the person's understanding, effort and ability to hold their breath, so frail or very breathless people and young children may not produce valid results. Smoking shortly before the test, carbon monoxide exposure and a recent lung bleed alter gas transfer. Full tests need a hospital laboratory and take considerably longer than spirometry.

    Conditions

    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Medications

    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.

    Guides and news

    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • A cough that will not go: causes and tests The NHS advises a GP visit for a cough lasting over 3 weeks, and an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for coughing up blood.
    • Spirometry: the lung function test explained Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast.
