    Electrocardiogram

    Also known as: electrocardiogram, 12-lead ECG, heart tracing

    Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds. The tracing shows rhythm, rate, the direction of electrical spread and signs of muscle strain, damage or blocked blood supply.

    Why is it used?

    • Chest pain (recorded within 10 minutes of first medical contact when a heart attack is suspected)
    • Palpitations, blackouts, breathlessness
    • Screening before surgery or in athletes
    • Electrolyte problems and drug monitoring
    • Suspected pulmonary embolism, pericarditis, heart failure

    What does it measure?

    ComponentWhat it measuresA higher value may be seen inA lower value may be seen in
    Rate and rhythmHow fast and how regularly the heart beatsSinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation or flutter, supraventricular and ventricular tachycardiasSinus bradycardia, heart block, sick sinus syndrome
    P wave and PR interval PRAtrial activation and conduction through the AV nodeLong PR: first-degree heart blockShort PR: pre-excitation (Wolff–Parkinson–White)
    QRS complex QRSVentricular activationWide QRS: bundle branch block, ventricular rhythms, high potassium; tall complexes: hypertrophy; Q waves: old infarctionSmall complexes: obesity, pericardial fluid, lung disease
    ST segment and T wave ST/TVentricular recovery; the part most changed by ischaemiaST elevation: acute infarction (STEMI) or pericarditis; tall T waves: high potassiumST depression and T inversion: ischaemia, strain, digoxin, low potassium
    QT interval QTcTotal time for ventricular activation and recovery, corrected for heart rateLong QT: inherited syndromes, many medicines, low potassium, magnesium or calcium; risk of dangerous rhythmsShort QT: rare inherited syndromes, high calcium

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    Lie still for a minute while the machine prints; painless, no electricity goes into the body.

    Specimen. None: a recording of the heart's electrical activity from skin electrodes

    Do I need to prepare?

    None; chest hair may be shaved for contact.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    ST elevation in two contiguous leadsSTEMI: an artery is blocked, needs emergency angioplasty
    Irregularly irregular rhythm, no P wavesAtrial fibrillation
    Tall peaked T wavesHigh potassium, needs immediate treatment
    Normal ECGReassuring but does not exclude a heart attack or intermittent rhythm problems
    Interpreted descriptively by a clinician, not against a numeric range. An ECG is interpreted against standard interval limits and waveform criteria rather than a laboratory range; the automated report on the machine is a starting point that a clinician confirms or overrules, ideally by comparison with a previous tracing.

    What can affect the result?

    • Electrode placement, movement, shivering and electrical interference
    • Body shape, lung disease and pericardial fluid change voltages
    • Electrolyte levels (potassium, calcium, magnesium) and many medicines
    • Heart rate, which changes the corrected QT
    • Timing: a tracing between episodes of an intermittent arrhythmia is often normal

    Medicines. Many medicines prolong the QT interval (some antibiotics including macrolides, antipsychotics, antidepressants, antiemetics); digoxin, beta blockers and calcium channel blockers slow conduction; sodium channel blockers widen the QRS.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • Whether a heart attack has occurred when the first tracing is normal (a normal ECG does not exclude one); serial ECGs and troponin are needed
    • How well the heart pumps or whether the valves work (echocardiography)
    • Whether the coronary arteries are narrowed when the person is at rest (stress testing or angiography)
    • Intermittent rhythm problems that were not happening during the ten seconds recorded

    A ten-second snapshot: intermittent arrhythmias need longer monitoring, and a normal first ECG does not rule out a heart attack.

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Ambulatory ECG monitoring A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during…
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

    Guides and news

    • Chest pain: causes and when to get help Most chest pain does not come from the heart, but sudden pain that does not ease or spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back needs a 999 call.
    • Dizziness and vertigo: causes and warning signs Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure.
    • ECG: what it records and what it can show An ECG records the heart's electrical activity through patches on the skin.
    • Echocardiogram: types, what happens and results An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to picture the heart.
    • Heart attack, cardiac arrest and angina A heart attack is a blocked blood supply to the heart muscle, cardiac arrest is the heart stopping, and angina is chest pain that eases with rest.
    • Heart rhythm monitors: Holter and loop recorders A Holter monitor records the heart's electrical activity for 24 to 48 hours or longer, to catch rhythm problems that come and go.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
    • Troponin blood test: what it shows A troponin test measures proteins released when heart muscle is damaged, and a raised result does not always mean a heart attack.
