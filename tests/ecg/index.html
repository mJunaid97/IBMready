    Electrocardiogram

    Also known as: ECG, EKG, 12-lead ECG, heart tracing

    Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds. The tracing shows rhythm, rate, the direction of electrical spread and signs of muscle strain, damage or blocked blood supply.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Rate and rhythmNormal sinus rhythm 60-100 per minute; irregular with no P waves in atrial fibrillation.
    P wave, PR intervalAtrial activation and the delay at the AV node; a long PR is heart block.
    QRS complexVentricular activation; wide when conduction is blocked in a bundle branch.
    ST segment and T waveRepolarisation; ST elevation is the signature of an acute heart attack, ST depression of ischaemia or strain.
    QT intervalTotal activation time; prolonged by drugs and electrolyte problems, risking dangerous rhythms.

    Why it is ordered

    • Chest pain (within 10 minutes of arrival)
    • Palpitations, blackouts, breathlessness
    • Screening before surgery or in athletes
    • Electrolyte problems and drug monitoring
    • Suspected pulmonary embolism, pericarditis, heart failure

    How it is done

    Lie still for a minute while the machine prints; painless, no electricity goes into the body.

    Preparation. None; chest hair may be shaved for contact.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    ST elevation in two adjacent leadsSTEMI: an artery is blocked, needs emergency angioplasty
    Irregularly irregular rhythm, no P wavesAtrial fibrillation
    Tall peaked T wavesHigh potassium, needs immediate treatment
    Normal ECGReassuring but does not exclude a heart attack or intermittent rhythm problems

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    A ten-second snapshot: intermittent arrhythmias need longer monitoring, and a third of heart attacks have a normal first ECG.

    Educational content. Reference ranges differ between laboratories, methods, ages and sexes, and results are always read against the person, the question asked and other findings. Educational only.
