    Endoscopy (as a test)

    Also known as: scope test, endoscopic examination

    A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut, so the lining can be seen directly and sampled. Each region has its own scope: the gullet and stomach (gastroscopy), the large bowel (colonoscopy and the shorter sigmoidoscopy), the airways (bronchoscopy), the bladder and urethra (cystoscopy, with ureteroscopy for the ureters), joints (arthroscopy), the abdomen and pelvis (laparoscopy), the womb (hysteroscopy), the cervix (colposcopy) and the nose and sinuses (nasal endoscopy). As a test, endoscopy is used to find the cause of symptoms such as stomach pain, indigestion, difficulty swallowing or bleeding, to take tissue samples and, at the same sitting, to treat what is found, for example by stopping bleeding or removing small growths. It is almost always a day-case procedure in a dedicated endoscopy unit.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Direct view of the liningInflammation, ulcers, bleeding, polyps, narrowings and tumours are seen and photographed; the operator records the site, size and appearance of each finding so it can be followed up or compared later.
    BiopsiesTiny samples taken painlessly from the lining with forceps passed through the scope, sent for microscopy; biopsies can bleed a little, which usually stops on its own and otherwise is treated through the scope.
    TherapyPolyps removed, bleeding stopped, narrowings stretched, stones retrieved at the same sitting, avoiding a separate operation in many cases.

    Why it is ordered

    • Difficulty swallowing, persistent indigestion, vomiting blood, weight loss (gastroscopy)
    • Bleeding from the bowel, change in bowel habit, positive stool screening test, anaemia (colonoscopy)
    • Coughing blood, a shadow on a chest scan (bronchoscopy)
    • Blood in the urine (cystoscopy)
    • Surveillance of Barrett oesophagus, ulcerative colitis, polyps
    • Taking tissue samples from the lining to confirm or exclude cancer or inflammatory disease
    • Treating a problem already found: stopping bleeding, removing polyps or stretching a narrowing

    How it is done

    Usually a day case with throat spray or sedation; 10-45 minutes depending on the scope, and a transnasal gastroscopy with a very thin scope can take only a few minutes. Sedation makes the person drowsy and relaxed but not unconscious, so they can still hear what is happening; a trained endoscopy nurse monitors breathing, heart rate and blood pressure throughout because sedatives can depress them briefly. Afterwards the person rests in a recovery area for an hour or two until the sedation wears off before going home. A sore throat or nose, bloating, wind and mild abdominal discomfort are common for a day and settle on their own. Full details of each examination are on its procedure page.

    Preparation. Fasting for upper scopes, because food in the stomach obscures the view and can be inhaled under sedation; the unit's instructions on when to stop eating and drinking must be followed or the test may be cancelled. Bowel preparation laxatives and a low-residue diet for colonoscopy and capsule endoscopy. The team is told about all medicines, including over-the-counter products and supplements, and about allergies; anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs may need to be paused on the unit's instructions, never stopped without advice. Consent is taken after the risks have been explained.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Normal examinationNo structural cause found in the part examined; symptoms are then attributed to functional disorders, reflux without visible damage or disease outside the reach of the scope, and other tests may follow
    Inflammation or ulcerationBiopsies establish the cause (acid, infection, inflammatory bowel disease); an ulcer with a visible vessel has a high re-bleeding risk and is treated endoscopically
    Polyp removed, adenoma on histologySurveillance interval set by number and size of the polyps
    Mass biopsied, malignantStaging scans and multidisciplinary team planning
    Narrowing (stricture)Benign scarring or a tumour; biopsied and, if appropriate, stretched or stented at the same or a later sitting

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Invasive, with small risks of bleeding and perforation (a tear in the wall) that are much lower than for open surgery but can need admission or an operation; sedation can transiently affect breathing and blood pressure, which is why monitoring is continuous. The small bowel is mostly out of reach of standard scopes (capsule endoscopy and enteroscopy cover it), and the view depends on preparation: a poorly cleared bowel or a stomach containing food can hide lesions and force a repeat. Endoscopy shows the surface of the lining; disease in the wall or outside the organ needs imaging. Abdominal pain, black or dark stools, vomiting or fever after the procedure are reasons to contact the unit promptly.

    Conditions

    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Pancreatic cancer Pancreatic cancer is a cancer that starts in the pancreas, an organ that helps with digestion.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Procedures

    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
