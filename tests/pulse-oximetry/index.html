    Pulse oximetry and blood gases

    Also known as: oxygen saturation, sats, oxygen level

    A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin is with oxygen. When more precision or a carbon dioxide level is needed, an arterial blood gas takes blood directly from an artery. Pulse oximetry is painless, takes seconds and can be done anywhere: in a GP surgery, clinic, ambulance, hospital ward or at home, which made it useful for remote monitoring during the COVID-19 pandemic. It is part of routine observations in hospital, and it is used to decide whether a person needs oxygen, to check that oxygen therapy or a lung medicine is working, to monitor breathing before, during and after surgery and sedation, and overnight to screen for sleep apnoea. The reading, SpO2, is an estimate of arterial oxygen saturation; it is not the same as the oxygen content of the blood or the adequacy of breathing, and it does not measure carbon dioxide.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    Oxygen saturation (SpO2)95-100% is the usual normal range in healthy adults; 88-92% is the oxygen target for people at risk of carbon dioxide retention, such as those with COPD (British Thoracic Society). The percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen, estimated from the ratio of red to infrared light absorbed by pulsing arterial blood. People with long-term lung disease may run a lower baseline even when well, so their usual reading, agreed with their clinician, is the comparison that matters. Oxygen can be low without any feeling of breathlessness.
    Arterial oxygen (PaO2)The pressure of dissolved oxygen; below 8 kPa on air is the usual definition of hypoxaemic (type 1) respiratory failure.
    Arterial carbon dioxide (PaCO2)High when ventilation is inadequate (type 2 respiratory failure); low with hyperventilation.
    pH and bicarbonateAcid-base status: respiratory or metabolic acidosis and alkalosis.
    LactateRises when tissues are starved of oxygen; a key sepsis marker.
    Pulse rateThe oximeter also displays the pulse detected at the fingertip; a steady, clearly detected pulse signal is a check that the saturation reading can be trusted.

    Why it is ordered

    • Any breathless or unwell patient (oximetry is a vital sign)
    • Pneumonia, COVID-19, asthma attacks, COPD flares
    • Monitoring oxygen therapy and ventilation
    • Suspected sepsis, diabetic ketoacidosis, poisoning (blood gas)
    • Sleep apnoea screening overnight
    • Home monitoring of people with COVID-19 or other acute respiratory illness who are well enough to stay at home
    • Checking that a lung medicine or oxygen therapy is delivering the intended effect
    • Before, during and after surgery and sedation

    How it is done

    The probe reads within seconds; a blood gas needs a needle in the radial artery at the wrist, which stings briefly, with pressure afterwards. For an accurate oximetry reading the person rests for a few minutes beforehand, sits with the hand warm and relaxed, and the clip is placed on a finger free of nail varnish or false nails; the number is recorded once it has stopped changing for several seconds. Home monitoring programmes ask people to take readings at set times and to write them down with their pulse and how they feel, with clear instructions on which readings should prompt a call for help. Devices sold for home use should carry a CE, UKCA or CE UKNI mark.

    Preparation. Warm hands and remove nail varnish or false nails for oximetry; rest for at least five minutes before taking a home reading.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    Low SpO2 on airLow oxygen saturation: needs urgent assessment (call 999 in an emergency, otherwise NHS 111 or the GP the same day); clinicians give oxygen to a target range, which is lower (88 to 92%) for people at risk of carbon dioxide retention
    Reading lower than the person's usual baselineDeterioration in someone with chronic lung disease even if the absolute number is one others would tolerate; compared with the target range agreed with their clinician
    Normal saturation with severe symptomsCollapse, confusion, new drowsiness or marked breathlessness need urgent care (call 999) regardless of the number; the oximeter does not measure carbon dioxide or how hard the person is working to breathe
    Erratic reading with a poor pulse signalCold hands, movement, poor circulation or a loose probe; warm the hand, keep still and repeat before acting on the value
    High PaCO2 with low pHAcute type 2 respiratory failure; may need ventilatory support
    Low pH, low bicarbonate, high lactateMetabolic acidosis from sepsis or poor perfusion

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Oximetry misreads with cold hands, poor circulation, nail varnish, movement, carbon monoxide poisoning and, to a degree, darker skin; it says nothing about carbon dioxide. A normal saturation does not mean breathing is adequate: a person can maintain their oxygen level by breathing hard until they tire, and someone retaining carbon dioxide may have a reassuring saturation while becoming drowsy. A weak pulse signal corrupts the reading, and the device estimates saturation rather than measuring oxygen directly, so when the result will change management or a carbon dioxide level is needed, an arterial blood gas is taken. Home readings are only as good as the technique and the device, which is why rest, warm hands, no nail varnish and a certified oximeter are stressed.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Obstructive sleep apnoea Obstructive sleep apnoea is a condition in which the throat narrows or closes during sleep, so breathing stops and starts again.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…
