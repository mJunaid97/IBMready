    Spirometry

    Also known as: breathing test, lung function test

    A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast. It is the test that diagnoses and grades asthma and COPD by separating airways that are narrowed (obstruction) from lungs that are stiff or small (restriction). The spirometer records the volume of air exhaled against time, starting from a maximal breath in, and draws two curves from it: volume against time and flow against volume. From these come the volume blown out in the first second (FEV1), the total volume blown out (FVC) and their ratio, each compared with the value predicted for a person of the same age, sex, height and ethnic background. A result is compared with the normal range for that person: many laboratories use the lower limit of normal, while UK COPD guidance uses a fixed post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio of 0.7. Spirometry is also repeated after an inhaled bronchodilator to see whether the obstruction reverses, which helps separate asthma from COPD, and it is used to follow a known lung disease over time. The manoeuvre is standardised by the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society, and the test is only as good as the effort and technique that go into it.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    FEV1The volume blown out in the first second; reduced when airways are narrowed. It is the main measure used to grade the severity of obstruction and to follow change over time.
    FVCForced vital capacity, the total volume blown out; reduced when lungs are small or stiff, or when air is trapped behind narrowed airways.
    FEV1/FVC ratioIn UK COPD guidance a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC below 0.7, with typical symptoms, confirms airflow obstruction; many laboratories also report a lower limit of normal. A low ratio means air leaves the lungs slowly relative to their size; a normal or high ratio with a low FVC suggests restriction.
    ReversibilityFEV1 rising by at least 12% and 200 mL after a bronchodilator supports asthma but is also seen in COPD; it is read with the history and other tests. The test is repeated after inhaling a bronchodilator and the change in FEV1 is measured.
    Peak expiratory flowThe fastest flow, measured at home on a hand-held meter to track asthma control.
    Flow-volume curve shapeThe shape of the curve of flow against volume helps distinguish an obstructive pattern from a restrictive one.

    Why it is ordered

    • Breathlessness, wheeze or chronic cough
    • Smokers over 35 with symptoms (COPD screening)
    • Asthma diagnosis and monitoring
    • Before lung or major surgery
    • Occupational lung disease surveillance
    • Measuring how well treatment is controlling a known lung disease, and detecting decline over time
    • Confirming or excluding an obstructive pattern before a COPD or asthma diagnosis is recorded

    How it is done

    Take the deepest breath possible and blast it out as hard and long as possible through a mouthpiece, repeated three times; 15-30 minutes with a bronchodilator test. The person sits upright with a nose clip on, seals their lips around the mouthpiece, fills the lungs completely and then empties them as forcefully and completely as they can while the operator coaches them to keep going. The manoeuvre is repeated until at least three technically acceptable blows have been obtained whose best values agree closely with each other, within a limited number of attempts, so that the result reflects the lungs rather than a poor effort. If reversibility is being tested, a bronchodilator is inhaled and the blows are repeated after a short wait. Results are reported as measured values, as a percentage of predicted and against the lower limit of normal, and they are interpreted with the clinical picture, never in isolation; depending on the findings, a chest X-ray, CT scan or a fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) test may follow.

    Preparation. Avoid inhalers, big meals, alcohol and vigorous exercise before the test as instructed. The department advises which inhalers to withhold and for how long so that reversibility can be assessed, and the person's height, age and sex are recorded accurately because the predicted values depend on them.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    FEV1/FVC below 0.7, poor reversibilityCOPD; severity graded by FEV1 as % predicted
    Obstruction that reverses with salbutamolSupports asthma
    Low FVC with normal ratioRestrictive pattern: fibrosis, chest wall or muscle disease, obesity; full lung function tests next
    Normal spirometry with suggestive symptomsDoes not exclude asthma, which varies over time; peak-flow diary, FeNO or repeat testing when symptomatic is used instead
    Technically unacceptable or non-reproducible blowsThe result cannot be interpreted; the test is repeated with coaching or referred to a specialist laboratory
    Falling FEV1 on serial testingProgression of the lung disease or poor control; prompts a review of treatment

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Depends on effort and technique; normal spirometry between attacks does not exclude asthma. Not safe soon after a heart attack, eye surgery or pneumothorax. Results are compared with reference values derived from populations, so the choice of reference equations and the handling of ethnicity affect who is labelled abnormal, and predicted values are less reliable at the extremes of age and height. Spirometry cannot measure the air left in the lungs after a full breath out or how well gas crosses into the blood, so a restrictive pattern needs full lung function tests to confirm, and it cannot by itself establish the cause of an obstructive pattern. Poor-quality tests are common when operators are not trained and quality-assured, and over-reading them leads to misdiagnosis in both directions.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Medical tests

    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…

    Guides and news

    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • Asthma in children: signs and NHS advice Asthma in children brings wheeze, cough and a tight chest; the NHS says to call 999 if the reliever is not helping during an attack.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
    • Spirometry: the lung function test explained Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast.
    • Stopping smoking: the support the NHS offers NHS stop smoking services offer nicotine replacement, medicines and professional support.
