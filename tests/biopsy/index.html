    Biopsy

    Also known as: tissue sample, histology, needle biopsy

    Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope. It is the only way to prove most cancers and many inflammatory diseases, and it grades and types a tumour so that treatment can be planned. A biopsy is usually arranged when imaging, blood tests or a screening test have found something that cannot be explained without looking at the tissue itself: a lump, a shadow, abnormal cells or an inflamed organ. It is almost always done in hospital, most often as a day case under local anaesthetic, and the sample goes to a pathology laboratory where it is processed and examined under the microscope by a pathologist.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    HistologyThe architecture of the tissue: normal, inflamed, precancerous or malignant, and which type. A normal result means the tissue looks healthy; an abnormal result means the cells or tissue have an unusual structure, shape, size or condition, which may be cancer or another disease depending on the site and the question asked.
    GradeHow abnormal the cells look compared with the normal tissue they came from, which predicts how aggressively a cancer is likely to behave and helps decide how intensively it is treated.
    Immunohistochemistry and molecular markersProtein and gene tests on the sample (HER2, EGFR, PD-L1) that decide targeted treatment. A core or surgical sample gives more tissue for these tests than a fine-needle aspirate, which can show that cells are malignant but may not be able to name the exact type or subtype.
    MarginsFor excision biopsies, whether abnormal tissue reaches the edge of what was removed; a clear margin suggests the lesion has been taken out completely.
    AdequacyWhether enough representative tissue was obtained. A fine-needle sample sometimes contains too few cells to answer the question, and the report says so rather than guessing.

    Why it is ordered

    • A lump, mass or shadow on imaging
    • Abnormal cells on a screening test
    • Unexplained organ inflammation (liver, kidney, muscle, skin)
    • Staging lymph nodes
    • Monitoring transplant rejection
    • Working out what is causing a symptom when imaging and blood tests cannot explain it
    • Confirming or excluding infection such as tuberculosis in tissue or the lining of the lung

    How it is done

    Depends on the site. Needle biopsies pass a hollow needle through the skin into the area, usually with ultrasound, CT or MRI guidance so the needle lands exactly on the target: a fine-needle aspiration uses a thin needle on a syringe to draw off a small number of cells from organs or lumps under the skin, while a core biopsy uses a wider spring-loaded needle to cut out slivers of tissue, which gives the pathologist more to work with. Skin lesions are sampled with a punch or shave that removes the top layers. During an endoscopy, small cutting forceps passed through the scope take samples from the lining of the gut, airway or bladder. An excision biopsy removes a whole lump or area surgically. Most biopsies are done awake under local anaesthetic, sometimes with sedation; some, particularly in children or deep organs, use a general anaesthetic. The procedure itself is often well under an hour. People commonly feel the sting of the anaesthetic injection, pressure as the sample is taken and a loud click from a spring-loaded needle. After sedation or general anaesthetic a period of observation follows, but most people go home the same day. Soreness and bruising at the site are common and settle within about a week. The sample is examined by a pathologist under the microscope, with extra stains or molecular tests as needed; results commonly take from several days to a few weeks depending on the hospital and the tests required, and are discussed with the person by the team that requested them.

    Preparation. The team explains which regular medicines to continue. Blood tests may be taken beforehand to check bleeding risk, and anticoagulant or antiplatelet drugs may need to be paused for several days; clotting is checked before liver and kidney biopsies. Fasting for a few hours is often requested, particularly if sedation or a general anaesthetic is planned, although clear fluids are usually allowed. A consent form is signed after the risks have been explained. Afterwards the dressing is kept clean and dry and strenuous exercise is avoided for a day or so.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    BenignNo cancer; the tissue may still explain the symptoms by showing inflammation, infection or a non-cancerous growth, and the report usually names the specific benign process
    DysplasiaPrecancerous change in the cells; treated or kept under surveillance depending on its degree and the site
    Malignant, with type and gradeCancer confirmed; the type, grade and marker results feed into staging with imaging and a multidisciplinary team discussion that plans treatment
    Abnormal but not malignantCells or tissue with an unusual structure, shape or size that point to a disease other than cancer, such as an inflammatory, infective or metabolic process; the meaning depends on the organ sampled
    Non-diagnostic or inconclusiveToo little tissue or too few cells to answer the question, most often after a fine-needle sample; repeated with a wider needle, an image-guided core or a surgical biopsy

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    A biopsy samples a tiny fraction of an organ and can miss a lesion that lies a few millimetres away, so a benign result from a needle sample is weighed against how suspicious the imaging looks and may be followed by repeat or surgical sampling. Fine-needle aspirates can confirm malignancy but may not identify the exact cancer type, which matters for planning treatment. Results take time, because tissue must be processed before it can be read, and further marker tests add to the wait. The main risks are infection at the site and bleeding, which is why clotting is checked and blood thinners are paused for deep organs; a lung biopsy carries a small risk of a collapsed lung (pneumothorax).

    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Bladder cancer Bladder cancer is cancer that forms in the tissues of the bladder, the organ that stores urine.

    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • Cervical screening (smear test) A sample of cells brushed from the transformation zone of the cervix.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Guides and news

    • Bleeding after menopause or between periods Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be checked by a GP.
    • Bone marrow biopsy: what happens and why A bone marrow biopsy takes a small sample of marrow, usually from the back of the hip bone.
    • Found a breast lump: what happens next Most breast lumps are harmless, but the NHS advises seeing a GP about any new lump so it can be checked.
    • Colonoscopy: preparation and what happens A colonoscopy uses a thin camera to look inside the large bowel.
    • Cystoscopy: what happens and what to expect A cystoscopy uses a thin camera passed through the urethra to look inside the bladder.
    • Gastroscopy: what happens and what to expect A gastroscopy uses a thin camera to look at the gullet and stomach.
    • Lumbar puncture: what happens and what to expect A lumbar puncture uses a needle in the lower back to collect the fluid around the spinal cord, and the pressure of the fluid may also be measured.
