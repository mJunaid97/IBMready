    Biopsy

    Also known as: tissue sample, histology, needle biopsy

    Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope. It is the only way to prove most cancers and many inflammatory diseases, and it grades and types a tumour so that treatment can be planned.

    What it measures

    MeasureWhat it tells you
    HistologyThe architecture of the tissue: normal, inflamed, precancerous or malignant, and which type.
    GradeHow abnormal the cells look, which predicts how aggressively a cancer behaves.
    Immunohistochemistry and molecular markersProtein and gene tests on the sample (HER2, EGFR, PD-L1) that decide targeted treatment.
    MarginsFor excision biopsies, whether abnormal tissue reaches the edge.

    Why it is ordered

    • A lump, mass or shadow on imaging
    • Abnormal cells on a screening test
    • Unexplained organ inflammation (liver, kidney, muscle, skin)
    • Staging lymph nodes
    • Monitoring transplant rejection

    How it is done

    Depends on the site: a needle through the skin guided by ultrasound or CT, forceps through an endoscope, a punch or shave of the skin, or surgical removal. Local anaesthetic for most; results in days to two weeks.

    Preparation. Blood thinners may be paused; clotting checked for liver and kidney biopsies.

    Reading the result

    FindingUsual meaning
    BenignNo cancer; may still explain symptoms (inflammation, infection)
    DysplasiaPrecancerous change; treat or survey
    Malignant, with type and gradeCancer confirmed; staged with imaging and discussed by a multidisciplinary team
    Non-diagnosticToo little tissue; repeat

    Reference ranges vary by laboratory, method, age, sex and clinical context; a result is read against the person, not a table.

    Limitations

    Samples a tiny fraction of an organ and can miss a lesion; small bleeding and infection risks, higher for deep organs.
