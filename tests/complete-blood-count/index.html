    Full blood count

    Also known as: complete blood count, blood count

    The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample, with the size and haemoglobin content of the red cells.

    FBC, CBC
    Kind

    Categories
    Blood & haematology
    Specimen
    Whole blood (EDTA)
    Method
    Automated cell counter
    Terminology
    LOINC 58410-2, 57021-8
    Type
    Blood test
    Sample
    Blood from a vein
    Used for
    General health, anaemia, infection, blood disorders, monitoring
    Measures
    Blood cells and related indices: red cells, white cells and platelets
    Result
    A panel of numeric results, each with its own laboratory reference interval, plus flags for the differential

    Why is it used?

    • Tiredness, pallor or breathlessness (anaemia)
    • Suspected infection or inflammation
    • Unexplained bruising or bleeding
    • Monitoring chemotherapy and many long-term drugs
    • Routine pre-operative check

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    A few millilitres of blood from an arm vein into a tube with an anticoagulant; results within hours.

    Specimen. Venous blood in an EDTA (anticoagulant) tube

    Do I need to prepare?

    None.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    Low haemoglobin with small red cellsIron deficiency, usually from blood loss (gut, periods) or poor intake
    High neutrophilsBacterial infection, inflammation, steroids or stress
    Low plateletsImmune destruction, marrow problems, liver disease, some drugs
    Everything low (pancytopenia)Bone marrow failure or infiltration, needs urgent specialist review
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. Every component has a reference interval that depends on the analyser, age, sex, pregnancy and ethnic background; children's values differ markedly from adults'. The report flags values outside the laboratory's own interval, and a flagged value is a prompt for interpretation, not a diagnosis.

    What can affect the result?

    • Pregnancy, dehydration and altitude change red-cell values
    • Recent infection, exercise, smoking, stress and steroids change white cells
    • Clumping, clotting or delay in the tube gives spurious platelet and cell counts
    • Ethnic background (benign ethnic neutropenia)
    • Recent transfusion, iron infusion or bleeding

    Medicines. Chemotherapy, methotrexate, carbimazole, clozapine and many other medicines lower counts; corticosteroids raise neutrophils and lower lymphocytes and eosinophils; heparin can lower platelets.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • The cause of an abnormal count: it shows that something has changed, and further tests (iron studies, B12 and folate, a blood film, sometimes marrow examination) show why
    • Whether platelets are working properly (only their number)
    • Whether a normal count excludes disease that has not yet affected the blood

    Tells you that something is wrong, not what: an abnormal count is a prompt for further tests such as iron studies, a blood film or marrow examination.

    Conditions

    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.
    • HIV test A blood test that looks for the human immunodeficiency virus.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Biomarkers

    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • Lymphocytes The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells).
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Clopidogrel An antiplatelet medicine that stops platelets clumping by blocking their P2Y12 receptor, taken to prevent clots after a heart attack, stent.
    • Hydroxychloroquine Hydroxychloroquine is a medicine used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and some skin conditions.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Methotrexate Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Guides and news

    • Preparing for a blood test: fasting explained Some blood tests need fasting or a change to what you eat, drink or take beforehand; the person who arranges the test should say which applies.
    • Blood transfusion: why and how it is done A blood transfusion gives red blood cells, plasma or platelets from a donor to someone who has lost blood or has a condition affecting their blood.
    • Bone marrow biopsy: what happens and why A bone marrow biopsy takes a small sample of marrow, usually from the back of the hip bone.
    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • High white blood cell count: what it means A high white blood cell count is a non-specific sign, most often from infection, and doctors read it with the type of cell raised.
    • Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot.
    • Low ferritin: what it means and what next A low ferritin level means the body's iron stores are low, and doctors then look for the cause, which is often blood loss.
    • Pregnancy scans and tests on the NHS NHS pregnancy care includes booking blood tests, a dating scan and a mid-pregnancy anomaly scan, plus optional screening for Down's.
    • Tiredness and fatigue: causes and next steps Tiredness is common and often has an ordinary cause such as poor sleep, stress or illness.
    • Unexplained weight loss: causes and next steps Losing weight without changing diet or exercise has many causes, from stress to thyroid or digestive conditions.
