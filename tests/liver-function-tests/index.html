    Liver function tests

    Also known as: liver panel, liver blood tests

    A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing, and whether the liver is still doing its manufacturing job.

    Jaundice, abdominal symptoms, alcohol, fatty liver, drug monitoring
    Measures
    Liver enzymes, bilirubin and albumin: injury, bile flow and synthesis
    Result
    A panel of numeric values, each with a laboratory reference interval; the pattern across the panel matters more than any single value

    Why is it used?

    • Jaundice, dark urine, pale stools
    • Abdominal pain, nausea, itching
    • Heavy alcohol use or viral hepatitis risk
    • Monitoring statins, methotrexate, antiepileptics and many other drugs
    • Fatty liver in obesity and diabetes

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations; the result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    How is it performed?

    Venous blood.

    Specimen. Venous blood (serum)

    Do I need to prepare?

    None, though fasting improves consistency for some labs.

    What do the results generally mean?

    FindingWhat it may indicate
    ALT and AST much higher than ALPHepatocellular injury: viral hepatitis, drugs, fatty liver, alcohol
    ALP and GGT high with raised bilirubinCholestasis: gallstone in the bile duct, pancreatic tumour, some drugs
    Low albumin and high INRLoss of synthetic function: cirrhosis or acute liver failure
    Isolated mildly raised bilirubinOften Gilbert syndrome, a harmless inherited variation
    Read against the laboratory's own reference interval. Each enzyme has its own laboratory reference interval that varies by method, sex and age (alkaline phosphatase is naturally high in growing children). A mild elevation of one enzyme is common and often needs only a repeat; the pattern (hepatocellular versus cholestatic), the size of the rise and the trend guide what happens next.

    What can affect the result?

    • Alcohol in the preceding days and weeks
    • Obesity and fatty liver, which cause the commonest mild abnormalities
    • Strenuous exercise and muscle injury (AST, and to a lesser extent ALT)
    • Pregnancy (ALP), growth in childhood, fasting (bilirubin in Gilbert syndrome)
    • Many medicines and herbal supplements

    Medicines. Statins, paracetamol in overdose, antibiotics such as flucloxacillin and co-amoxiclav, antiepileptics, methotrexate, antifungals, antituberculous drugs and some herbal products can raise liver enzymes; oral contraceptives and anabolic steroids can cause cholestasis.

    What can the test not tell you?

    • How much scarring (fibrosis) the liver has: a normal panel does not exclude cirrhosis, and fibrosis is assessed with scores such as FIB-4, elastography or biopsy
    • The cause of liver injury without further tests (viral serology, autoimmune antibodies, iron studies, ultrasound)
    • True 'function' in most cases: the enzymes show injury, and only albumin and clotting reflect synthesis

    Despite the name, they mostly detect damage rather than measure function; a normal panel does not exclude cirrhosis. Muscle injury raises AST, bone growth raises ALP.

    Conditions

    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Fatty liver disease (MASLD) A common condition in which fat builds up in the liver, often linked to being overweight.
    • Gender dysphoria (gender incongruence) The distress that can arise when a person's gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Hepatitis B tests A set of blood tests that shows whether someone has hepatitis B now, had it in the past, is immune from vaccination, or carries the virus long term.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Biomarkers

    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) A membrane enzyme of bile-duct and liver cells that rises with cholestasis, alcohol and many medicines.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • INR The prothrombin time expressed as a standardised ratio, the number used to dose warfarin and to gauge the liver's production of clotting factors.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Ezetimibe Ezetimibe is a prescribed cholesterol-lowering medicine that works by reducing how much cholesterol the gut absorbs.
    • Methotrexate Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Simvastatin Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes.

    Guides and news

    • Alcohol units: the UK low-risk drinking advice The UK Chief Medical Officers advise adults not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.
    • Liver blood tests: what the results mean Liver blood tests can point towards a possible problem but do not name the cause, and results that are not normal are very common.
    • Swollen ankles and legs: causes and red flags Swollen ankles and legs often come from sitting or standing for long periods, but can signal a clot or a heart, kidney or liver problem.
    Educational content. Reference ranges differ between laboratories, methods, ages and sexes, and results are always read against the person, the question asked and other findings. This page cannot interpret an individual result.
