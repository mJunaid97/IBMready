    Altitude sickness: symptoms, serious forms and how the NHS says to prevent it

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.

    Key points

    • The NHS lists headache, loss of appetite, feeling or being sick, tiredness, dizziness and difficulty sleeping as symptoms of altitude sickness.
    • The NHS says altitude sickness usually gets better with rest, and that going to a lower altitude is advised if symptoms do not improve.
    • The NHS describes two serious forms: swelling of the brain and a build-up of fluid in the lungs, both caused by low oxygen.
    • Going up slowly gives the body time to get used to lower oxygen levels, which is the main prevention measure the NHS describes.
    • TravelHealthPro says the key to preventing altitude illness is gradual ascent with regular rest days, and that medicines may be used to help in some people.

    What is altitude sickness?

    Altitude sickness is illness that can affect people who go to a high place, where the air holds less oxygen than at lower levels. It is also called acute mountain sickness. The NHS says it is usually mild and eases with rest, but it can become serious. TravelHealthPro, the UK travel health service, describes three altitude illnesses: acute mountain sickness, high-altitude cerebral oedema and high-altitude pulmonary oedema. The cerebral form is swelling of the brain, and the pulmonary form is a build-up of fluid in the lungs; both are caused by a lack of oxygen.

    What symptoms does altitude sickness cause?

    The NHS lists a headache, loss of appetite, feeling or being sick, tiredness, dizziness and difficulty sleeping. The NHS says these symptoms usually start 6 to 10 hours after reaching a high altitude. They usually get better within one to three days, according to the NHS. Headache, nausea and dizziness are symptoms with many other causes, so the list does not by itself show that altitude is the reason.

    What are the serious forms, in plain words?

    The NHS describes high-altitude cerebral oedema as swelling of the brain caused by a lack of oxygen. It describes high-altitude pulmonary oedema as a build-up of fluid in the lungs. Warning signs listed by the NHS include confusion, problems with balance or coordination, and hallucinations, meaning seeing or hearing things that are not there. For the lungs, the NHS lists breathlessness at rest and a cough with frothy or bloody spit. NHS 111 Wales adds blue or grey lips, tongue or nails, and being very sleepy or hard to wake. TravelHealthPro says brain or lung symptoms of this kind need immediate descent and emergency medical treatment.

    What does the NHS say about rest and going down?

    The NHS says altitude sickness usually gets better without treatment if the person rests. NHS 111 Wales advises staying at the same altitude until feeling better, and not travelling or climbing higher in the meantime. The NHS says that if symptoms get worse or do not improve after a day, going to a lower altitude is advised if possible, around 300 to 1,000 metres lower. TravelHealthPro says anyone with symptoms of altitude illness should avoid going higher.

    How can altitude sickness be prevented?

    The main measure described by the NHS is to travel or climb to a high altitude slowly, giving the body time to get used to the lower oxygen levels. The NHS gives an example for people already at 3,000 metres or more: a rest day at the same altitude every three to four days. TravelHealthPro likewise says gradual ascent with regular rest days is the key to prevention. NHS 111 Wales also advises against drinking alcohol while travelling or climbing.

    Can medicine help prevent altitude illness?

    TravelHealthPro says medicines may be used to help prevent altitude illness in certain individuals. The medicine it names is acetazolamide. It presents this as an aid to acclimatisation, meaning the body adjusting to the height, and not as a substitute for going up gradually. TravelHealthPro lists dosing details on its own page, which this guide does not repeat. Whether any medicine suits a particular person is a question for a doctor or travel clinic before the trip.

    When is urgent help needed?

    The NHS says to get medical help immediately if someone at a high altitude has symptoms of altitude sickness and feels very unwell. The warning signs on that list include confusion, problems with balance or coordination, hallucinations, breathlessness at rest and a cough with frothy or bloody spit. NHS 111 Wales also lists being very sleepy or hard to wake. TravelHealthPro describes brain and lung altitude illness as needing immediate descent and emergency medical treatment.
