    Lowering blood pressure with lifestyle changes: what the NHS and UK charities list

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS advises everyone with high blood pressure to make lifestyle changes, such as eating less salt, being more active and cutting back on alcohol.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS says everyone with high blood pressure is advised to make healthy lifestyle changes, with or without medicine.
    • The NHS gives a limit of no more than 6g of salt a day for adults, and much of the salt people eat is already in everyday foods.
    • Blood Pressure UK advises at least five portions of fruit and vegetables a day, partly because they contain potassium.
    • The NHS also lists regular activity, losing weight if overweight, keeping alcohol low and stopping smoking.

    Do lifestyle changes matter if medicine is offered?

    The NHS says everyone with high blood pressure is advised to make healthy lifestyle changes. Whether medicine is needed as well depends on the blood pressure readings and on the person's risk of heart attack or stroke. NICE publishes a patient decision aid for adults, titled How do I control my blood pressure? Lifestyle options and choice of medicines.

    The NHS lists the main changes as eating less salt, eating a healthy balanced diet, cutting back on alcohol and caffeine, being more active, losing weight if overweight and stopping smoking.

    What do the sources say about salt?

    The NHS advises adults to have no more than 6g of salt a day. The NHS says much of the salt people eat is already in everyday foods such as bread, breakfast cereal and ready meals. Blood Pressure UK says most people eat almost twice the recommended six grams a day.

    The NHS salt page lists foods that are high in salt, such as bacon, cheese, stock cubes, soy sauce and salted nuts. When a label lists only sodium, multiplying the sodium figure by 2.5 gives the amount of salt. NICE recommends that clinicians encourage people to keep their dietary sodium intake low. Blood Pressure UK says salt substitutes made from potassium chloride can be high in potassium, so people with kidney problems or diabetes are advised to check with a doctor or nurse first.

    What about fruit, vegetables and potassium?

    Blood Pressure UK advises at least five portions of fruit and vegetables every day. It says they also contain potassium, which can help to balance the effects of salt. Canned, frozen and dried fruit and vegetables also count, and the charity advises choosing canned products without added salt or sugar. The British Heart Foundation explains that potassium helps the kidneys remove sodium from the blood and pass it out in urine.

    The British Heart Foundation describes the DASH diet as a plan that trims sodium while raising potassium, calcium and fibre. It contains plenty of fruit, vegetables, wholegrains and low-fat dairy products, and is low in salt, saturated fat, sugary foods and alcohol. The British Heart Foundation advises getting potassium through food rather than supplements, as too much can be harmful.

    How do alcohol, weight and activity fit in?

    The NHS advises against regularly drinking more than 14 units of alcohol a week, and says to spread drinking over three days or more. The NHS lists losing weight, if overweight, among the changes that can help lower blood pressure. The NHS advice is to be active regularly, with at least 150 minutes of exercise a week, and says moderate activity is enough.

    What else do the NHS pages mention?

    The NHS also lists cutting back on caffeine and stopping smoking among the changes that can help. NHS dietitian fact sheets say supplements of calcium, magnesium or potassium are not recommended as a way to lower blood pressure. Sources do not all give the same figures, so the NHS pages are the ones used here for limits on salt and alcohol.
