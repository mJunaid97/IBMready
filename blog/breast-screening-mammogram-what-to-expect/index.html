    Breast screening: who is invited and what happens

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    NHS breast screening uses mammograms to look for breast cancer in people without symptoms. This guide covers who is invited, the appointment, results and harms.

    Open in the 3D explorerBreasts anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS offers breast screening from age 50 up to the 71st birthday, with invitations every three years.
    • A mammogram takes two X-rays of each breast, with each breast pressed between two plates.
    • Breast screening is offered to people without symptoms to look for breast cancer, and it does not prevent breast cancer.
    • Screening can miss a cancer and can raise false alarms, and some people are diagnosed with a cancer that would never have caused harm.

    Who is invited for breast screening?

    The NHS says screening is offered from age 50 up to the 71st birthday. The first invitation usually comes between the ages of 50 and 53, and further invitations follow every three years until the 71st birthday. Anyone registered with a GP as female is invited automatically. People over 71 are not invited automatically but can ask for screening every three years. For trans and non-binary people, eligibility for breast screening depends on the sex registered with the GP and on any hormone treatment or surgery, so NHS guidance advises checking with the GP or local screening service.

    What happens at the appointment?

    A mammogram takes two X-rays of each breast. Each breast is pressed between two plates, which some people find uncomfortable or painful, but any discomfort should not last long. NHS pages say the mammogram itself takes only a few minutes and the whole appointment should take no longer than 30 minutes.

    How and when are the results given?

    Results usually come within two weeks of the appointment and are also sent to the GP. If the mammogram needs a closer look, the person is invited back to an assessment clinic for further tests. A Royal Marsden page says about 4 in 100 women are asked to come back, and about three in four of those have normal results after further tests.

    What are the benefits?

    Breast screening is offered to people without symptoms, using mammograms to look for breast cancer. It does not prevent breast cancer.

    What are the harms and limits?

    Screening does not always find a cancer that is there, so some people with breast cancer are missed. Some people are asked back for more tests and then found not to have cancer, and the follow-up tests can be invasive and time-consuming. Some people who are screened are diagnosed and treated for a breast cancer that would never have been found or caused them harm.

    How is it presented as a choice?

    The NHS leaflet 'Helping you decide' describes breast screening as a personal choice and sets out its benefits and risks. It says screening does not prevent breast cancer from developing. Overdiagnosis means finding a cancer that would never have caused harm, and estimates of how often it happens vary between studies. A 2012 independent review estimated that among 10,000 UK women invited to screening from age 50 for 20 years, about 43 breast cancer deaths would be prevented and 129 cancers would be overdiagnosed, although other estimates differ.

    What if there is a lump between screens?

    Cancer Research UK says anyone who notices changes to their nipples, chest area or armpits should not wait for screening and should tell their doctor.
