    Alcohol units: the UK low-risk drinking advice

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The UK Chief Medical Officers advise adults not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy

    Key points

    • The UK Chief Medical Officers advise that adults should not regularly drink more than 14 units of alcohol a week.
    • The guidance says there is no completely safe level of regular drinking; 14 units a week is described as a low-risk level, not a safe one.
    • One unit is 10 millilitres, or 8 grams, of pure alcohol, and the strength printed on a label as ABV can be used to work out units.
    • For anyone who is pregnant or could become pregnant, the Chief Medical Officers advise that the safest approach is not to drink alcohol at all.

    What is the UK low-risk drinking advice?

    The UK Chief Medical Officers advise that, to keep the risk of harm from alcohol low, adults should not regularly drink more than 14 units a week. For someone who drinks as much as 14 units, the advice is to spread it evenly over three days or more. The guidance also says there is no completely safe level of regular drinking, so 14 units is a way to keep risk low rather than a safe amount. Some older leaflets still quote daily limits, but the weekly figure is the one in the current guidance.

    What is an alcohol unit?

    One unit is 10 millilitres of pure alcohol, which is 8 grams. The strength of a drink is shown on the label as alcohol by volume, or ABV. Units can be worked out by multiplying the volume in millilitres by the ABV percentage and dividing by 1,000. Using that method, a pint of strong lager at 5.2% ABV comes to about three units. As a guide, 14 units is about six pints of average-strength beer or six medium glasses of average-strength wine.

    What does drinking above the guidance do to health?

    The Chief Medical Officers' guidance says regular drinking above the low-risk level raises the risk of dying from long-term illnesses and from accidents and injuries, and alcohol is a risk factor for seven types of cancer. The guidance also names accidents and injuries among the short-term risks of heavy drinking. Drinking a large amount in a short time carries a particular risk of alcohol poisoning. Mouth cancers are more common among people who drink, and the risk is higher still when drinking is combined with smoking or any form of tobacco.

    Does keeping to 14 units remove the risk?

    The 2016 review behind the guidance estimated that the lifetime risk of dying from an alcohol-related condition is around 1 per cent, or a little under, for people drinking at the low-risk level. Heavier drinking raises the risk further. The guidance is therefore described as a way to keep risk low, not to remove it.

    Who is advised not to drink at all?

    For anyone who is pregnant or thinks they could become pregnant, the Chief Medical Officers advise that the safest approach is not to drink alcohol at all.

    When might someone need support with drinking?

    Signs listed as a reason to seek support include drinking more over time, struggling to cut down, poor sleep, thinking ahead to the next drink, risky behaviour and concern from other people.

    How does the NHS suggest cutting down?

    The NHS Better Health site points people to their local alcohol support service, or to Drinkline on 0300 123 1110, which it describes as free, confidential help for anyone concerned about their own or someone else's drinking. It also offers the NHS Drink Free Days app for setting a weekly unit target. NHS pages describe a drinks diary as a way to see how much is being drunk, in which situations, and how cutting down could start. Having several alcohol-free days each week is another suggestion, as are choosing lower-strength drinks and alternating alcoholic drinks with soft drinks.

    What treatment exists for alcohol dependence?

    The NHS says treatment for alcohol use disorder is available from a GP or a specialist alcohol service. Options it lists include counselling, self-help groups, talking therapies such as CBT, and medicines. Alcoholics Anonymous and SMART Recovery are named as self-help groups, and Al-Anon Family Groups as support for families and friends of people who drink. The NHS says a person who is not ready to stop, or who only wants to cut down, will be supported to reduce drinking to a safer level. The NHS says it can be very dangerous to stop drinking suddenly for someone who is dependent on alcohol, and that anyone who gets withdrawal symptoms should get medical help before trying to stop; for severe symptoms such as confusion, hallucinations or a seizure it advises calling 999 or going to A&E.
