    Supporting someone with a mental health problem

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Listening, asking open questions and offering practical help are the NHS's main suggestions for supporting someone with a mental health problem, without forcing them to talk.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS suggests telling the person you are worried, carrying on as usual, and offering to listen.
    • Open questions that start with how, what, where or when give the person room to answer in their own words.
    • The NHS advises against forcing someone to talk or to get help.
    • If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 999; NHS 111 (mental health option) and Samaritans on 116 123 offer urgent support.

    How can you start the conversation and help?

    The NHS suggests telling the person that you are worried about them. It also suggests carrying on as usual, so the person is not treated differently because of what they are going through. Open questions that start with how, what, where or when are recommended. The NHS says not to force someone to talk to you. The NHS gives going with the person to professional appointments as an example of practical help.

    How can you point someone towards professional help?

    For people who need more support, the NHS lists contacting a GP, calling NHS 111, or self-referring to NHS talking therapies. The NHS directs anyone who cannot cope or keep themselves safe to urgent support.

    What if the person wants to start talking?

    The NHS guidance for people talking about their own mental health suggests choosing someone they trust who can offer the kind of support they need, and planning what to say. It notes that some people find it easier to talk while walking, travelling in a car or cooking, and that a person does not have to say everything at once. If the person chosen does not feel able to help, the NHS says not to take it personally.

    Where can someone get urgent help?

    If someone is in immediate danger, or has been physically harmed, call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that is not an emergency, NHS 111 has a mental health option (option 2), which trusts describe as available at any time, for yourself or on someone else's behalf. Samaritans listen at any time of day or night, and the number, 116 123, is free to call.
