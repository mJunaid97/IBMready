    Jet lag: what it is, why it happens and what the NHS says helps

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Jet lag is a temporary sleep disturbance after long flights across time zones; the NHS says it usually improves within a few days.

    Key points

    • Jet lag is a temporary disturbance to the sleep pattern that can follow a long flight across time zones.
    • The body clock is set partly by cues such as sunrise and sunset, so it takes a few days to catch up with a new time zone.
    • Symptoms include trouble sleeping at night, waking early, daytime tiredness and poor concentration or memory.
    • The NHS suggests plenty of rest before travel, drinking water on the flight, and avoiding daytime sleep and sleeping at night after arrival.
    • The NHS jet lag page says melatonin is not recommended on the NHS because evidence is lacking, while the NHS melatonin page says it can be taken short term by adults.
    • NHS inform advises contacting a GP practice when better sleep habits have not helped or poor sleep affects daily life.

    What is jet lag?

    Jet lag is a disturbance to the normal sleep pattern that can follow a long flight across time zones. The NHS says it usually improves within a few days as the body clock adjusts, and that medicines are usually not needed.

    nidirect, the Northern Ireland health information service, describes it in the same way.

    Why does jet lag happen?

    MedlinePlus, a US health information service, notes that the body clock is set partly by cues from the environment, such as sunrise and sunset. After a long flight these cues no longer match the clock, so it takes time to adjust.

    US CDC travel guidance says jet lag is typically worse after flying east than west. It explains that eastward travel needs the body clock to move earlier, which is harder than moving it later.

    What are the symptoms of jet lag?

    The NHS lists trouble sleeping at night, waking early, tiredness during the day, and poor concentration or memory.

    These symptoms are the body clock and the local time being out of step. The NHS describes them as temporary.

    What helps before and during a flight?

    The NHS suggests getting plenty of rest before travelling and gradually moving bedtime and waking time towards the destination's schedule. MedlinePlus gives the same idea, going to bed earlier before an eastward trip and later before a westward one.

    For a short trip of two to three days, the NHS says it can be easier to eat and sleep at the times used at home.

    On the flight, the NHS advises drinking water, moving around the cabin and trying to sleep if it is night at the destination. It mentions an eye mask or earplugs if these help. nidirect says too much caffeine or alcohol can make jet lag worse.

    What helps after arriving?

    The NHS advises avoiding sleep during the day and sleeping at night. MedlinePlus recommends spending time in sunlight to help reset the body clock.

    CDC travel guidance says the best timing of light depends on the direction of travel and the number of time zones crossed. Light in the morning tends to move the body clock earlier, while light in the evening tends to move it later.

    On meals, the CDC says most dietary changes have not been found to reduce jet lag. In one study, flight crews who ate at more regular times on days off reported a small improvement in their overall jet lag rating.

    Do melatonin or sleeping tablets help?

    The NHS jet lag page says melatonin tablets are not recommended on the NHS for jet lag, because there is not enough evidence that they work.

    The NHS melatonin page takes a different tone. It says melatonin can be taken as a short-term treatment for jet lag in adults, and that it may not be available on an NHS prescription. The two NHS pages therefore differ in emphasis.

    On sleeping tablets, NHS pages say they may help if jet lag causes insomnia, but they can be addictive. They say these should be used only for a short time and only if symptoms are severe.

    When should someone get medical advice?

    The NHS says jet lag usually improves within a few days. NHS inform, in its general guidance on insomnia, advises contacting a GP practice if better sleep habits have not helped or if poor sleep is affecting daily life.

    This guidance is about sleep problems in general rather than jet lag in particular.
