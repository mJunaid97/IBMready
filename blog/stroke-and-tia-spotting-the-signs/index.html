    Stroke and TIA: spotting the signs and why 999 is the route

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A stroke and a TIA are both emergencies: the NHS and the Stroke Association say to call 999 even if the symptoms pass.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Key points

    • The FAST test checks for face drooping, arm weakness and speech problems, and the NHS says to call 999 if any sign is present.
    • A TIA, sometimes called a mini-stroke, is a temporary disruption of the blood supply to the brain, and its symptoms recover completely within 24 hours.
    • Symptoms of a TIA can pass, but the person still needs to be assessed in hospital, so call 999.
    • Treatment differs for strokes caused by a blood clot and strokes caused by bleeding, which is why a hospital scan comes first.
    • The Stroke Association runs a support helpline for people affected by stroke.

    What are the signs of a stroke?

    The FAST test is used across the UK to spot a stroke. It checks the face, the arms and the speech: has the mouth or eye drooped, can the person raise both arms fully and keep them there, and is their speech slurred or hard to understand.

    The NHS and the Stroke Association say that any one of these signs is a reason to call 999. NHS England has found that many people wait too long to call 999, with an average of 88 minutes between the first symptom and the call.

    If any one of these signs is seen, the NHS says to call 999. It also says not to wait for more symptoms to appear.

    A stroke is a medical emergency, so call 999 even if symptoms seem minor or go away.

    What is a TIA, and how does it differ from a stroke?

    A transient ischaemic attack, or TIA, is often called a mini-stroke. A TIA is a temporary disruption of the blood supply to part of the brain.

    NHS hospital guidance says the symptoms of a TIA recover completely within 24 hours. If symptoms last longer than that, it is classed as a stroke.

    The NHS and the Stroke Association say to call 999 for a suspected stroke or TIA, even if the symptoms seem minor or go away.

    A TIA is a temporary disruption of the blood supply to part of the brain.

    Why does a TIA matter if the symptoms pass?

    After a suspected TIA there is a risk of a stroke, which is why aspirin is given straight away to help prevent one.

    For this reason the NHS says a suspected TIA should be assessed urgently, even if the symptoms have gone by the time the ambulance arrives. A person whose symptoms have cleared still needs to be seen in hospital.

    What happens in hospital?

    A scan is used to find out whether a stroke is caused by a blood clot (ischaemic) or by bleeding in the brain (haemorrhagic), because the treatments differ.

    For an ischaemic stroke, emergency options include clot-busting medicine, called thrombolysis, and removal of the clot with a catheter, called thrombectomy. A stroke caused by bleeding is treated differently, with treatment aimed at stopping the bleeding and relieving pressure on the brain, sometimes with surgery.

    Hospital teams aim to scan the brain soon after symptoms begin, which is part of why calling 999 matters.

    A brain scan is done soon after symptoms begin to show whether a stroke is caused by a clot or a bleed.

    Clot-busting drugs carry a risk of bleeding, so doctors weigh the risk against the benefit for each person.

    Thrombectomy is used for clots that are too large for clot-busting drugs to break down.

    How is a suspected TIA treated?

    For a suspected TIA, the NHS says aspirin is usually given to lower the risk of a stroke, unless there is a medical reason to avoid it. The NHS says the person should be referred to a specialist within 24 hours of the start of their symptoms.

    Care after a TIA can include a blood thinner such as aspirin, a statin, and blood pressure tablets if needed. Medicines that thin the blood or reduce clotting may be used, and sometimes surgery is offered to clear a narrowed artery in the neck.

    After a TIA, treatment can include a blood thinner, a statin and blood pressure tablets if needed, and some people have surgery to unblock a narrowed neck artery.

    Where can people find support?

    The Stroke Association runs a Stroke Helpline on 0303 3033 100 for stroke survivors, their families and carers.

    A GP can explain what the diagnosis means and arrange follow-up care. In an emergency, the route is always 999.

    The Stroke Association helpline is free to use and supports stroke survivors, families and carers.
