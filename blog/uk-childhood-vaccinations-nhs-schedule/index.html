    Childhood vaccinations: the NHS schedule

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS offers babies and children vaccines from 8 weeks old through school, and a GP surgery is the contact for any dose that was missed.

    Key points

    • The NHS lists routine childhood vaccines by age, starting with the babies' visits at 8, 12 and 16 weeks.
    • Since 1 January 2026 the MMRV vaccine, which adds chickenpox protection, has been part of the routine schedule at 12 and 18 months for children born on or after 1 January 2025.
    • Older children are offered HPV in Year 8, and MenACWY and the 3-in-1 teenage booster in Year 9.
    • For a missed dose, the NHS advises contacting the GP surgery, and the 6-in-1 vaccine can still be given up to the age of 10.
    • Redness, swelling, fever and irritability are common after vaccines, and the NHS gives a route to the GP surgery or NHS 111 for a baby under 3 months with a high temperature.

    Which vaccines does the NHS offer in the first year?

    The NHS lists the routine vaccines by age, and its table starts with the babies' visits at 8, 12 and 16 weeks. At 8 weeks, babies in England are offered the 6-in-1 vaccine, the first rotavirus dose and the first MenB dose. The 6-in-1 vaccine protects against diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough, polio, Hib and hepatitis B. Rotavirus vaccine is given as drops into the mouth and protects against a stomach illness that can be severe in babies. MenB vaccine protects against meningitis and septicaemia caused by group B meningococcal bacteria. The second 6-in-1, rotavirus and MenB doses are due at 12 weeks. At 16 weeks, babies are offered the third 6-in-1 dose and the first pneumococcal vaccine, which protects against pneumococcal infection. At 1 year, the schedule includes a pneumococcal booster, a MenB booster and the first dose of a measles-containing vaccine.

    What is offered at 18 months and before school?

    According to the NHS, children born on or after 1 July 2024 are offered a fourth dose of the 6-in-1 vaccine at 18 months. The Hib/MenC vaccine is no longer part of the routine schedule for children born on or after 1 July 2024, because manufacture of it stopped and the Joint Committee on Vaccination and Immunisation advised that MenC vaccine was no longer needed. Hib protection continues through the 6-in-1 doses. A pre-school booster against diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough and polio is listed at 3 years and 4 months. GOV.UK publishes separate guides for different birth dates, so the guide that matches a child's date of birth applies.

    What changed with chickenpox and MMRV in 2026?

    The MMRV vaccine protects against measles, mumps, rubella and chickenpox. Since 1 January 2026, GOV.UK says all children are routinely offered the combined MMRV vaccine, and children born on or after 1 January 2025 have two doses, at 12 and 18 months. Children born from 1 July 2024 to 31 December 2024 are offered MMR at 12 months and MMRV at 18 months and 3 years 4 months. Children born from 1 September 2022 to 30 June 2024 are offered one dose of MMRV at 3 years 4 months. GOV.UK also describes a selective catch-up from 1 November 2026 to 31 March 2028. It offers a single MMRV dose to children born between 1 January 2020 and 31 August 2022 who have not previously had chickenpox or two doses of chickenpox vaccine. Eligibility depends on date of birth rather than age, and children born on or before 31 December 2019 are not eligible.

    What is offered at school?

    School teams offer HPV vaccine to boys and girls in Year 8, to protect against genital warts and several cancers linked to HPV. Those offered it at school stay eligible up to their 25th birthday. The MenACWY vaccine and the 3-in-1 teenage booster, called Td/IPV, are offered in Year 9, although one NHS service says Year 9 or 10. MenACWY covers meningococcal groups A, C, W and Y, which can cause meningitis and septicaemia, and the 3-in-1 booster covers tetanus, diphtheria and polio. The MenACWY vaccine does not protect against every cause of meningitis and septicaemia. The NHS children's flu vaccine is offered to children aged 2 or 3 years on 31 August, to children aged 4 who have not started school, to school-aged children from reception to Year 11, and to children with certain long-term health conditions. School-aged children mostly have it at school, while children aged 2 or 3 can usually have it at the GP surgery or a pharmacy. The nasal spray contains a small amount of gelatine from pigs, and the NHS says an injected vaccine without gelatine is available for families who want to avoid it.

    How are vaccines arranged, and what if one is missed?

    If a dose is missed, the NHS advises contacting the GP surgery. For the 6-in-1 vaccine, the NHS says a child can still have it up to the age of 10. The NHS says a missed school dose of MenACWY can still be given until the age of 25. For young people aged 14 or over who missed their HPV, MenACWY and 3-in-1 teenage booster vaccines, GP practices can give them. Local school immunisation teams and GP practices set how this works in each area, so catch-up arrangements vary.

    What side effects are common, and when should someone get advice?

    NHS England lists pain, redness and swelling where the injection was given as common after the 6-in-1 vaccine, along with fever, vomiting and irritability. NHS inform Scotland says fevers are usually mild. Allergic reactions are rare, and the people giving vaccines are trained to treat them straight away. The NHS says to call the GP surgery or NHS 111 if a baby under 3 months old has a high temperature after vaccination, or if a parent is worried. NHS inform Scotland advises contacting the practice if a child still has a fever 48 hours after vaccination. If a baby has a fit or any serious medical problem after going home, the NHS says to call the GP or to call 999 for an ambulance immediately. A child who is unwell on the day may need to wait until they feel better, the NHS says.
