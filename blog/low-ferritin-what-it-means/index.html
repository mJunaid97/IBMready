    Low ferritin: what it means and what next

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A low ferritin level means the body's iron stores are low, and doctors then look for the cause, which is often blood loss.

    Open in the 3D explorer

    Key points

    • Ferritin is a protein that stores iron, so a low level means there is not much iron in store.
    • Ferritin can fall before blood iron levels fall, which makes it the most useful indicator of iron deficiency.
    • Iron deficiency is often caused by blood loss or pregnancy, so doctors look for the source.
    • Inflammation, infection and liver disease can raise ferritin, so it is often read alongside CRP.
    • The report shows the reference range used by the laboratory, which differs between laboratories.

    What does a ferritin test measure?

    Ferritin is a protein that stores iron in the body. The test estimates how much iron is held in store. Ferritin levels are low in long-term iron deficiency and high in long-term iron overload. Lab Tests Online UK describes it as the most useful indicator of iron deficiency.

    What does a low ferritin level mean?

    A low ferritin points to iron deficiency. Ferritin tracks the body's iron stores, so a low result reflects low stores. Stores can drop well below normal before the iron level in the blood itself falls, so ferritin can show the problem early. Low iron can exist without anaemia, and symptoms such as tiredness can still be present. Ferritin can also fall when protein levels are very low, as in some cases of malnutrition.

    Why do iron stores run low?

    The NHS says iron deficiency anaemia is caused by a lack of iron, often because of blood loss or pregnancy. NHS formulary guidance lists heavy periods, bleeding in the digestive tract, coeliac disease and a poor diet among the causes. It also lists ulcers, medicines such as aspirin and anti-inflammatory painkillers, and bowel cancer as possible digestive causes, which is why the cause is looked for. UK guidance recommends checking for coeliac disease in people with iron deficiency anaemia. An NHS leaflet says blood loss from the gut is the most common cause of iron deficiency anaemia in adult men and postmenopausal women. A UK NHS guideline calls oral iron tablets the first-line treatment for iron deficiency anaemia in pregnancy. A UK NHS guideline lists intravenous iron for anaemia that has not improved despite oral iron, or when oral iron is not tolerated.

    What symptoms go with low iron?

    The NHS lists tiredness, shortness of breath, noticeable heartbeats and pale skin as common symptoms. Less common symptoms include hair loss, ringing in the ears, a sore tongue and a craving for non-food items. That craving, called pica, can be for items such as ice, paper or clay. The NHS advises seeing a GP when symptoms like these appear, and a GP appointment is the usual route for a ferritin test.

    Why can a ferritin result be misleading?

    Ferritin is not only a store of iron. Lab Tests Online UK says levels can be high in inflammatory conditions, infection and liver disease. Because inflammation can push ferritin up and hide a shortage of iron, CRP is often checked alongside it. A high ferritin can signal long-term iron overload, especially haemochromatosis, but further investigation is needed before that diagnosis is made. NHS laboratory guidance for GPs notes that when inflammation is also present, ferritin may be normal or raised even when iron stores are absent.

    What happens next?

    A GP usually takes a blood test, and ferritin is the key marker of how much iron is stored. If the cause is not clear, the GP may arrange further tests to find the source. A GP may refer to a specialist if no cause can be found. Iron tablets are the usual first treatment, and the NHS says they are taken for about six months. Side effects of iron tablets, mainly in the digestive system, are common. Iron can also be given by infusion into a vein, which is used less often. Guidance says patients are monitored in the first weeks for a haemoglobin response to iron tablets.

    What helps iron stores recover?

    The NHS says iron deficiency anaemia is treated with iron tablets and with iron-rich foods. NHS guidance points to dark green leafy vegetables and dried fruit as food sources. NHS guidance says drinking orange juice after an iron tablet may help the body absorb the iron. Tea, coffee, milk and dairy foods, and wholegrain cereals can stop the body absorbing iron from other foods and tablets. One NHS page suggests taking iron tablets with or soon after food to reduce the chance of side effects. Formulary guidance says treatment continues for a period after the haemoglobin level returns to normal. Iron tablets can cause constipation, diarrhoea, tummy pain, nausea and darker stools. A Scottish guideline notes that pregnant women lose iron during and after delivery. It recommends that women who start oral iron continue it through pregnancy and for some months after the birth.
