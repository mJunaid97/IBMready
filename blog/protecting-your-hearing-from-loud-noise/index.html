    Protecting your hearing from loud noise: headphones, earplugs and warning signs

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The WHO and NHS sources say noise-related hearing loss is permanent but can be prevented, mainly by lowering volume, taking breaks and using ear protection.

    Open in the 3D explorerEar anatomy

    Key points

    • The WHO says noise-induced hearing loss can be prevented through safe listening, although the damage is permanent.
    • NHS inform advises choosing headphones that block out more outside noise rather than turning up the volume.
    • Reusable musicians' earplugs reduce the volume of music without muffling it, according to an NHS trust leaflet.
    • The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for sudden hearing loss in one or both ears.

    Can loud noise cause permanent hearing loss?

    The WHO says noise-induced hearing loss can be prevented by following safe listening, although the damage itself is permanent. Torbay and South Devon NHS Foundation Trust says that once the cochlea has been damaged no treatment or surgery can undo it. The WHO estimates that 1.1 billion young people worldwide could be at risk of hearing loss from unsafe listening practices. NHS inform says hearing loss often develops with age or is caused by repeated exposure to loud noise, and that it is possible to reduce the risk of noise-induced hearing loss.

    What do the sources say about headphones?

    NHS inform advises choosing headphones that block out more outside noise rather than turning up the volume. The Royal Free London leaflet says noise-cancelling headphones can help because they remove the urge to turn the volume up to drown out outside noise. The WHO advises keeping the volume low, taking short listening breaks and, where possible, using carefully fitted noise-cancelling earphones. The WHO's recommendations for young people include limiting daily use of personal audio devices to less than one hour. The Royal Free leaflet advises keeping the volume modest and taking a short break from headphones each hour. The WHO notes that smartphone apps can help monitor listening levels.

    How do earplugs and earmuffs differ?

    NHS leaflets describe earplugs and earmuffs as working by reducing the level of sound that reaches the ears. Earplugs are described as probably best for long-term use, while very high noise levels may call for high-attenuation earmuffs. The Royal Free leaflet says reusable musicians' earplugs lower the volume of music without muffling it. Torbay and South Devon audiology says ordinary pharmacy earplugs are often designed for swimming or background noise and may not protect against damaging noise. The WHO recommends wearing earplugs in noisy venues.

    What are the warning signs of too much noise?

    It suggests moving away from loudspeakers at loud events and taking a break from the noise every 15 minutes. RNID warns of a risk of hearing damage after just 15 minutes in a nightclub playing music at an average of 100 decibels without earplugs. The WHO advises watching for signs of hearing loss, such as ringing in the ears or trouble hearing some sounds or conversations.

    When does a change in hearing need medical advice?

    The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for sudden hearing loss in one or both ears, hearing that has worsened over days or weeks, or hearing loss with earache or discharge from the ear. It advises a routine GP appointment when hearing is getting gradually worse. Arrangements for hearing assessments vary by area, and some NHS audiology services ask for a referral from a GP.
