    Sedating and non-sedating antihistamines

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS lists chlorphenamine as sleep-inducing and cetirizine, loratadine and fexofenadine as non-drowsy, though cetirizine can still cause sleepiness.

    Key points

    • Antihistamines block the effects of histamine, which the body releases when it meets something it is allergic to.
    • The NHS lists chlorphenamine (Piriton) among sleep-inducing antihistamines, and cetirizine, loratadine and fexofenadine among non-drowsy ones.
    • Non-drowsy does not mean never drowsy: the NHS says some people find cetirizine quite sleepy, and not to drive or ride a bike if it makes them sleepy.
    • The NHS says chlorphenamine can be taken in pregnancy, and that loratadine is usually the antihistamine recommended if one is needed.
    • A pharmacist or doctor can advise on choosing an antihistamine; the NHS names NHS 111 for some side effects and 999 for signs of a serious allergic reaction.

    What do antihistamines do?

    Antihistamines are medicines that block the effects of histamine. The body releases histamine when it meets something it is allergic to, and histamine sets off allergy symptoms such as itching, sneezing and watery eyes.

    The NHS lists cetirizine as an antihistamine used for hay fever, conjunctivitis (red, itchy eyes), eczema, hives and reactions to insect bites and stings. Cetirizine and loratadine can be bought without a prescription from supermarkets, convenience stores and pharmacies.

    Which antihistamines are sedating and which are not?

    Antihistamines are often sorted into two groups, called sedating and non-sedating. The NHS lists chlorphenamine (Piriton), cinnarizine, diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine and promethazine as sleep-inducing antihistamines, and acrivastine, cetirizine, fexofenadine and loratadine as non-drowsy ones. The NHS says non-drowsy antihistamines are less likely to make a person sleepy.

    A leaflet from Cambridge University Hospitals names cetirizine, loratadine and fexofenadine as non-sedating tablets that usually do not cause drowsiness, so they can be taken during the day, unlike sedating chlorphenamine (Piriton).

    Even within the non-drowsy group there are differences. The NHS says cetirizine is more likely to cause sleepiness than loratadine, desloratadine or fexofenadine.

    What side effects can they cause?

    For cetirizine, the NHS lists feeling sleepy and tired as the most common side effect. Headaches, dry mouth, feeling sick, dizziness and diarrhoea are also listed. The NHS adds that although cetirizine is classed as non-drowsy, some people find it makes them quite sleepy.

    For chlorphenamine, the NHS lists drowsiness and blurred vision among the common side effects. It says children and people aged over 65 have more chance of getting some side effects, such as feeling restless or excited.

    The NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away if the eyes or skin turn yellow, if someone bruises or bleeds more easily than normal, or if they have difficulty peeing.

    What do the NHS and hospital leaflets say about driving and alcohol?

    For cetirizine, the NHS says not to drive or ride a bike if it makes a person sleepy, and not to drive, cycle or use tools or machinery when feeling dizzy or shaky. The NHS also recommends avoiding alcohol with cetirizine because it can increase sleepiness. MedlinePlus, a US source, similarly advises avoiding alcoholic drinks with it. The Cambridge leaflet says cetirizine can enhance the effects of alcohol.

    For chlorphenamine, the NHS says not to drive, ride a bike or use tools or machinery if it leaves a person sleepy, with blurred vision, dizzy or unable to concentrate. It adds that it is an offence to drive if your ability to drive safely is affected, and that a doctor or pharmacist can advise anyone unsure whether it is safe to drive. The NHS also notes that some liquid products contain a little alcohol.

    What about pregnancy, breastfeeding and children?

    The NHS says chlorphenamine can be taken in pregnancy and that there is no good evidence it harms the baby. It can cause drowsiness, so a doctor or pharmacist may recommend a non-drowsy antihistamine such as loratadine. The NHS also says loratadine is usually the antihistamine recommended in pregnancy.

    For breastfeeding, the NHS says loratadine or cetirizine are usually OK. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service names chlorphenamine as the preferred sedating antihistamine and cetirizine or loratadine as preferred non-sedating ones, and suggests monitoring the baby for drowsiness, irritability, poor feeding and inadequate weight gain. The NHS chlorphenamine page says side effects in breastfed babies are very rare, and to contact NHS 111 or a healthcare professional if a baby feeds less well than usual or seems unusually sleepy or irritable.

    The NHS says most adults and children aged 1 year or over can take chlorphenamine. It lists people who may not be able to take it, including those with primary angle closure glaucoma, epilepsy, an enlarged prostate, bladder or urination problems, asthma or other lung conditions, heart disease, or liver or kidney problems.

    When should someone get advice, and when is it an emergency?

    The NHS points people who are unsure about taking an antihistamine, for example in pregnancy or when driving, to a pharmacist or doctor. NHS 111 is named for some side effects, such as yellow eyes or skin, and for a breastfed baby who feeds poorly or seems unusually sleepy.

    The NHS says to call 999 for signs of a serious allergic reaction. These include swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, fast or difficult breathing, a tight throat, and blue, grey or pale skin. They also include sudden confusion, drowsiness or dizziness, and fainting without waking.

    The NHS says taking too much chlorphenamine can be dangerous and that medical advice should be sought. It says to call 999 or go to A&E for signs such as a very fast, irregular or pounding heartbeat, breathing problems, a seizure, uncontrolled movements, confusion or agitation, or hallucinations.
