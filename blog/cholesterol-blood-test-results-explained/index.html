    Cholesterol test results explained

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A cholesterol test reports total, HDL and non-HDL cholesterol plus triglycerides, and doctors read them with other risk factors.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Key points

    • A standard cholesterol test reports total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and non-HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides are usually measured with them.
    • Non-HDL cholesterol is total cholesterol minus HDL, and it covers all the harmful types together.
    • Doctors use cholesterol results with age, blood pressure and health conditions to estimate the risk of cardiovascular disease.
    • Where treatment is needed, guidance recommends lifestyle support and often a statin, with the test repeated afterwards.

    What does a cholesterol blood test measure?

    A lipid profile measures lipids in the blood, such as cholesterol and triglycerides. The sample is taken from a vein in the arm, sometimes after fasting. A standard test reports total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and non-HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides are usually measured alongside them. NHS laboratories usually calculate LDL cholesterol from total cholesterol, HDL and triglycerides rather than measuring it directly. Total cholesterol is the combined figure, covering both good and bad cholesterol together. NICE guidance dropped routine fasting for a lipid profile, according to one NHS laboratory.

    What do HDL, non-HDL and triglycerides mean?

    HDL is generally treated as the protective part of the total, and a low HDL counts as a risk marker. Non-HDL cholesterol is total cholesterol minus HDL. It captures the combined harmful types of cholesterol, and some NHS laboratories prefer it to LDL when samples are not fasting. Lab Tests Online UK notes that non-HDL does not need a fasting sample, which makes it practical. Triglycerides are a type of fat in the blood. Fasting is no longer routinely required, though some laboratories ask for a fasting repeat when a non-fasting triglyceride result is very high. The BHF gives healthy cholesterol targets and says the goal is lower after a heart attack or stroke. Sources differ slightly on where they place the line for triglycerides, and laboratories differ in their reference ranges. For that reason the laboratory report shows the range used for each result.

    How does a doctor use the result?

    Doctors use the cholesterol result with age, blood pressure and health conditions to estimate the risk of cardiovascular disease. The QRISK tool estimates cardiovascular risk and helps decide whether treatment is needed. For people who already have cardiovascular disease, pathways move to secondary prevention, which does not use QRISK. People can ask their GP, or may be offered a test at a pharmacy or through an NHS Health Check in England.

    What helps lower raised cholesterol?

    People at higher risk are offered support with lifestyle changes such as diet, exercise, stopping smoking and managing weight. In primary prevention, lifestyle support is given for a few months before the level is reassessed. Pathways list atorvastatin as the usual first statin for primary prevention, with a higher starting dose for people who already have cardiovascular disease. Ezetimibe is a second-line option if the aim is not reached. Someone who has side effects on a high-intensity statin can be offered a lower dose or another statin. The test is repeated after treatment starts to see whether the level has fallen.

    Why does raised cholesterol matter?

    The BHF explains that extra cholesterol can build up as fatty material in the arteries and narrow them. That gradual build-up raises the risk of heart attack and stroke. Having more risk factors, such as smoking or high blood pressure, raises the overall risk further. Most people with high cholesterol have no symptoms.

    What is familial hypercholesterolaemia?

    Familial hypercholesterolaemia is an inherited form of high cholesterol. In familial hypercholesterolaemia the high cholesterol is usually present from birth. The BHF says a heart attack or stroke at a younger age, or relatives with early heart disease, can point to it. The BHF lists lumps around the knuckles, knees or back of the ankle (Achilles tendon) and a pale white ring around the coloured part of the eye as possible signs of familial hypercholesterolaemia. The BHF says that if one parent has familial hypercholesterolaemia, a child has a 1 in 2 chance of also having it.

    What do statins do, and who is offered them?

    NHS guidance says statins may be offered to people at higher risk of cardiovascular disease when healthy lifestyle changes have not helped or are unlikely to help on their own. They are also used in people who already have heart disease and in people with inherited high cholesterol. If statins are unsuitable, other cholesterol-lowering medicines can be offered. The NHS says statins should not be taken by people with liver disease or blood tests suggesting the liver may not be working properly, or by anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding. The NHS says statins may be offered if a QRISK health assessment shows an increased risk of cardiovascular disease in the next 10 years, or if you have a condition such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease or inherited high cholesterol. The BHF says most people have no side effects from statins.
