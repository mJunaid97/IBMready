    Puberty: what happens and when to get advice

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Puberty brings body and mood changes at different ages, and NHS pages advise a GP appointment if it starts early or seems late.

    Open in the 3D explorer

    Key points

    • MedlinePlus says puberty usually starts between ages 8 and 13 for girls and between ages 9 and 14 for boys.
    • Girls typically notice breast development first, boys usually begin with growth of the testicles and penis, and both have a growth spurt and may notice mood changes.
    • The NHS says signs of puberty before age 8 could be early puberty, and parents who notice early signs are advised to book a GP appointment.
    • NHS sources give different ages for delayed puberty, and delayed puberty often runs in families.
    • In a crisis, 999 is for immediate danger, NHS 111 has a mental health option, and Samaritans is on 116 123 at any time.

    What is puberty, and when does it start?

    Puberty is the time when a child's body changes as it grows towards adulthood. MedlinePlus says it usually starts between ages 8 and 13 for girls and between ages 9 and 14 for boys. One NHS trust gives an average of 11 for girls and 12 for boys, and describes any start within those ranges as normal. Girls are often the earlier of the two, and the timing varies a lot from child to child.

    Both girls and boys have a growth spurt, and both may notice changes in mood. MedlinePlus says growth spurts typically last about 2 to 3 years. Sources differ on some age thresholds, so the figures in this guide are attributed to the page that gave them.

    What changes happen in girls?

    Girls typically notice breast development first. Pubic and underarm hair growth often comes with puberty in both sexes, and increased sweating is common too. Girls also have a growth spurt, and one source says a rapid increase in height usually occurs around 10 to 14 years.

    One source says periods usually start between 8 and 14, and that most girls get them around 12 to 13.

    What changes happen in boys?

    Boys usually begin with growth of the testicles and penis. Pubic and underarm hair growth and increased sweating are also common. One source says boys usually start their growth spurt about 2 years after puberty begins.

    How can puberty affect feelings and skin?

    One NHS leaflet says a child may become more emotional during this time. Hormonal shifts can cause mood swings, including intense emotions such as love, happiness, sadness and rage. A Healthier Together page notes that the part of the brain which regulates mood, memory and planning is still developing. Another local NHS page explains that changing hormone levels during puberty can affect mood.

    Rising hormones increase sweat and skin oil, which can cause body odour, greasy hair and spots. Body odour and acne are common in both boys and girls.

    What happens with the first periods?

    A Healthier Together page says lots of young people have irregular periods when they first start, and it can take 2 to 3 years before a period settles into a normal rhythm. A Chelsea and Westminster hospital page says that in the first few years periods can be heavier and more irregular, and that over time they often become lighter and more regular.

    The same page suggests that medical advice is sensible if periods cause very distressing symptoms or significantly affect daily life, for example by making it hard to attend school or usual activities. One source notes that medical advice is also sensible if periods start before age 8, or have not started by 15 (or by 13 if there are no other signs of puberty).

    What counts as early or late puberty, and who can help?

    The NHS says that signs of puberty before age 8 could be early puberty. Other NHS guidance uses the term precocious puberty when a girl starts before age 8 and a boy starts before age 9. Parents who notice these early signs are advised to book an appointment with their GP practice. Early puberty can sometimes cause a child to stop growing sooner than expected, and Scottish referral guidance says it may reduce final adult height.

    A Kingston and Richmond NHS leaflet says that in most cases of early puberty there is no underlying cause, particularly in girls, though sometimes it runs in the family. A GP may refer a child to a hospital paediatric outpatient clinic to rule out underlying health problems. Medicine is usually only recommended if early puberty is thought likely to cause emotional or physical problems, which might include short stature or early periods in girls.

    NHS sources give different ages for delayed puberty. The main NHS page says that if puberty has not started by age 15, this could be delayed puberty. The Kingston and Richmond leaflet uses no signs of puberty by 13 in girls and by 14 in boys, and a Healthier Together page suggests seeing a GP if there are no changes by age 14. Delayed puberty often runs in families.

    Another source lists puberty that starts but does not progress, or signs that appear in an unusual order, as reasons to contact the GP. If a GP suspects a problem, they may order blood tests, an X-ray of the wrist to estimate likely adult height, or an ultrasound or MRI scan. The GP may refer a child to a specialist, usually a general paediatrician or an endocrinologist (a doctor who knows about hormone problems), if there may be an underlying cause that needs investigation. If delayed puberty is caused by a disease or an eating disorder, treating the cause may help puberty develop normally.

    Where can young people get support?

    A Hampshire and Isle of Wight NHS trust page says that anyone under 19 can call Childline on 0800 1111, and an Aneurin Bevan Healthier Together page describes Childline as a free, private and confidential service. For a routine question about puberty starting early or late, the NHS pages above point to a GP appointment.

    For a mental health crisis, the NHS says to call 999 for an ambulance or go straight to A&E if someone has seriously harmed themselves or feels they may be about to harm themselves. For urgent help that is not an emergency, the NHS Every Mind Matters page points to NHS 111 online or calling 111 and choosing the mental health option, where a trained mental health professional answers. Samaritans is a free 24-hour helpline on 116 123, and callers do not need to be suicidal.
