    The Eatwell Guide: what a balanced diet looks like in the UK

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The Eatwell Guide shows the proportions of food groups in a balanced UK diet, with fruit, vegetables and starchy foods making up just over a third each.

    Open in the 3D explorerStomach anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS Eatwell Guide divides food into groups and shows how much of each makes up a balanced diet.
    • Fruit and vegetables and starchy foods each make up just over a third of the guide, with higher-fibre starchy choices preferred.
    • NHS advice is at least five portions of a variety of fruit and vegetables a day, and at least two portions of fish a week, one of them oily.
    • Foods high in fat, salt and sugar sit outside the main groups and are meant to be eaten less often and in small amounts.

    What is the Eatwell Guide?

    The Eatwell Guide is the UK government's picture of a balanced diet, and the NHS explains it on its Live Well pages. It divides the foods people eat into groups and shows roughly what share of the diet each should make up. Foods and drinks high in fat, salt and sugar sit outside the main groups, and the guide says they should be eaten less often and in small amounts.

    How much fruit, vegetables and starchy food?

    Fruit and vegetables make up just over a third of the food in the guide. The NHS advises at least five portions of a variety of fruit and vegetables a day. A portion of fresh, canned or frozen fruit or vegetables is 80 grams. Starchy foods also make up just over a third of the guide, and the NHS recommends choosing wholegrain or higher-fibre versions where possible.

    What about protein, dairy and oils?

    The NHS lists beans, pulses, fish, eggs and meat among good sources of protein. The NHS advises at least two portions of fish a week, including at least one portion of oily fish. For dairy and alternatives the guide points to lower-fat and lower-sugar choices where possible. Unsaturated oils and spreads are the choice for oils, eaten in small amounts.

    What does the NHS say about fat, sugar and salt?

    NHS healthy eating advice includes cutting down on saturated fat and sugar and eating less salt. Fruit juice and smoothies are limited to a total of 150 millilitres a day, because they are high in sugar. The NHS says sugary drinks can be swapped for diet, sugar-free or no-added-sugar versions.

    Where do drinks, breakfast and weight fit in?

    The NHS tips say that a healthy, balanced diet can help maintain a healthy weight, and that regular exercise may help reduce the risk of serious health conditions. They describe a breakfast high in fibre and low in fat, sugar and salt as one way to be part of a balanced diet.

    How do salt and saturated fat fit in?

    The NHS advises adults and children aged 11 and over to have no more than 6 grams of salt a day, and younger children less. The NHS says around three quarters of the salt people eat comes from packaged and everyday foods. Salt is linked to high blood pressure, which can lead to heart disease or stroke. Too much saturated fat can increase cholesterol in the blood and so the risk of heart disease, and the NHS suggests swapping towards unsaturated fats such as vegetable oils, oily fish and avocados.

    What do food labels show?

    UK nutrition labels show energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. Front-of-pack labels use red, amber and green colours to show whether a food is high, medium or low in fat, saturated fat, sugars and salt. The NHS lists sweets, sugary drinks, cakes, biscuits, pastries and breakfast cereals among common sources of added sugar, and says the label and ingredients list can show how much sugar a food contains.
