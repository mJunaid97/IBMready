    Bone marrow biopsy: what happens and why

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A bone marrow biopsy takes a small sample of marrow, usually from the back of the hip bone, to find out why blood counts are abnormal or to look for abnormal cells.

    Key points

    • A bone marrow biopsy takes a small sample of bone marrow, usually from the back of the hip bone.
    • It is done to find out why blood counts are abnormal and to look for abnormal cells in the marrow.
    • Local anaesthetic numbs the area, and sedation may be offered; aching or bruising for a few days afterwards is common.
    • Results can take from a few days up to about four weeks, depending on the hospital and the tests, and are usually discussed at a clinic appointment.

    What is a bone marrow biopsy?

    Bone marrow is the spongy tissue inside bones where blood cells are made. A bone marrow biopsy removes a small sample so that it can be examined in a laboratory.

    Macmillan says the sample is taken from the back of the hip bone, which is part of the pelvis, or occasionally from the breastbone. The test usually has two parts. An aspirate draws out liquid marrow with a syringe, and a trephine biopsy takes a small core of marrow using a slightly larger needle.

    Why might I need one?

    The test is usually done to work out why blood counts are abnormal and to look for abnormal cells. NHS leaflets list a low or high number of red cells, white cells or platelets, and infection, among the reasons.

    The results help the doctor diagnose the illness and make decisions about care and treatment.

    What happens during the test?

    Local anaesthetic is used to numb the area, and gas and air may also be available. Medicine to help the person relax, called a sedative, may be offered.

    It may feel uncomfortable for a few seconds while the liquid marrow is drawn into the syringe. A Macmillan booklet says the test takes about 20 to 30 minutes, though hospitals vary. One hospital leaflet says the whole appointment takes about an hour.

    What is it like afterwards?

    A dressing is placed over the area, and hospital leaflets say how long to keep it on and dry. After sedation, Macmillan advises not driving for 24 hours and having someone take the person home and stay with them overnight. Aching or a bruised feeling for a few days is common.

    Hospital leaflets say patients are usually asked about blood-thinning medicines beforehand. Leaflets say to contact the hospital team about swelling or redness at the site; a GP or NHS 111 can also advise.

    What are the risks?

    Soreness and bruising at the site are common and usually last a few days. Serious complications are uncommon.

    Bleeding at the site usually stops with pressure, sometimes leaving a small bruise. Infection and nerve injury are described as rare, and an allergic reaction to the numbing medicine is listed as a possible risk.

    When do results come back?

    Timing varies by hospital. One trust says results can take up to two weeks, and another up to four weeks. Results are usually given at the next clinic appointment.
