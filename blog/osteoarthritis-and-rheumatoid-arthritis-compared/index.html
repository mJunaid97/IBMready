    Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis: how they differ

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.

    Key points

    • Osteoarthritis is the most common type of arthritis in the UK and mostly affects the knees, hips and small joints of the hands.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the lining of the joints.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis usually affects joints on both sides of the body and brings long morning stiffness, as well as tiredness.
    • Osteoarthritis is managed with exercise, weight management and pain relief; rheumatoid arthritis needs medicines that reduce inflammation.
    • Early assessment matters for rheumatoid arthritis, so a GP appointment is the first step for persistent joint swelling or stiffness.

    What do the two conditions have in common?

    Both osteoarthritis (OA) and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are types of arthritis, and both cause joint pain and stiffness.

    They are not. They have different causes, tend to affect different joints and are treated differently.

    What is osteoarthritis?

    Osteoarthritis is a condition that makes joints painful and stiff. The NHS says it is the most common type of arthritis in the UK.

    The protective cartilage on the ends of the bones gradually breaks down. The NHS lists several things that raise the risk of osteoarthritis, including age, joint injury, extra weight, being female and family history.

    It most often causes problems in the knees, hips and small joints of the hands. The NHS says osteoarthritis does not necessarily get any worse over time.

    There is no cure for osteoarthritis, but treatment can help with symptoms.

    Hot or cold packs can ease osteoarthritis symptoms.

    What is rheumatoid arthritis?

    Rheumatoid arthritis is a long-term condition that causes swollen, stiff and painful joints, most often in the hands and feet. It is an autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks the lining of the joints.

    The NHS says it usually affects joints on both sides of the body, and that morning stiffness often lasts longer than it does in osteoarthritis. Some people also have tiredness, a fever or weight loss.

    The trigger is not clear, but a higher risk is linked with being female, having a family history of the condition and smoking.

    Rheumatoid arthritis can also cause firm lumps called rheumatoid nodules under the skin.

    How are they treated?

    For osteoarthritis, the NHS recommends regular exercise, keeping to a healthy weight, suitable footwear and aids that reduce strain on the joint. Medicines can be used to relieve the pain of osteoarthritis, and physiotherapists and occupational therapists can provide structured exercise plans and advice on aids.

    Surgery, most often a hip or knee replacement, is generally considered when other treatments are not enough.

    Rheumatoid arthritis is treated with medicines that slow the disease and reduce inflammation, known as disease-modifying drugs, sometimes alongside biological treatments. Painkillers and steroids can ease symptoms, and physiotherapy and occupational therapy are part of care. There is no cure, but treatment can help to reduce inflammation, relieve pain and prevent or slow joint damage.

    How are they diagnosed?

    A GP asks about symptoms and examines the joints. Osteoarthritis can often be diagnosed from the symptoms and an examination.

    For suspected rheumatoid arthritis, blood tests that look for inflammation are used, and the site's pages on CRP and ESR explain two of them. Scans such as X-rays may be done to check for joint inflammation and damage.

    No single blood test can prove or rule out rheumatoid arthritis, so doctors combine blood tests, scans and an examination.

    Osteoarthritis does not usually need X-rays or blood tests, but they may be used to rule out other causes such as rheumatoid arthritis.

    If rheumatoid arthritis is suspected, the GP may do blood tests and refer the person to a specialist.

    When should someone see a GP?

    A GP appointment is the usual route for joint pain, swelling or stiffness that does not settle. The NHS emphasises that early diagnosis and treatment of rheumatoid arthritis can help to prevent joint damage.

    If the skin around a joint is red or hot, or sore joints come with a high or low temperature, the NHS advises asking for an urgent GP appointment or getting help from NHS 111.

    For sore joints with a high or low temperature, or red or hot skin around a joint, the NHS says to ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111.
