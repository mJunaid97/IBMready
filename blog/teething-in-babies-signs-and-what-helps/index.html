    Teething in babies: signs and what helps

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS lists sore red gums, a flushed cheek and extra dribbling as signs of teething, and suggests gum massage or a cool teething ring.

    Key points

    • The NHS says teething can cause few or no symptoms, and lists a sore red gum, one flushed cheek, dribbling, chewing and fretfulness as possible signs.
    • NHS sources say teething does not usually cause diarrhoea or a high temperature, so those signs may have another cause such as an infection.
    • A worried parent can contact a GP or call NHS 111, and NHS pages say to call 999 when a baby has a fit.
    • Suggested comfort measures include gum massage, a teething ring cooled in the fridge and chilled fruit or vegetables for older babies, with supervision.
    • The NHS says there is little evidence that teething gels work, and the MHRA says gels with a local anaesthetic are sold from pharmacies only.
    • Tooth cleaning with a smear of fluoride toothpaste starts when the first tooth comes through, and local NHS guides advise registering the baby with a dentist.

    What are the usual signs of teething?

    Teething is the time when a baby's first teeth, called milk teeth, push through the gums. The NHS says a baby will probably get their first tooth some time in their first year, but the timing varies, and some babies start before 4 months or after 12 months. One NHS service says most babies start teething at around 6 months. Baby teeth sometimes come through with no pain or discomfort, or with only mild symptoms that last a few days. When there are signs, the NHS lists a sore, red gum where the tooth is coming through and one flushed cheek. Other signs it lists include dribbling more than usual, chewing on things and being more fretful than usual. A baby may also sleep badly, and one NHS page mentions a slightly raised temperature of less than 38C. Bottom teeth often come through before the matching upper teeth.

    Which symptoms are not caused by teething?

    NHS leaflets say teething does not usually make a baby unwell, and that it does not usually cause diarrhoea or a high temperature. Those signs may have another cause, such as an infection. The NHS also says some people think teething causes diarrhoea, but there is no evidence for this. One NHS guide warns against explaining away signs of illness by assuming it is only teething. A baby with a raised temperature who is sleeping more than usual, is hard to feed or has fewer wet nappies than normal is described by one NHS family service as showing warning signs. A baby who seems unusually listless and unhappy may have something other than teething. A Welsh NHS patient leaflet says to contact a GP or call NHS 111 if a baby seems unwell or in distress.

    When should a parent get medical help?

    The NHS says a GP can be contacted, or NHS 111 called, if a symptom is worrying. NHS guidance says a high temperature in a baby is a reason to contact a GP or NHS 111 rather than assume teething, and one NHS resource says a high fever may point to a condition unrelated to teething and should be checked by a GP. For most children with a high temperature, the NHS describes care at home, with a GP or NHS 111 contacted if a parent is worried. NHS pages say to call 999 when a baby has a fit, and they also list a rash that does not fade when pressed and a baby who is difficult to wake among the signs that need urgent help. Where a parent is unsure, the emergency route for a baby having a fit is 999, and the routes for a baby who seems unwell are a GP or NHS 111.

    What does the NHS suggest to ease teething?

    The NHS describes gently rubbing the baby's gums as a common way to help. A teething ring can also help, and some rings can be cooled first in the fridge. The NHS says freezing a ring could damage a baby's gums, and a teething ring should never be tied around a baby's neck. Some NHS pages also mention wiping away dribble to help prevent a rash. An NHS resource says that for babies of 6 months or older, chilled pieces of fruit or vegetables such as banana, cucumber or melon can help. These should be given with supervision, because of the risk of choking. A Nottinghamshire NHS leaflet adds that healthy foods such as raw fruit and vegetables can be helpful.

    What about teething gels and medicines?

    The NHS says there is little evidence that teething gels work. The MHRA, the UK medicines regulator, reviewed gels containing the local anaesthetic lidocaine and found a lack of evidence of benefit. It advised that these products should be a second-line option, used after non-medicinal measures and after talking to a healthcare professional. GOV.UK reports that such gels have been sold from pharmacies only since 2019. NHS pages say adult oral gels can be harmful to babies. The MHRA also says oral gels containing choline salicylate are no longer suitable for people younger than 16 for teething pain. The reason is a theoretical risk of Reye's syndrome, which the NHS describes as a rare condition that can be fatal. NHS pages also mention paracetamol or ibuprofen for babies who are old enough and still in pain after other measures, and a pharmacist can advise if there is any doubt.

    When should tooth cleaning start, and when is the dentist involved?

    The NHS says brushing starts as soon as the first milk tooth breaks through. It describes a baby toothbrush with a tiny smear of fluoride toothpaste, used as soon as teeth start to come through. Local NHS guides describe brushing twice a day, including last thing at night. They say the toothpaste should be spat out and the mouth not rinsed with water afterwards, because rinsing washes away the fluoride. One local NHS guide says a baby should be registered with a dentist when the teeth start coming through. Local services differ on the timing of the first visit. Some recommend a first visit before the age of one, and one Yorkshire health service suggests going as soon as the first tooth appears.
