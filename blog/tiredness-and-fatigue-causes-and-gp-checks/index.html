    Tiredness that will not go away: common causes and what a GP checks

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Tiredness is common and often has an ordinary cause such as poor sleep, stress or illness. The NHS advises a GP appointment when it lasts a few weeks without explanation.

    Key points

    • The NHS lists poor sleep, an unhealthy lifestyle, hormonal changes, some illnesses and some medicines among the common causes of tiredness.
    • The NHS advises booking a GP appointment when tiredness has lasted a few weeks without a clear reason, affects daily life or comes with other symptoms such as weight loss.
    • A GP may arrange blood tests to look for conditions such as anaemia, diabetes or an overactive thyroid.
    • Self-help advice from the NHS covers diet, exercise, relaxation and sleep habits.

    What counts as fatigue?

    Everyone feels tired from time to time, and the NHS treats tiredness as a common symptom with many possible causes. Fatigue is described by the NHS as tiredness that is not relieved by rest and sleep. If there is no obvious reason for the tiredness and it goes on for a while without improving, the NHS says it may be a sign of a medical condition.

    What are the common causes?

    The NHS names poor sleep or difficulty getting to sleep (insomnia) and an unhealthy lifestyle as common causes. Hormonal changes can also make people tired. Stress, depression and bereavement are also listed as triggers. Some medicines, including chemotherapy, can cause tiredness. Among medical conditions, the NHS mentions sleep apnoea, iron deficiency anaemia, diabetes and an overactive thyroid as possible reasons. Gasping, snorting or choking noises during sleep are one of the reasons the NHS gives for seeing a GP, because they can point to a sleep problem.

    When is it worth seeing a GP?

    The NHS advises a routine GP appointment if tiredness has lasted a few weeks and the cause is unclear. A GP appointment is also advised if the tiredness affects daily life. Other symptoms alongside the tiredness, such as weight loss or changes in mood, are further reasons to book one.

    What might a GP do?

    A GP will usually ask about lifestyle and general health to look for an explanation. The NHS says tests may be needed, such as blood tests to check for anaemia, diabetes or an overactive thyroid. If the cause is still unclear, a GP may refer the person to a specialist.

    What does the NHS suggest for self-care?

    The NHS self-help advice covers eating a healthy diet, taking exercise, finding ways to relax and improving sleep. Caffeine can disrupt sleep, which is one reason it appears in the NHS tips. The NHS also says there is some evidence that talking therapies such as counselling or cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) may help with fatigue, and a GP can arrange a referral. Eating regular meals and healthy snacks through the day is part of the NHS advice for keeping energy steady. The NHS self-help page says regular physical activity improves energy over time. It also links excess weight with tiredness, because carrying extra weight puts extra strain on the heart. Stress uses up a lot of energy, so the NHS suggests relaxing activities. Alcohol lowers the quality of sleep, which can leave a person tired the next day.

    Could it be ME/CFS?

    Myalgic encephalomyelitis or chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a long-term condition whose most common symptom is extreme tiredness. There is no specific test for ME/CFS, so a diagnosis is made from the symptoms and by ruling out other conditions that could explain them.
