    Joint pain: causes and when to see a GP

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Joint pain has many causes, including osteoarthritis, injury and gout, and the NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for a hot, swollen joint with a fever.

    Open in the 3D explorerKnee joint anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS says to rest the joint but not stop moving it altogether, and that a pharmacist or GP can advise about painkillers.
    • The NHS suggests a GP appointment if joint pain stops normal activities or affects sleep, keeps getting worse or coming back, or has not improved after 2 weeks of treating it at home.
    • The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for severe pain and swelling in one joint that started suddenly.

    What causes joint pain?

    Gout is a form of arthritis in which small crystals form inside and around the joints. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system targets the joints, with stiffness that is often worse in the morning or after a period of inactivity. The NHS says rheumatoid arthritis is usually first felt in the small joints of the hands and feet. The NHS arthritis overview says gout often affects the big toe joint, which can become painful, swollen and hot. Psoriatic arthritis causes joint pain, stiffness and swelling, and is linked with the skin condition psoriasis, which causes a red, scaly rash.

    What can ease joint pain at home?

    The NHS says to rest the joint but not stop moving it altogether, and that a pharmacist or GP can advise about painkillers. NHS 111 Wales says being overweight often makes osteoarthritis worse, because it puts extra strain on some joints. NHS 111 Wales describes exercise as one of the most important treatments for osteoarthritis, and says hot or cold packs can relieve symptoms in some people. NHS 111 Wales says osteoarthritis has no cure but does not necessarily get worse over time.

    When is a GP appointment sensible?

    The NHS suggests a GP appointment if joint pain stops normal activities or affects sleep, keeps getting worse or coming back, or has not improved after 2 weeks of treating it at home. It also lists joints that stay stiff for more than 30 minutes after waking. For gout, the NHS advises seeing a GP for a first episode, or when the usual treatments are not helping.

    Which joint pain needs urgent help?

    The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for severe pain and swelling in one joint that started suddenly. A warm, swollen joint that is hard to move, with a high temperature or feeling hot and shivery, may mean a joint infection called septic arthritis.

    How is the cause found?

    A GP examines the joints for swelling, checks how easily they move and asks about symptoms. Blood tests such as ESR, CRP and a full blood count may be used, and X-rays or MRI scans may show inflammation and damage. No blood test can definitively prove or rule out rheumatoid arthritis, although several tests can show signs of it. If rheumatoid arthritis is suspected, the GP usually refers the person to a rheumatologist. A rheumatologist is a doctor who specialises in conditions of the joints and immune system.
