    Statins: what they do and the common concerns, as the NHS describes them

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS says statins lower the cholesterol the body makes, to reduce the risk of heart attacks and strokes, and they are usually taken long term.

    Key points

    • Statins work by lowering the amount of cholesterol the body makes, which helps stop cholesterol building up in blood vessels.
    • The NHS says statins may be offered to people at higher risk of cardiovascular disease and to people who already have it.
    • Statins are usually taken long term, and the NHS says stopping can let cholesterol rise again.
    • Muscle pain, tenderness, weakness or cramps with no clear cause is something the NHS says to get medical advice about.
    • Grapefruit juice affects some statins more than others, so the NHS says to ask a pharmacist or GP about the one prescribed.
    • The NHS says that stopping a statin can let cholesterol rise again, so it advises talking to a doctor first.

    What do statins do?

    Statins are medicines that lower cholesterol.

    Cholesterol is a fatty substance made by the liver and carried in the blood.

    According to the NHS, statins reduce how much cholesterol the body makes. This helps prevent a build-up of cholesterol in the blood vessels. High cholesterol can lead to cardiovascular disease, which includes heart attacks, strokes and peripheral arterial disease.

    Atorvastatin and rosuvastatin are two of the statins the NHS describes, along with others such as simvastatin and pravastatin.

    Who are statins for?

    The NHS says statins may be offered to people at higher risk of cardiovascular disease when healthy lifestyle changes have not helped or are unlikely to help on their own. They may also be offered to people who already have cardiovascular disease, to lower the risk of a heart attack or stroke.

    NICE has published a patient decision aid, "Are statins the best choice for me?", for people weighing up whether statins are right for them.

    The NHS says most adults can take atorvastatin, and that it is sometimes prescribed for children aged 10 and over. Caution is needed for people with liver or kidney problems or a past allergic reaction to a statin. The NHS advises against atorvastatin in pregnancy.

    How long are statins taken for?

    The NHS says statins are usually taken long term.

    For atorvastatin, the NHS says it can be taken for many years and works best when taken for a long time. It says cholesterol should fall within about four weeks if the medicine is taken as prescribed.

    The NHS advises continuing atorvastatin even when a person feels well.

    What side effects can statins cause?

    For atorvastatin, the NHS lists headaches, dizziness, nausea, muscle aches and digestive upset such as diarrhoea or indigestion as common side effects.

    For rosuvastatin, the NHS says side effects often ease as the body gets used to the medicine, and that serious ones are rare.

    The NHS lists rare serious effects including liver inflammation, pancreatitis, muscle damage and allergic reactions. It says yellowing of the skin or eyes can be a sign of liver problems, and that prompt medical attention is needed. For rosuvastatin, severe breathing or swallowing problems, or sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, are emergencies for which the NHS says to call 999.

    For rosuvastatin, the NHS says to contact a doctor if side effects last more than a few days or get worse.

    What about muscle pain?

    The NHS lists muscle pain, tenderness, weakness or cramps with no clear cause as possible signs of muscle damage. For rosuvastatin it says to get medical advice promptly if these happen.

    For atorvastatin, the NHS advises contacting a doctor or NHS 111 straight away for these symptoms. The NHS says that if side effects are the reason someone wants to stop, the doctor may be able to change the dose or switch medicine.

    Do grapefruit juice and other things affect statins?

    The NHS says grapefruit and grapefruit juice can affect some medicines by raising the level of the medicine in the blood, which makes side effects more likely.

    The NHS treats statins differently: grapefruit juice is a particular concern with simvastatin, while atorvastatin is affected mainly by large amounts and some other statins are generally not a concern.

    The NHS also says drinking a lot of alcohol regularly increases the chance of side effects and liver problems with atorvastatin.

    It advises talking to a GP or pharmacist about grapefruit if it is a regular part of the diet and a medicine that is affected has been prescribed.

    What does the NHS say about stopping a statin?

    The NHS says stopping atorvastatin may cause cholesterol to rise, which increases the risk of heart attacks and strokes.

    It says there are no withdrawal symptoms and no evidence that atorvastatin is addictive.

    For rosuvastatin, the NHS advises talking to a doctor before stopping because of side effects, since the problem may be unrelated to the medicine. The NHS also advises not stopping a medicine without advice.
