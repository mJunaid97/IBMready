    Chest pain: causes and when to get help

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Most chest pain does not come from the heart, but sudden pain that does not ease or spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back needs a 999 call.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS says that in most cases chest pain is not caused by a heart problem.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for sudden chest pain that does not ease, or pain that spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back.
    • The NHS says a person with these symptoms should not drive themselves to A&E.
    • For sharp or stabbing chest pain that gets worse with deep breathing, swallowing, coughing or lying down, as can happen with pericarditis, the NHS pericarditis page suggests an urgent GP appointment or help from NHS 111.

    What causes chest pain?

    The NHS says that in most cases chest pain is not caused by a heart problem. Common causes that do not involve the heart include heartburn or indigestion, a strained chest muscle, anxiety or panic, and chest infections such as pneumonia or pleurisy. Shingles can also cause chest pain, often with a skin rash. Angina, heart attack and pericarditis are heart conditions that can cause chest pain. Pericarditis is inflammation of the lining around the heart, and the NHS says it often follows a viral infection such as a cold or flu.

    How do angina and a heart attack differ?

    Angina is chest pain that is a sign of a heart problem, and GTN can be used to ease an attack or before a known trigger such as exercise. Angina attacks usually last less than 10 minutes. The NHS says chest pain is the most common symptom of a heart attack, but symptoms vary from person to person. Other signs include pain spreading to the arms, jaw, neck or back, sweating, breathlessness and feeling or being sick, and some people have these without any chest pain. Unlike the pain of angina, the symptoms of a heart attack are not usually relieved by a nitrate tablet or spray. A heart attack can be life-threatening and needs emergency treatment in hospital.

    When is chest pain an emergency?

    The NHS says to call 999 for sudden chest pain that does not ease, or pain that spreads to the arm, jaw, neck or back. Chest pain that comes with sweating, nausea, light-headedness or breathlessness is in the same group. The NHS says a person with these symptoms should not drive themselves to A&E. For someone already diagnosed with angina, the NHS treats pain that does not settle after rest or after their usual angina medicine as an emergency that needs a 999 call.

    When should chest pain be checked the same day?

    For sharp or stabbing chest pain that gets worse with deep breathing, swallowing, coughing or lying down, as can happen with pericarditis, the NHS pericarditis page suggests an urgent GP appointment or help from NHS 111. Angina that feels worse than before, happens more often, lasts longer or happens at rest is a reason to contact a GP urgently.

    What happens at the GP or in hospital?

    If a GP thinks someone may have angina, they can be referred to a heart specialist (cardiologist). Tests to find out whether someone is having a heart attack include an electrocardiogram (ECG) and blood tests, and further tests can include a coronary angiogram. An ECG records the heart's electrical activity. An NHS emergency department leaflet says staff check for serious causes such as a heart attack, a blood clot in the lung and a collapsed lung. The Sussex hospital leaflet says staff may test for troponin, which shows whether the heart is involved, and for D-dimer, which looks for clots in the lungs. The Sussex hospital leaflet says a normal troponin result makes a heart problem less likely, but does not necessarily mean the pain is unrelated to the heart.

    How are angina and a heart attack treated?

    Tests for a heart attack include an ECG and blood tests, and further tests can include a coronary angiogram, which can show exactly where blood flow to the heart is blocked. A heart attack needs emergency treatment in hospital, and treatment can include medicine, coronary angioplasty (opening blocked arteries with a stent) and coronary artery bypass surgery. NHS treatment for angina aims to help stop attacks and reduce the risk of further problems such as a heart attack. Medicines for angina include GTN, beta blockers, which slow the heart, and calcium channel blockers, which relax the arteries, and surgery may be recommended if medicines are not controlling angina. A GP may ask about lifestyle, such as diet, smoking and drinking, and healthy lifestyle changes alongside treatment can help control angina.
