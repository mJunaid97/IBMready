    Colonoscopy: preparation and what happens

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A colonoscopy uses a thin camera to look inside the large bowel. This guide covers bowel preparation, sedation, risks and when results arrive.

    Open in the 3D explorerLarge intestine anatomy

    Key points

    • A colonoscopy passes a thin, flexible tube with a camera through the bowel to look for the cause of symptoms, to screen for bowel cancer, or to follow up earlier problems.
    • The bowel must be empty for a clear view, so a bowel preparation, which can include laxatives, is taken beforehand.
    • Polyps, which are growths on the bowel lining, can be removed during the test and examined under a microscope.
    • Perforation, a tear in the bowel wall, is the main serious risk and is uncommon, with higher risk when polyps are removed.

    Why is a colonoscopy done?

    A colonoscopy is often done to look for the cause of bowel symptoms, such as abdominal pain or a change in bowel habit. Colonoscopy is also used to follow up after earlier polyps or bowel cancer, according to MedlinePlus, a US source. People who have had polyps removed before are routinely advised to have repeat colonoscopies.

    How is the bowel prepared?

    For a clear view, the bowel needs to be empty. Preparation can include laxatives to empty the bowel. Preparation can include clear liquids the day before and laxatives, according to MedlinePlus, a US source. A review of bowel preparation lists vomiting, colicky stomach ache and dizziness among the most common problems. Preparation depends on which type the doctor recommends, and it can differ between hospitals.

    What happens during the procedure?

    A nurse sees the person first to discuss their medical history, carry out simple checks and explain what will happen. A thin, flexible tube with a camera is passed through the anus and around the large bowel. Air is pumped in to open the bowel, which can cause bloating or cramps. The NHS says a colonoscopy can be uncomfortable but should not be painful. NHS England says a colonoscopy should take 30 to 45 minutes, and a person may be at the hospital for around two hours in total. A leaflet says some people have sedation, some breathe a pain-relieving gas called Entonox, and some have no pain relief. Sedation is given through a thin tube called a cannula in the hand or arm.

    What are polyps and biopsies?

    Polyps are growths on the bowel lining, and they are usually not cancer. Doctors usually cannot tell the type of polyp just by looking, so suspicious polyps are usually removed and examined under a microscope. Staff can also take small tissue samples, called biopsies, which people typically do not feel. Some findings may be shared once the sedation has worn off, according to MedlinePlus, a US source.

    What are the risks?

    NHS trust leaflets describe colonoscopy as very safe, with serious complications rare. The main risks are a tear in the bowel wall, called perforation, and bleeding. Trust leaflets give perforation figures that differ, and one says about 1 in 1,000 procedures. The risk rises when polyps are removed, especially large or complex ones. A perforation is treated in hospital, and surgery is sometimes needed, according to NHS trust leaflets. It is common to pass a small amount of blood from the anus after a biopsy or polyp removal. Trust leaflets say a colonoscopy can occasionally miss a problem, including, rarely, a cancer. The NHS says to call NHS 111 or the hospital where the test was done for some symptoms after a colonoscopy, and to call 999 or go to A&E for more serious ones; its page lists which symptoms apply.

    When do the results come?

    Some NHS trusts say a letter or call with the results comes about two to three weeks after a colonoscopy, though waits can differ. The result will say whether anything abnormal was found and whether further treatment is needed, according to NHS trust pages. If a biopsy was taken, trust leaflets give differing waits for the biopsy results, from several days to several weeks.
