    Cervical screening: what happens and results

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Cervical screening checks a sample from the cervix for high-risk HPV. This guide covers who is invited, the appointment and what each result means.

    Open in the 3D explorerUterus & cervix anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS in England invites people with a cervix aged 25 to 64 for cervical screening.
    • The sample is tested first for high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), the virus that can lead to cell changes.
    • A negative HPV result means the person is offered screening again in five years.
    • Cancer Research UK says cervical screening is not a test for cancer; it looks for HPV and cell changes.

    Who is invited and how often?

    The NHS in England invites people with a cervix aged 25 to 64 for cervical screening. Routine screening is every five years. From 1 July 2025 in England, people who test negative for HPV are offered screening again after five years rather than three; guidance says some people last invited before that date may still be on a three-year interval.

    What is cervical screening for?

    The NHS describes cervical screening as a test of the health of the cervix that helps to prevent cervical cancer, rather than a test for cancer. The NHS says screening is still important for people who have had the HPV vaccine. Booking is usually through the GP surgery, online or in the NHS App, and some sexual health clinics also offer it.

    What happens at the appointment?

    Cervical screening is usually done by a female nurse or doctor, and a person can ask for a female practitioner when booking. The clinician gently places a tool called a speculum in the vagina and uses a soft brush to collect cells from the cervix. One source says the test itself should take less than five minutes, and the whole appointment usually lasts 10 to 15 minutes. People can ask for a smaller speculum or ask to insert the speculum themselves. Light spotting for a few hours afterwards is normal.

    How can the appointment be made easier?

    The NHS suggests wearing something that can stay on during the test, such as a skirt or long top, and bringing someone for support. The NHS says the person can ask to stop at any time and can ask for adjustments, such as a smaller speculum or a different position, to feel more comfortable.

    Why is the sample tested for HPV?

    Current NHS practice tests the sample for high-risk HPV first. The NHS England guide says high-risk HPV can cause abnormal cell changes that may lead to cervical cancer. If HPV is found, the laboratory then looks more closely at the cells for changes.

    What do the results mean?

    If HPV is not found, the person is offered screening again in five years. If HPV is found but the cells are normal, the person is usually retested in about 12 months. If HPV is found with abnormal cells, the person is referred for a colposcopy. If more tests are needed, the results letter says what to do next. Results are sent by letter or given by telephone, and the letter says what to do if more tests are needed.

    What is a colposcopy?

    A colposcopy is the usual next step after an abnormal screening result. Cancer Research UK says cervical screening is not a test for cancer; it looks for HPV and cell changes. A biopsy is examined by a pathologist, who grades any cell changes as CIN1, CIN2 or CIN3.

    Bleeding or symptoms between screens

    Cancer Research UK advises that bleeding between periods, after sex or after the menopause, or unusual discharge, should be checked by a GP.
