    Looking after someone with dementia: what the NHS advises

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS suggests a daily routine, and says carers can ask the council for a free carer's assessment.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Key points

    • Guidance from dementia charities and NHS trusts suggests speaking slowly with simple words and keeping eye contact.
    • The NHS says that if a change in behaviour comes on suddenly, the cause may be a health problem.
    • A carer's assessment is free, and the NHS says anyone over 18 can ask for one.
    • A lasting power of attorney must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used.
    • The NHS route for sudden confusion is to call 999 or go to A&E.

    How can I talk with someone who has dementia?

    Guidance from dementia charities and NHS trusts suggests speaking a little more slowly and using simple words. It also suggests keeping eye contact, which helps the person focus on the speaker. Asking fewer questions can help, and so can offering options or questions with a yes or no answer.

    One guide advises that if the person does not understand even after a repeat, the speaker can try saying it in a slightly different way. When the person is distressed, the advice includes reassurance, for example by holding their hand if that feels appropriate.

    What helps with daily routines and safety at home?

    The NHS suggests having a daily routine, including daily walks. Another source notes that a familiar routine, with activities in the same order, can really help the person.

    For people who walk about and may go missing, carers are advised to consider tracking technology, because a switched-on mobile phone may make it possible to trace someone. Alzheimer's Society material also describes movement monitors and pressure pads that send an alert to a carer when they are stepped on. These can be placed at the front door.

    Why might behaviour change, and what can help?

    The NHS says that if a change in behaviour comes on suddenly, the cause may be a health problem. It says it is very important to ask a doctor to rule out or treat any underlying cause, such as uncontrolled pain, untreated depression, infection (for example a urinary tract infection) or side effects of medicines.

    Alzheimer's Society advises asking the doctor to review all the medicines the person takes, including any bought without a prescription. If the GP rules out health problems as the cause, they may refer the person to a mental health team. Alzheimer's Society says antipsychotic drugs should only be prescribed by a doctor as a last resort, when other treatments have been tried and did not work. It adds that they should only be used if the person's behaviour is persistently causing severe distress or putting them or others at risk of physical harm.

    What if confusion comes on suddenly?

    The NHS route for sudden confusion is to call 999 or go to A&E. NHS 111 Wales also advises going to A&E or calling 999 if someone suddenly becomes confused, and notes that it may be life threatening.

    The NHS lists infection, such as a urinary tract infection (UTI), a stroke or TIA (mini-stroke), and low blood sugar in people with diabetes among the most common causes. Other causes include head injury, some prescription medicines, and alcohol or drug issues. NHS Inform Scotland says infections, including urinary and chest infections, can trigger delirium, which is more common in older people and those with dementia.

    Dementia UK describes delirium as a state of sudden, intense confusion that can make people increasingly anxious, distressed and disorientated. It also says that for an emergency the route is 999 or A&E, and that NHS 111 is the contact for medical advice that is not an emergency.

    What support is there for the person doing the caring?

    A carer's assessment is free, and the NHS says anyone over 18 can ask for one. It is arranged through the local council's adult social services. A council worker asks how caring affects the carer's physical and mental health, work, free time and relationships. The NHS guide says the assessment is usually face to face, though some councils can do it over the phone or online.

    Several councils say a carer does not need to live with the person they look after, or care full-time, to have an assessment. The assessment is separate from any needs assessment the person being cared for might have, but both can be done at the same time. If the carer qualifies for help, the carer and the council agree a care and support plan.

    Admiral Nurses are specialist dementia nurses. Dementia UK runs a free Admiral Nurse Dementia Helpline that gives information, advice and support on any aspect of dementia. It is open Monday to Friday 9am to 9pm, and on weekends and bank holidays 9am to 5pm.

    How can a family plan ahead?

    There are two kinds of lasting power of attorney (LPA), one for health and welfare and one for property and financial affairs. An attorney can only make a decision on the donor's behalf where, even with support, the donor is not able to make that decision on their own. A health and welfare LPA can only be used if the donor does not have mental capacity. A property and financial affairs LPA can be used as soon as it is registered, if the donor chooses that.

    The property and financial version covers things such as managing a bank or building society account, paying bills, collecting benefits or a pension, and selling a home. The health and welfare version covers daily routine, medical care, moving into a care home, and life-sustaining treatment.

    An LPA must be registered with the Office of the Public Guardian before it can be used. If an LPA can no longer be used, someone may have to apply to the Court of Protection to become the person's deputy. A deputy must send a report to the Office of the Public Guardian and pay a fee every year.

    Where can I get urgent help if things become too much?

    Caring can be hard on the carer. One source encourages carers to talk to a friend or family member, or to a professional such as a counsellor or dementia adviser.

    NHS Every Mind Matters says that if there is an immediate danger to life or safety, the route is to call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent mental health help that is not an emergency, the route is to dial NHS 111 and choose the mental health option. Callers are connected to someone trained to help people in an urgent mental health crisis, who can listen, assess what support is needed and help find the right service.

    NHS trust pages say Samaritans can be called free on 116 123 at any time.
