    Levothyroxine: why timing and other medicines matter, as the NHS describes

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS says levothyroxine replaces a missing thyroid hormone and is taken at the same time each day on an empty stomach.

    Open in the 3D explorer

    Key points

    • The NHS describes levothyroxine as a medicine for an underactive thyroid that replaces a hormone the body is not making enough of.
    • The NHS says it is taken at the same time each day, on an empty stomach, before the first meal, any caffeinated drink or other medicines.
    • MedlinePlus, a US source, says calcium, iron, multivitamins and some antacids are best spaced apart from thyroid medicine.
    • The NHS says regular blood tests continue until the right amount is reached, then usually once a year.
    • The NHS says treatment is usually lifelong, and both the NHS and MedlinePlus say not to stop without medical advice.

    What does levothyroxine replace?

    The NHS describes levothyroxine as a medicine for an underactive thyroid, also called hypothyroidism. The NHS says an underactive thyroid is usually treated with daily hormone replacement tablets called levothyroxine. It explains that the tablets simply replace a hormone that is missing, which is why they do not usually cause side effects. The NHS also says that in the UK, combining levothyroxine with a second thyroid hormone, T3, is not routinely used because there is not enough evidence that it works better than levothyroxine alone.

    Why does the NHS say to take it at a consistent time?

    The NHS says levothyroxine is usually taken once a day, at the same time each day. It also says the tablet is taken on an empty stomach, before the first meal of the day, any drink with caffeine such as tea or coffee, or any other medicine, and swallowed with water. NHS inform Scotland says the morning is usually recommended, though some people prefer night. MedlinePlus, a US source, says thyroid medicine works best on an empty stomach and suggests asking a provider whether bedtime suits. A systematic review of studies, published in a journal and held on PubMed Central, reported that morning and bedtime dosing were equally effective. The NHS says that for a forgotten tablet, take it when remembered unless the next one is nearly due, and do not take two together to make up.

    How can food, coffee and other medicines affect it?

    The NHS says other medicines, supplements and foods can affect how well levothyroxine is absorbed, which is why it is taken before food, caffeinated drinks and other medicines. A systematic review on PubMed Central, Levothyroxine Interactions with Food and Dietary Supplements, found that coffee, soy products, fibre, calcium or iron supplements and enteral nutrition all decreased absorption, although the evidence was limited. It reported that keeping a gap between levothyroxine and these products is one way to avoid the problem. MedlinePlus, a US source, lists fibre supplements, calcium, iron, multivitamins, aluminium hydroxide antacids and bile-acid binding medicines as products to space apart from thyroid hormone. NHS trust leaflets also advise against taking calcium or iron at the same time. Sources differ on the gap needed, so a pharmacist or the prescriber can advise for one person, using the leaflet in the pack.

    Why are regular blood tests needed?

    The NHS says a person starts on a low amount that is adjusted over time, with regular blood tests until the right amount of levothyroxine has been reached. It says that once the amount is right, a blood test is usually done once a year to monitor hormone levels. MedlinePlus, a US source, also says hormone levels are checked at the start and then at least once a year. The NHS advises telling the GP when pregnant or planning to be, because further tests are needed and the amount of levothyroxine is likely to need changing.

    What does the NHS say about stopping?

    The NHS says to keep taking the medicine unless advised to stop. It says an underactive thyroid is a lifelong condition, so people usually need levothyroxine for the rest of their lives. MedlinePlus, a US source, says to keep taking it even when feeling well and not to stop without talking to a doctor. A pharmacist or GP is the usual route for questions about changes to treatment.

    What side effects can happen, and who can advise?

    The NHS says levothyroxine does not usually have side effects, because the tablets replace a missing hormone. It says most side effects happen when the amount is too high, and they often improve once the amount is changed. Effects listed include trouble sleeping, headaches, feeling anxious, sweating and hair loss, and too much can also bring chest pain, diarrhoea and being sick. The NHS says serious side effects are rare and names an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), with a swollen throat or tongue, a raised itchy rash or difficulty breathing, and thyroid crisis, with a high temperature, a fast or irregular heartbeat, dizziness or faintness, or jaundice. The NHS page says to call NHS 111 when someone taking levothyroxine thinks they might be having serious side effects. For other side effects, the NHS says to speak to a doctor or pharmacist. If more than the prescribed amount has been taken, the NHS says to speak to a pharmacist as soon as possible, and to call NHS 111 if one cannot be reached or it was done on purpose.
