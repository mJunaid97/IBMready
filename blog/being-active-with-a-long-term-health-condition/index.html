    Getting active with a long-term health condition: what UK guidance says

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    UK guidance describes building up activity gradually from the current level, and the NHS suggests speaking to a GP first when there are medical concerns.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Key points

    • A GOV.UK resource says one in three adults in England live with a long-term condition, and that they are twice as likely to be among the least active.
    • The UK Chief Medical Officers' guidelines say health benefits also come from lower amounts and intensities of activity, particularly for people with low fitness and disabled adults.
    • The NHS says moderate activity is the level at which a person can still talk but not sing.
    • The NHS Better Health site has a We Are Undefeatable section for people managing a long-term condition.

    Why does activity matter with a long-term condition?

    A GOV.UK resource, Health matters: physical activity, sets out the benefits of activity for preventing and managing long-term conditions in adults. It says one in three adults in England live with a long-term health condition, and that they are twice as likely to be among the least physically active. It is aimed at local authorities and healthcare professionals.

    What do the UK guidelines say?

    The UK Chief Medical Officers' guidelines include advice for disabled adults. For disabled adults, the evidence review behind them describes 150 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity a week, building up first in frequency and duration and later in intensity. They also describe two sets of challenging strength and balance exercises, twice a week. The guidelines say health benefits can also come from lower volumes, intensities and frequencies of activity, particularly for people with low levels of fitness and disabled adults.

    How is the right level of effort judged?

    The NHS says one way to tell whether activity is at moderate intensity is that a person can still talk, but not sing. The NHS advises speaking to a GP first for anyone who has not exercised for some time or who has medical conditions or concerns, and making sure the activity and its intensity suit their fitness. NHS Tayside says that when activity is increased gradually the risk of serious adverse events is very low.

    Which symptoms need medical advice?

    A Newcastle Hospitals leaflet lists feeling dizzy, fever or acute illness, and new sudden joint pain or swelling as reasons to stop exercising and monitor symptoms. The usual route in an emergency, such as severe chest pain or collapse, is to call 999. A Guy's and St Thomas' NHS Trust page says that if pain stops someone exercising, a GP might be able to recommend treatment or review medicines.

    What help is there to get started?

    The NHS Better Health Get active page has a We Are Undefeatable section for people managing a long-term health condition. It suggests the Five in Five workout, a five-minute mini-workout designed for people living with long-term conditions, as a starting point. The same page points to the NHS Active 10 app for tracking walking and to Couch to 5K. In some areas a GP or physiotherapist can refer people to structured exercise programmes; one council scheme offers one-to-one sessions with a qualified exercise referral instructor.
