    Stomach ache: causes by area and warning signs

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Where a stomach ache sits helps point to causes such as appendicitis, gallstones or IBS, and sudden severe pain needs 999 or A&E.

    Open in the 3D explorerStomach anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS lists trapped wind, indigestion, constipation and a stomach bug or food poisoning as common everyday causes of stomach ache.
    • The NHS stomach ache page links pain in the lower right side of the tummy with appendicitis.
    • The NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if the pain started suddenly and is severe.
    • The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 if the ache suddenly gets much worse, if the person is pregnant, if there is blood in the poo, or if diarrhoea or vomiting has lasted several days.

    What usually causes a stomach ache?

    The NHS lists trapped wind, indigestion, constipation and a stomach bug or food poisoning as common everyday causes of stomach ache. The NHS links intense pain around the time of a period, sometimes with pain during or after sex and when pooing, with endometriosis.

    Does the area of the pain point to a cause?

    The NHS stomach ache page links pain in the lower right side of the tummy with appendicitis. NHS 111 Wales links severe pain that lasts for hours in the upper centre of the tummy, or just under the ribs on the right, with gallstones. It links ongoing cramps, bloating, diarrhoea and constipation with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS).

    NHS inform describes appendicitis as painful swelling of the appendix, and says the pain and cramping of IBS may be relieved by going to the toilet. Stomach ulcer pain is usually in the upper tummy, may feel burning and is often worse at night. Gastritis can cause pain, indigestion and feeling sick. Gastritis is inflammation of the lining of the stomach. Acute pancreatitis causes severe pain around the top of the tummy that develops suddenly, and gallstones and alcohol are its most common causes.

    What do appendicitis, gallstones and diverticular disease look like?

    Appendicitis is when the appendix becomes inflamed; it usually causes pain in the lower right side of the tummy and is sometimes accompanied by nausea and loss of appetite. An NHS 111 Wales page says appendicitis needs urgent treatment in hospital. Many people with gallstones have no symptoms, but a stone can cause pain, jaundice or pancreatitis. NHS inform says diverticulitis, the inflamed form of diverticular disease, tends to cause more severe tummy pain, especially on the left side, and a fever. NHS 111 Wales advises urgent help from a GP or NHS 111 for blood or mucus in the stool, pain that gets worse or does not go away, or a high temperature or shivers.

    When is stomach pain an emergency?

    The NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if the pain started suddenly and is severe. Other reasons on the NHS page include struggling to breathe, confusion, and blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue. NHS inform adds chest pain and difficulty breathing to the reasons to call 999. For acute pancreatitis, the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E for sudden, severe tummy pain together with any of: pain spreading to the back, bloating that does not go away, a fast heartbeat or difficulty breathing.

    When should it be checked the same day or by a GP?

    The NHS advises an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 if the ache suddenly gets much worse, if the person is pregnant, if there is blood in the poo, or if diarrhoea or vomiting has lasted several days.

    What might a GP do?

    Treatment depends on the cause. For gastritis, a GP might arrange a breath test for Helicobacter pylori bacteria, a stool test or a blood test. Stomach ulcers may be treated with antibiotics for a Helicobacter pylori infection or with medicines that reduce stomach acid, such as proton pump inhibitors.
