    Cystoscopy: what happens and what to expect

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A cystoscopy uses a thin camera passed through the urethra to look inside the bladder. It can be flexible, usually with local anaesthetic gel, or rigid, usually under general or spinal anaesthetic.

    Key points

    • A cystoscopy looks inside the bladder through the urethra using a thin tube with a camera.
    • A flexible cystoscopy is usually done under local anaesthetic, and a rigid one under general or spinal anaesthetic.
    • It can be used to check bladder symptoms, take small tissue samples or treat bladder conditions.
    • Mild burning and a little bleeding when passing urine are common and usually settle within a few days.

    What is a cystoscopy?

    A cystoscopy is a test that looks inside the bladder. A thin tube with a camera, called a cystoscope, is passed along the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body, and into the bladder.

    NHS sources describe two types. A flexible cystoscopy uses a thin, bendable tube and is often used to check bladder symptoms. A rigid cystoscopy uses a straight tube that does not bend and is often used to treat bladder conditions. A cystoscopy can also let clinicians take biopsies, which are small pieces of tissue sent for testing.

    What anaesthetic is used?

    A flexible cystoscopy is usually done under local anaesthetic, with a numbing gel, and does not need a general anaesthetic.

    A rigid cystoscopy is done under general anaesthetic, which means being asleep. A spinal anaesthetic is also possible. For this type, people are usually told not to eat or drink for several hours beforehand.

    What happens during a flexible cystoscopy?

    Local anaesthetic gel is applied first.

    The cystoscope is then passed in. The bladder is filled with water, which can bring on a feeling of needing to pass urine. Men may find the passage past the prostate briefly uncomfortable.

    How soon can I go home, and what are the after-effects?

    Most flexible cystoscopies are day cases, and people can usually leave once they feel ready.

    Common effects include mild burning when passing urine and a little bleeding, which usually settle within a few days. Leaflets suggest drinking extra fluids afterwards.

    Less common problems include a urine infection and difficulty passing urine. After a rigid cystoscopy, narrowing of the urethra is a listed risk, and bleeding is more likely after a biopsy.

    Leaflets say to contact a GP for severe pain, heavy bleeding, a fever or being unable to pass urine. NHS 111 can advise out of hours, and 999 is for emergencies.
