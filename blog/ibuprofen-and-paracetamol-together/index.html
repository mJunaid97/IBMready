    Can you take ibuprofen and paracetamol together? What the NHS says

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS says adults can take paracetamol and ibuprofen together if needed, but children should not be given them at the same time.

    Key points

    • The NHS says adults can take paracetamol and ibuprofen at the same time if needed, and NHS inform says the two can be taken together if required.
    • For children, the NHS says paracetamol and ibuprofen should not be given at the same time but one at a time.
    • The NHS lists groups who should avoid ibuprofen or check first, including people with repeated stomach ulcers or bleeding, asthma, or severe kidney failure.
    • Many cold and flu products contain paracetamol or ibuprofen, so using several products can make it easy to take more than the recommended amount.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for signs of a serious allergic reaction, and to get urgent advice from NHS 111 or a doctor for other serious effects such as stomach bleeding or kidney problems.

    What is each medicine used for?

    Ibuprofen belongs to a group of painkillers called non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, or NSAIDs. The NHS describes ibuprofen as a painkiller that also treats inflammation. It describes paracetamol as a painkiller for pain and high temperature. For a dental abscess, NHS inform says ibuprofen is the preferred painkiller, and paracetamol can be taken instead if ibuprofen cannot be taken for medical reasons. The NHS also says that if you need pain relief while taking warfarin, paracetamol is the one to take instead of ibuprofen.

    Can adults take both at the same time?

    For adults, the NHS says paracetamol can be taken at the same time as ibuprofen if needed. It also says paracetamol can be taken with other painkillers that do not contain paracetamol, such as ibuprofen, aspirin or codeine. NHS inform, the NHS information service for Scotland, also says the two can be taken together if required. For a dental abscess, it says that if one painkiller does not work, taking both paracetamol and ibuprofen may help. The advice is different for children. For them, the NHS says paracetamol can be given alongside ibuprofen but not at the same time, and the two should be given one at a time.

    Who is told to check with a pharmacist or doctor first?

    The NHS lists several groups who should avoid ibuprofen or check with a professional before using it. These include people who have had an allergic reaction to aspirin or other NSAIDs, with signs such as wheezing, a runny nose or skin reactions. They also include people who have had a hole in the stomach, bleeding in the stomach or a stomach ulcer more than once, and people with severe heart, kidney or liver failure. Asthma, hay fever and allergies are also on the NHS list. The NHS also says to avoid ibuprofen in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist advises it, and to check with a pharmacist or doctor when breastfeeding. People over 65 are more likely to get stomach ulcers with ibuprofen, according to the NHS. Other medicines can interact with ibuprofen. The NHS interactions page lists warfarin and other anticoagulants, medicines for high blood pressure, steroids such as prednisolone, certain antibiotics and some antidepressants. It advises against taking ginkgo biloba with ibuprofen because it can increase the chance of bleeding. For other herbal products, it says there is not enough information to say whether they can be taken with ibuprofen. NHS inform advises anyone already taking other medication to check with a pharmacist first.

    Why do other products containing the same ingredients matter?

    Many cold and flu products contain paracetamol or ibuprofen. NHS inform says this makes it easy to take more than the recommended dose. The NHS says to avoid taking other medicines that contain paracetamol at the same time as paracetamol, such as cold and flu remedies, because too much can be dangerous. It adds that taking two different medicines that contain paracetamol carries a risk of overdose, and that too much paracetamol can sometimes damage the liver. For ibuprofen, the NHS says to check the label of cold and cough remedies for aspirin, ibuprofen or other NSAIDs. Taking ibuprofen with other NSAIDs such as aspirin or naproxen is not advised without speaking to a pharmacist or doctor, because side effects such as stomach ache become more likely.

    What side effects do the NHS pages describe?

    The NHS lists common side effects of ibuprofen as indigestion, stomach aches, feeling sick, being sick and headaches. Serious ones include stomach ulcers or bleeding in the stomach, and allergic reactions. The NHS also lists kidney problems, such as swollen ankles or blood in the urine, as a reason to get urgent advice. For paracetamol, the NHS says there may be no obvious symptoms at first after taking too much, so feeling well does not rule out harm.

    When should someone get help, and from whom?

    For signs of a serious allergic reaction to ibuprofen, such as swollen lips, mouth, throat or tongue, fast or difficult breathing or a tight throat, the NHS says to call 999. The NHS also says to call 999 for severe chest or stomach pain, which can signal a hole in the stomach or gut, and for difficulty breathing or worsening asthma symptoms. Vomiting blood or black, tar-like poo can be signs of bleeding in the stomach, and the NHS says to get urgent advice from NHS 111 or a doctor. Swollen ankles or blood in the urine can point to a kidney problem, and the NHS says to contact NHS 111 or a doctor. If too much paracetamol has been taken, the NHS says to contact NHS 111 for advice, even when the person feels well. For questions that are not urgent, NHS inform says to speak to a pharmacist if unsure.
