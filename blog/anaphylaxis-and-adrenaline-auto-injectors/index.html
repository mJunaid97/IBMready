    Anaphylaxis: what it is and what the NHS says about adrenaline auto-injectors

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction; the NHS describes adrenaline, calling 999 and hospital care, and says people at risk usually get two auto-injectors.

    Key points

    • Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction in which the immune system overreacts to a trigger, often a food, a medicine or an insect sting.
    • The NHS lists swelling of the throat and tongue, fast or difficult breathing, feeling faint or dizzy, and blue, grey or pale skin or lips as signs.
    • The NHS describes using an adrenaline auto-injector straight away if the person has one, then calling 999 and saying they think it is anaphylaxis.
    • The NHS says a person who has had a severe reaction or been given adrenaline should always be taken to hospital, because symptoms can come back.
    • NHS inform says people at risk are usually prescribed two auto-injectors, and MHRA guidance covers carrying two, checking expiry dates and brand-specific training.

    What is anaphylaxis and what triggers it?

    Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction. NHS inform describes it as the immune system overreacting to a substance that is usually harmless, which prompts the release of chemicals such as histamine. The NHS treats it as a medical emergency.

    Common triggers named in NHS material include certain foods, some medicines and insect stings, such as wasp or bee stings. NHS inform adds that the cause is sometimes never found.

    What symptoms does the NHS describe?

    The NHS lists swelling of the throat and tongue, difficulty breathing or breathing very fast, and feeling faint or dizzy. It also lists skin, lips or tongue turning blue, grey or pale.

    Resuscitation Council UK guidance says skin or mucosal changes on their own, without airway, breathing or circulation problems, do not mean anaphylaxis. The same guidance says skin changes can be absent in up to a fifth of cases, so their absence does not rule it out.

    What does the NHS describe as emergency treatment?

    The NHS says to use an adrenaline auto-injector if the person has one and the helper knows how to use it correctly. It then advises calling 999 for an ambulance and saying the person may have anaphylaxis. This applies even if they start to feel better.

    On position, the NHS describes lying the person down with their legs raised, unless breathing difficulty means they need to sit up. A pregnant woman is described as lying on her left side. If an insect stinger is stuck in the skin, the NHS describes removing it.

    The NHS says that if symptoms do not improve after 5 minutes and a second auto-injector is available, another injection can be given. Some hospital leaflets give a different timing, so the NHS wording is the one followed here. MHRA guidance says that when there is doubt about the severity of an episode, adrenaline should be given without delay.

    Why is hospital care needed afterwards?

    The NHS says a person who has had a severe allergic reaction, or has been given adrenaline, should always be taken to hospital for further observation and treatment. Symptoms can come back after the first treatment, which doctors call a biphasic reaction.

    The NHS treatment page describes hospital care that can include oxygen and fluids given into a vein. Resuscitation Council UK guidance says there is no reliable way to predict who will have a second reaction, so decisions about discharge are made for each person by an experienced clinician.

    Who is prescribed adrenaline auto-injectors?

    NHS inform says people at risk of anaphylaxis are usually prescribed two adrenaline auto-injectors. The most common brands it names are EpiPen, Jext and Emerade.

    MHRA guidance on the GOV.UK site says people prescribed these devices should carry two at all times. It also says to check expiry dates and obtain replacements before they run out, because an expired device is expected to be less effective, and a pharmacist can help with replacements.

    What practical points do the MHRA and NHS raise?

    The MHRA says technique varies between auto-injector brands, so people with allergies and their carers should be trained on the particular device they have been prescribed. Manufacturers can supply training pens that contain no adrenaline, and regular practice with these is recommended.

    The MHRA guidance also refers to carers. It says carers, as well as the person with the allergy, should be trained on the specific device prescribed.

    What happens after a reaction?

    NHS inform says that after anaphylaxis a person may be referred to an allergy clinic, which gives advice and medication to help prevent further reactions. The NHS says blood tests may be done in hospital to help confirm anaphylaxis.

    Resuscitation Council UK guidance states that everyone who presents with anaphylaxis should be referred to an allergy clinic to look for the cause, with an adrenaline auto-injector offered in the meantime unless a medicine caused the reaction.
