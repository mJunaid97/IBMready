    Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD: what they are and when to see a GP

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).

    Open in the 3D explorerOesophagus anatomy

    Key points

    • Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest caused by stomach acid travelling up towards the throat.
    • When reflux keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GORD.
    • Lifestyle changes and pharmacy medicines are the usual first steps, and a pharmacist can advise on options.
    • Difficulty swallowing, unintentional weight loss or frequent vomiting need prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111.
    • A GP can prescribe acid-reducing medicine and arrange tests if symptoms continue.

    What is heartburn, and how does it relate to GORD?

    Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest. NHS sources describe it as caused by stomach acid travelling up towards the throat, which is known as acid reflux.

    If it keeps happening, the condition is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, or GORD.

    What are the symptoms?

    The main symptoms of acid reflux are heartburn and an unpleasant sour taste in the mouth caused by stomach acid. Some people also have a hoarse voice, bad breath, bloating or feeling sick.

    Symptoms tend to get worse after eating, when lying down or when bending over.

    What can bring it on?

    NHS sources list several contributors: certain foods and drinks, being overweight, smoking, stress or anxiety, having a hiatus hernia, and pregnancy.

    Some medicines, including anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen, can also play a part.

    Heartburn can also come with bad breath.

    What helps?

    Lifestyle changes are usually the first step. NHS advice includes eating smaller, more frequent meals, avoiding foods that trigger symptoms, and not eating within a few hours of going to bed. Not smoking is also advised.

    Antacids and alginates, which are available from pharmacies, can ease symptoms, and a pharmacist can talk through the options.

    Antacids and alginates are not meant to be taken regularly for long periods, so a pharmacist can advise on them.

    When should someone see a GP?

    A GP appointment is the usual route if lifestyle changes and pharmacy medicines do not help, or if heartburn happens most days.

    Some symptoms need prompt attention, by contacting the GP or NHS 111. NHS sources list difficulty swallowing, unintentional weight loss, food getting stuck in the throat and frequent vomiting as reasons to contact a GP or NHS 111.

    Chest pain can also be a sign of a heart problem. The NHS says to call 999 for sudden chest pain that does not ease, especially with sweating, feeling sick, light-headedness or breathlessness, as it may be a heart attack.

    Food getting stuck in the throat is another reason to see a GP.

    Vomiting blood, or vomit that looks like coffee grounds, needs emergency medical care.

    Severe chest pain can be mistaken for heart-related pain, so treat sudden severe chest pain as an emergency and call 999.

    What can a GP offer?

    A GP may prescribe a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that reduces the amount of acid the stomach makes. A GP may prescribe a proton pump inhibitor for a short time, and some people need a long-term prescription, and the NHS advises anyone whose symptoms continue or return to go back to the GP.

    If symptoms continue despite treatment, a GP may refer someone for a gastroscopy, a test to look at the lining of the food pipe.

    Surgery to strengthen the valve at the bottom of the food pipe exists but is not usually needed.

    Long-lasting reflux can sometimes inflame or ulcerate the food pipe.
