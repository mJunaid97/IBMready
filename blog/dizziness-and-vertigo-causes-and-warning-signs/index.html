    Dizziness and vertigo: common causes and the signs that need urgent help

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure. The NHS advises calling 999 when it comes with signs of a stroke.

    Open in the 3D explorerEar anatomy

    Key points

    • Vertigo is the feeling that you or the room is spinning, and the NHS says inner ear problems are its most common causes.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for vertigo with double vision, trouble speaking or weakness or numbness in an arm or leg.
    • A severe headache or feeling very sick with vertigo calls for an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.
    • Dizziness that keeps coming back, or comes with hearing, eyesight or pulse changes or fainting, is a reason to contact a GP.

    Is dizziness the same as vertigo?

    Dizziness is a broad word that people use for several sensations, including light-headedness and a feeling of spinning. Vertigo is a particular kind of dizziness in which it feels as if you or the room is spinning, even when nothing is moving. The NHS says inner ear problems, which affect balance, are the most common causes of vertigo.

    What causes light-headedness?

    NHS inform lists labyrinthitis, migraine, low blood sugar and dehydration among the causes of dizziness. A sudden drop in blood pressure on standing up is another listed cause. Less often, an irregular heart rhythm called atrial fibrillation, or carbon monoxide poisoning, is on the list.

    What helps while dizzy?

    NHS inform says dizziness usually goes away on its own. Its self-care advice describes lying down until the feeling passes and then getting up slowly. NHS inform also says not to do anything that could be dangerous while dizzy, such as driving, climbing a ladder or using heavy machinery. Most vertigo improves without treatment, and when treatment is needed it depends on the cause. BPPV usually goes away on its own after several weeks or months, and repositioning manoeuvres are used to treat it. For light-headedness on standing, a GP can measure blood pressure lying down and standing up and may arrange blood tests to check thyroid and sugar levels.

    What are the common causes of vertigo?

    The NHS vertigo page lists benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), labyrinthitis, vestibular neuritis, Ménière’s disease and migraine among the causes. In BPPV, certain head movements trigger short spells of spinning. Rolling over in bed or tilting the head back to look up are typical triggers. Labyrinthitis is usually caused by a viral infection such as a cold or flu, so antibiotics will not help, and the vertigo usually lasts a few days. Hearing loss and ringing in the ears (tinnitus) can come with labyrinthitis. Ménière’s disease is a rarer cause, with attacks that can include vertigo, hearing loss and tinnitus.

    When should you see a GP?

    NHS inform advises contacting a GP if dizziness will not go away or keeps coming back. Changes in hearing, eyesight or pulse, or fainting, are further reasons to make contact. For vertigo, the NHS suggests seeing a GP if it comes on suddenly, does not go away, keeps returning or affects daily life.

    When is it urgent?

    The NHS says to ask for an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111 if vertigo comes with a severe headache, being sick or feeling very sick, or a very high temperature. It says to call 999 or go to A&E if vertigo comes with double vision or loss of vision, hearing loss, trouble speaking, or weakness, numbness or tingling in an arm or a leg. The NHS also advises not to drive yourself to A&E. Dizziness is among the symptoms the NHS lists for stroke, and any sign of stroke is treated as an emergency: the NHS FAST test stands for face, arms, speech and time to call 999.
