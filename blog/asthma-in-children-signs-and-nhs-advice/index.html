    Asthma in children: signs and NHS advice

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Asthma in children brings wheeze, cough and a tight chest; the NHS says to call 999 if the reliever is not helping during an attack.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS lists wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath and chest tightness as the main signs of asthma, and they usually come and go.
    • Younger children are usually not able to do lung tests, so doctors rely on the history of symptoms and on examination.
    • Reliever inhalers treat symptoms when they occur, and preventer inhalers are used every day.
    • One NHS England primary care document says every child with asthma should have a written plan and an annual review.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for an ambulance during an asthma attack if the person feels worse or does not feel better after following the steps.

    What does asthma look like in a child?

    Asthma is a long-term condition that affects the airways, the tubes that carry air in and out of the lungs. The NHS lists wheezing, coughing, shortness of breath and chest tightness as the main symptoms of asthma. The NHS says these symptoms usually come and go, and they may be worse at night and early in the morning. Asthma and Lung UK lists the same four main symptoms in children. It says young children often describe chest tightness as a sore tummy, and parents may notice a child rubbing their tummy or chest. It also says children with asthma often cough at night, in the early morning, after exercise, or when they are excited or laughing. Asthma and Lung UK advises speaking to a GP or asthma nurse if a child needs the blue reliever inhaler three or more times a week, wakes at night coughing or wheezing, struggles with exercise, or misses school because of asthma.

    How is asthma diagnosed in children?

    The NHS says asthma is diagnosed from the symptoms, from listening to the chest, and from tests of how well the lungs are working. Current guidance recommends objective tests to confirm the diagnosis where possible. Spirometry, which checks airflow before and after a reliever medicine, is described as the standard objective test in guidance for clinicians. One guideline notes that many children aged 5 can perform spirometry. Peak flow is an alternative when spirometry is not available, but guidance says peak flow variability alone should not be used to diagnose asthma. Younger children are usually not able to do these tests, so doctors rely more on the history of symptoms and on examination. Many young children who wheeze with viral infections do not go on to have persistent asthma. For a child under 5 with suspected asthma, NICE guideline NG245 advises starting inhaled corticosteroid treatment and reviewing the child regularly. The reasoning is that viral infections can cause cough and wheeze that look like asthma at this age, so the treatment is a trial. NICE says stopping treatment can be considered if symptoms have resolved after 8 to 12 weeks, with a review of symptoms after a further 3 months.

    What can trigger asthma in a child?

    A trigger is something that sets off asthma symptoms. The NHS says triggers include allergies, exercise, smoke, cold air and infections. The NHS also says it is not fully understood what causes asthma. Asthma and Lung UK says colds and viruses are the most common reason that children wheeze. The NHS adds that some medicines, including aspirin, ibuprofen and beta blockers, can set off symptoms in some people with asthma. Asthma action plans list a child's known triggers so that they can be avoided or treated.

    What treatment do children with asthma get?

    The NHS says the main treatment for asthma is medicine breathed in through an inhaler. There are two main types. Reliever inhalers are used to treat symptoms when they occur. Preventer inhalers are used every day to reduce the inflammation and sensitivity of the airways. Guidance for families says the preventer is given as set out in the child's plan, even when the child feels well. An NHS page on salbutamol inhalers says spacers are especially useful for giving salbutamol to young children. One guidance document says children can move from a facemask spacer to a mouthpiece spacer once they can drink through a straw.

    What are an asthma action plan and a review?

    One NHS England primary care document says every child with asthma should have a personalised written plan, an annual review, and a review within 48 hours after an attack. The plan lists the child's known triggers and describes what the child looks and acts like when asthma is getting worse, so that carers can recognise it. The annual review is meant to assess how well the asthma is controlled and to update the plan. The same document says clinicians check whether the child can ask for the reliever when needed, and record it on the plan if not. A children's charity suggests bringing the action plan, the inhalers, the spacer and any peak flow meter to the appointment. Some guidance also suggests keeping a spare reliever and spacer at school, and a second set if the child lives between two homes. After hospital care, one NHS trust says a child should be reviewed by the GP or asthma nurse within two working days, while another says within 48 hours, so the timing differs between trusts.

    How can parents recognise an asthma attack, and who should they call?

    The NHS lists worsening symptoms, a reliever inhaler that is not helping, and being too breathless to speak, eat or sleep as signs of an asthma attack. NHS advice notes that children may also complain of a tummy or chest ache. NHS attack advice says to call 999 for an ambulance if the person feels worse at any point or does not feel better after following the attack steps, and to call 999 straight away if there is no inhaler. Asthma and Lung UK lists difficulty breathing, the chest or tummy sucking in with each breath, ribs standing out, being too breathless to talk or eat, and a reliever that is not working as reasons to call 999. For children, the advice is to keep them upright and reassure them, and to use a spacer if they have one. Sources differ on details such as the number of puffs, so the child's own action plan sets the exact steps. If symptoms improve and 999 is not needed, the NHS advises an urgent same-day appointment with a GP or asthma nurse. One NHS trust guide says that if the reliever is needed more often than every four hours but the child is still talking in full sentences, the route is an urgent GP appointment or, if the surgery is closed, NHS 111. For symptoms that are more frequent but not urgent, Asthma and Lung UK advises speaking to a GP or asthma nurse.
