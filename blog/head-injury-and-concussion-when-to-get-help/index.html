    What happens after a head injury, which signs need 999 or A&E, and how concussion usually settles

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Most head injuries are not serious, but the NHS says being knocked out and not waking, a fit or being unable to stay awake need a 999 call.

    Key points

    • The NHS says to call 999 if a person was knocked out and has not woken up, cannot stay awake or keep their eyes open, or has a fit (seizure).
    • NHS inform says to go to A&E or call 999 for repeated vomiting, a severe or worsening headache, increasing drowsiness or confusion; the NHS says to get help from NHS 111 if the person is being sick or feels dizzy.
    • NICE sets out when a CT head scan is advised in hospital, with separate rules for adults and for children under 16.
    • The NHS says someone should stay with the injured person for the first 24 hours; NHS inform describes a responsible adult watching for 72 hours when concussion is suspected.
    • Headway says fewer than one in ten concussions involve loss of consciousness, and that going back to sport too early risks a second blow before the brain has recovered.

    What counts as a head injury, and what is concussion?

    The NHS says most head injuries are not serious, but it advises getting medical help for any symptoms. Concussion is a temporary brain injury, and the NHS says it can last a few weeks.

    Headway describes concussion as the brain shaking back and forth inside the skull, causing mild damage. Being knocked out is not required. Headway says fewer than one in ten concussions involve loss of consciousness.

    Headway lists confusion, dizziness and forgetfulness after a significant blow to the head as early signs. It says headaches, poor concentration and sleep problems can appear over the following days.

    Which signs mean calling 999?

    The NHS head injury page says to call 999 if the person has been knocked out and has not woken up. It gives the same route if the person cannot stay awake or keep their eyes open, or has a fit (seizure).

    The NHS also lists a fall from more than 1 metre or five stairs, problems with vision or hearing, clear fluid coming from the ears or nose, and bleeding or bruising behind the ears. NHS 111 Wales adds numbness or weakness in part of the body, and problems with walking, balance, understanding or speaking.

    NHS 111 Wales also says that 999 is the route if someone cannot be taken to A&E safely.

    When is A&E the route, and when NHS 111?

    NHS inform says to go to A&E or call 999 for repeated vomiting, a worsening or severe headache, increasing drowsiness or confusion. NHS 111 Wales lists A&E for a person who was knocked out but has since woken, has vomited since the injury, has a headache that painkillers do not ease, shows a change in behaviour or has memory problems.

    The NHS says to get help from NHS 111 if the person is being sick or feels dizzy. NHS inform says that when concussion is suspected but there are no signs needing urgent A&E care, it advises phoning NHS 111 for advice, even if a hospital visit is not needed.

    What happens at A&E, and who has a CT scan?

    In hospital, staff assess the person after a head injury. NICE guidance, written for health professionals, covers assessment and early management and sets out when a CT head scan is advised.

    For adults aged 16 and over, NICE advises a scan if the score on a standard alertness scale is still lower than normal two hours after the injury. A scan within eight hours is advised for people who were knocked out or have memory loss since the injury, and for people taking anticoagulant (blood-thinning) medicines.

    For children under 16 the rules are separate. NICE also describes observing children for a minimum of four hours when no risk factors are present.

    What about babies and children?

    The advice comes from local NHS trust leaflets, which differ a little. Many describe an adult watching the child for 48 hours after a head injury, while some say 24 to 48 hours.

    The leaflets list signs for 999 or A&E, including repeated vomiting (more than twice), becoming drowsy or hard to wake, confusion, a fit, difficulty speaking or understanding, weakness in the arms or legs, loss of balance, problems with eyesight, and clear fluid from the nose or ears. For babies they add not waking for feeds, or crying that cannot be soothed.

    Some leaflets put a headache that does not go away in the 999 or A&E group, while Alder Hey places it under NHS 111 or an urgent GP review unless it is getting worse.

    What care at home does the NHS describe?

    The NHS says someone should stay with the injured person for the first 24 hours. NHS inform describes a responsible adult keeping watch for 72 hours when concussion is suspected, and says the person should not drive, drink alcohol, take recreational drugs or play sport.

    NHS 111 Wales says a person who is tired does not need to stay awake. NHS inform describes a staged recovery plan, starting with relative rest for 24 to 48 hours and light activity with little screen time.

    Trust leaflets differ on timings, so the advice from the treating team is the one that applies.

    What if symptoms carry on, and when can sport resume?

    Headway says to see a GP if symptoms persist. One of its leaflets gives about two weeks and another gives 28 days, and it says symptoms that are severe or getting worse need prompt medical assessment.

    Headway advises against returning to play or training until cleared by a medical professional, even if the person feels fine, and describes a staged return to sport. It says another blow to the head before the brain has recovered can worsen the damage and, on rare occasions, can be fatal.

    NHS inform's post-concussion syndrome page describes pacing, which means increasing activity gradually, to help with returning to work and leisure.
