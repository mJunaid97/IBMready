    Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth all cause rashes in children, but their other signs and the advice on getting help differ.

    Key points

    • Chickenpox causes red, itchy spots that turn into fluid-filled blisters and then scabs.
    • Measles starts with cold-like symptoms, and a rash appears a few days later.
    • Hand, foot and mouth disease usually clears up on its own in seven to ten days, and antibiotics will not help.
    • For measles, the NHS advises phoning the GP surgery before going in, because it can spread easily.
    • A rash that does not fade under a glass can be a sign of sepsis caused by meningitis, and the NHS says to call 999 straight away.

    How can a parent tell these three rashes apart?

    All three illnesses can cause a rash, so the other symptoms and the pattern of the spots help to tell them apart. Chickenpox may start with mild flu-like symptoms, and these tend to be more common and worse in adults than in children. The rash is made of red, itchy spots that turn into fluid-filled blisters. The blisters then crust over to form scabs, which eventually drop off.

    Measles begins with cold-like symptoms, such as a runny nose, a cough, red and watery eyes and a high temperature. A rash then appears a few days later. It usually starts on the head or neck and spreads over the body. The rash is made of small red-brown spots that may join into blotchy patches, and it is not usually itchy.

    Hand, foot and mouth disease typically starts with a fever, a sore throat, a poor appetite and feeling unwell. Mouth ulcers and a rash of red spots on the hands and feet follow. The spots become blisters that are grey in the centre and can be painful.

    How do the infections spread?

    Chickenpox is infectious from one to two days before the rash starts. UK government guidance says children with chickenpox should stay away from nursery or school until five days after the rash first appears.

    Measles virus is contained in tiny droplets that come out of the nose and mouth when an infected person breathes, coughs or sneezes. The NHS says people are infectious from when symptoms start until about four days after the rash first appears. Measles can spread easily to others, which is why the NHS advises calling the GP surgery before going in.

    Hand, foot and mouth disease spreads through coughs, sneezes and poo. NHS guidance says people are infectious for a few days before symptoms begin and for the first five days after symptoms start.

    What is the usual course of each illness?

    There is no treatment for chickenpox itself. Pharmacy remedies include paracetamol for fever and calamine lotion or cooling gels for itching. After one to two weeks the crusted skin falls off naturally.

    With measles, UK guidance is to keep a child away from nursery or school for at least four full days from when the rash first appears. The NHS says normal activities can restart after four days if the person feels well and no longer has a temperature.

    Hand, foot and mouth disease usually clears up on its own in seven to ten days, and antibiotics will not help. NHS guidance says drinking fluids helps to prevent dehydration, and it advises avoiding acidic drinks such as fruit juice.

    When should a parent phone NHS 111 or the GP?

    For chickenpox, NHS guidance for parents says to contact the GP straight away if the blisters become infected. NHS guidance says to phone NHS 111 if the GP practice is closed.

    For measles, the NHS says a child with the illness will need to see a GP. It advises phoning the surgery before going in, because measles can spread to other people easily.

    For hand, foot and mouth disease, NHS guidance suggests seeing a GP if symptoms do not improve after seven to ten days, if the fever is very high or if dehydration develops. Signs of dehydration include sunken eyes, drowsiness or no urine for 12 hours. Swollen lips or tongue with struggling to breathe is listed as an urgent warning sign, with advice to call NHS 111 or seek emergency care.

    Which signs mean calling 999?

    The NHS says a rash that does not fade under a glass can be a sign of sepsis caused by meningitis, and to call 999 straight away. The NHS adds that not everyone with meningitis gets a rash, so other signs matter too. These include a high temperature, a headache, vomiting, drowsiness, a stiff neck and sensitivity to light.

    On darker skin the rash is harder to see, so NHS guidance suggests checking paler areas such as the palms, the soles of the feet, the roof of the mouth and the eyelids.

    For measles, the NHS lists a seizure, severe breathing difficulty, being unable to stay awake, sudden confusion or a rash that does not fade under a glass as reasons to call 999 or go to A&E. NHS guidance for parents lists breathing difficulty, confusion, a seizure or a rash that does not fade under a glass as emergencies in chickenpox.

    What if someone is pregnant or has a weak immune system?

    People at higher risk from chickenpox include pregnant women, people with a weakened immune system and very young babies. UK guidance says these groups should contact the GP for advice if they have been exposed, even when there is no rash or other symptom. The NHS chickenpox page advises urgent help for anyone who has been near someone with chickenpox and is pregnant or has a weakened immune system.

    Complications of measles are more likely in people with a weakened immune system, in babies under one year old and in pregnant women. Anyone who is pregnant and has been in close contact with measles is told to contact the GP urgently.

    For hand, foot and mouth disease, NHS guidance says there is normally no risk to the pregnancy or baby. An infection shortly before birth can mean the baby is born with a mild version of it. NHS guidance advises contacting a GP or midwife if pregnant and infected or exposed.

    Can vaccination prevent these illnesses?

    Measles can be prevented with the MMR vaccine, which is given in two doses as part of the NHS childhood vaccination programme. The NHS says two doses of MMR give the best protection against measles, mumps and rubella.

    UKHSA says the MMRV vaccine, which also protects against chickenpox, has been added to the routine childhood schedule from January 2026. GOV.UK guidance for healthcare professionals says children born on or after 1 January 2020 who need MMR catch-up should be offered MMRV. Children born on or after 1 January 2025 are due two doses of MMRV, at 12 months and 18 months.
