    How long antidepressants take to work, and what the NHS says about side effects and stopping

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS says antidepressants usually start to help after 1 to 2 weeks and can take up to 8 weeks to work fully.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS says antidepressants usually take 1 to 2 weeks to start having an effect and can take up to 8 weeks to work fully.
    • For sertraline and citalopram the NHS says it usually takes 4 to 6 weeks to work fully, with some people feeling better after 1 to 2 weeks.
    • Early side effects such as feeling sick, headaches and trouble sleeping are common, and the NHS says they usually ease after a couple of weeks.
    • The NHS and NICE both say that stopping suddenly can cause withdrawal symptoms, so changes to the dose are decided with the prescriber.
    • If a person or someone else is in immediate danger, call 999; NHS 111 (mental health option) and Samaritans on 116 123 (free, any time) are other routes.

    How long do antidepressants take to work?

    The NHS says antidepressants usually take 1 to 2 weeks to start having an effect. They can take up to 8 weeks to work fully. For sertraline, the NHS says it usually takes 4 to 6 weeks to work fully, although some people start to feel better after 1 to 2 weeks. For citalopram, the NHS gives the same pattern, with a usual 4 to 6 weeks before the full benefit and some improvement possible after 1 to 2 weeks.

    Both medicines belong to a group called selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or SSRIs. For citalopram, the NHS advises against stopping after only 1 or 2 weeks just because it seems not to be helping, since the full benefit takes longer to appear.

    What can happen in the first weeks?

    The NHS lists nausea, headaches and diarrhoea among the common side effects of sertraline. It also lists dizziness or drowsiness, dry mouth and sleep problems. For citalopram, common effects include tiredness, feeling nervous, dry mouth and sweating. The NHS says common side effects like these are usually mild and go away after a couple of weeks as the body gets used to the medicine.

    The NHS lists sexual problems, such as a lower sex drive, among the common side effects of citalopram. Sertraline can make a person feel more or less hungry than usual, so weight may go down or up at the start, the NHS says. The NHS suggests avoiding alcohol with sertraline because it can stop the medicine working properly. It also says grapefruit juice can increase the amount of sertraline in the body and the risk of side effects.

    If side effects last beyond 1 or 2 weeks, the NHS suggests asking a pharmacist or doctor about them.

    What if nothing seems to change?

    The NHS says a GP may recommend trying a different antidepressant if the first one is not helping. People respond differently to different antidepressants, so a change of medicine can sometimes help. Anyone worried about their progress can raise it with a GP or pharmacist.

    Why is stopping suddenly a question for the prescriber?

    The NHS says antidepressants should not be stopped suddenly, because this may cause withdrawal symptoms. For citalopram, the NHS says the doctor will usually recommend reducing the dose gradually over several weeks, or longer after a long course. For sertraline, the NHS says stopping is usually done by reducing the dose gradually.

    NICE says reducing the dose in stages, known as tapering, helps to lessen withdrawal effects, and that the taper is agreed with the person's healthcare professional. NICE guidance for adults with depression also says people who want to stop should talk to the person who prescribed the medicine. Tapering is typically needed, and most people manage to stop successfully. The NHS adds that stopping too early can make depression come back.

    How long does treatment usually last?

    The NHS says a course of antidepressants usually lasts for at least 6 months after a person starts to feel better. For citalopram, the NHS says most doctors recommend taking antidepressants for 6 months to a year. For sertraline, the NHS says most doctors recommend treatment for 6 months to a year after a person no longer feels depressed. The decision to stop or continue depends on symptoms and how well the medicine is working, and is made with the prescriber.

    Where can someone get help in a crisis?

    If a person or someone else is in immediate danger, the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent help that is not an emergency, NHS 111 has a mental health option. Samaritans can be called free on 116 123 at any time of the day or night. In England, adults aged 18 or over can refer themselves directly to NHS talking therapies without a GP referral. These services are for anxiety and depression and are not for urgent crisis help.
