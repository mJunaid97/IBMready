    Heavy periods: what can cause them, which checks a GP offers and how they are treated

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them. The NHS advises a GP visit when they affect daily life.

    Open in the 3D explorerUterus & cervix anatomy

    Key points

    • Some people naturally have heavier periods than others, and the NHS says help is available when they affect daily life.
    • Often there is no underlying medical reason, though fibroids, endometriosis and some other conditions can cause heavy periods.
    • The NHS advises seeing a GP if heavy periods affect life, have lasted some time, come with severe pain, or if there is bleeding between periods or after sex.
    • Treatment choices include some contraceptives, medicines to reduce bleeding and surgery, and heavy periods do not always need treatment.

    What counts as a heavy period?

    The NHS describes heavy periods in practical terms, such as needing to change pads or tampons every one to two hours. Some people naturally have heavier periods than others, but the NHS says that when periods are so heavy that they affect life, help is available. Feeling tired or short of breath, or avoiding daily activities because of periods, are other signs that bleeding may be a problem.

    What causes them?

    Heavy periods often have no underlying cause. Fibroids, which are non-cancerous growths in or around the womb, are one of the identifiable causes. Other conditions that can cause heavy periods include endometriosis, adenomyosis, polycystic ovary syndrome and pelvic inflammatory disease. Some medicines, such as anticoagulants and some chemotherapy medicines, can also be responsible. Rarely, heavy periods can be a sign of womb cancer.

    When should you see a GP?

    NHS 111 Wales advises seeing a GP if heavy periods affect your life, have lasted for some time, or come with severe pain. Bleeding between periods or after sex is also a reason to see a GP. For severe period or pelvic pain that is worse than usual and not helped by painkillers, the NHS advises getting urgent advice, from NHS 111 if needed.

    Which tests might a GP arrange?

    A GP can investigate why bleeding is heavy, which may include a physical examination, blood tests or scans. A blood test to look for iron deficiency anaemia is usually offered to people with heavy periods. A pregnancy test, an ultrasound scan and, less often, a hysteroscopy (a look inside the womb with a small camera) may follow.

    How are heavy periods treated?

    Heavy periods do not always need to be treated. If they affect daily life, options include a hormone-releasing intrauterine system (IUS) or the combined contraceptive pill. Medicines to reduce the bleeding, such as tranexamic acid, and anti-inflammatory painkillers such as mefenamic acid or naproxen are also used. Surgical options also exist for heavy periods.
