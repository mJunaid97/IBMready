    What helps a nosebleed, and when should it be checked?

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Most nosebleeds can be treated at home by sitting forward and pinching the soft part of the nose, and some need urgent or routine medical checks.

    Key points

    • The NHS says most nosebleeds start just inside the nostril, where fragile blood vessels can break easily.
    • The NHS describes sitting down, leaning forward and pinching the soft part of the nose for 10 to 15 minutes while breathing through the mouth.
    • The NHS advises going to A&E if bleeding lasts longer than 10 to 15 minutes, seems heavy, or comes with dizziness or difficulty breathing.
    • The NHS lists regular nosebleeds, a nosebleed in a child under 2, and nosebleeds in people taking blood-clot-preventing medicines such as warfarin as reasons to see a GP.
    • NHS inform advises spitting out blood that collects in the mouth or throat rather than swallowing it.

    What causes a nosebleed?

    The NHS says most nosebleeds start just inside the nostril, where the blood vessels are fragile and break easily. NHS guidance lists nose picking and blowing the nose very hard among common triggers. A minor injury to the nose can also cause one.

    How does the NHS say to stop a nosebleed?

    The NHS describes sitting down and leaning forward, rather than tilting the head back. The soft part of the nose is pinched, just above the nostrils, for 10 to 15 minutes. Breathing is through the mouth during this time. NHS inform advises spitting any blood that collects in the mouth or throat into a bowl rather than swallowing it.

    When does a nosebleed need A&E or 999?

    The NHS advises going to A&E if a nosebleed lasts longer than 10 to 15 minutes or the bleeding seems heavy. Other reasons it lists include bleeding after a blow to the head, feeling dizzy, and difficulty breathing. The NHS advises against driving to A&E; someone else can drive, or 999 can be called for an ambulance. Some NHS trust leaflets give longer limits, such as 20 minutes, so the exact threshold varies between sources.

    When should a nosebleed be checked by a GP?

    The NHS lists regular nosebleeds as a reason to make a GP appointment. It also lists nosebleeds in people taking medicines that prevent blood clots, such as warfarin, which are also called anticoagulants. One NHS trust leaflet says people on these medicines whose bleeding does not stop can contact the GP or NHS 111. Leaflets also describe referral to an ear, nose and throat (ENT) department when nosebleeds keep coming back.

    What about nosebleeds in children?

    The NHS lists a nosebleed in a child under 2 as a reason for a GP appointment. Red Cross first-aid guidance for children describes sitting up and leaning forward, not tilting the head back, so blood does not run into the airway or stomach. It describes pinching the soft part of the nose and breathing through the mouth, with blood spat out rather than swallowed. A children's hospital leaflet advises A&E if bleeding goes on longer than 20 minutes despite first aid, a longer limit than the NHS page gives for adults.

    What treatment might a hospital offer?

    NHS inform says treatment may include ointments for the nose, cautery to seal blood vessels, or nasal packing. Hospital leaflets describe cautery as using a silver nitrate stick when bleeding continues.

    What helps after a nosebleed has stopped?

    NHS trust leaflets advise avoiding hard nose blowing for a time afterwards, and sneezing with the mouth open if needed. The same leaflets avoid hot drinks for a period, though they differ on how long, from about a day up to a few days. They also differ on nose blowing, with advice ranging from avoiding forceful blowing to up to a week.
