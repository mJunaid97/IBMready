    X-ray: what to expect, safety and results

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    An X-ray uses a small beam of radiation to picture bones, lungs and other parts. This guide covers the test, radiation risk, pregnancy and results.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Key points

    • An X-ray can produce images of many parts of the body, and the NHS lists fractures, teeth, lungs and the digestive system among them.
    • The NHS says a standard X-ray leaves no after-effects and the cancer risk is thought to be very small.
    • Trust leaflets say X-rays during pregnancy do not raise the risk of miscarriage, and that X-rays of the tummy or near the womb need special care.
    • The NHS says results may be given on the same day or at an appointment a few weeks later.

    What is an X-ray used for?

    An X-ray uses a beam of electromagnetic radiation to make an image of the inside of the body. According to the NHS, X-rays can produce images of many parts of the body, including bones, teeth, lungs and the digestive system. They are used, for example, to check for broken bones after an accident.

    What happens during an X-ray?

    The health professional who takes X-rays in hospital is called a radiographer. The radiographer positions the person sitting, lying or standing, depending on the part of the body. The X-ray itself takes only a few minutes, according to the NHS. The NHS says people are asked to remove metal items such as jewellery and zips. Some types need fasting for several hours beforehand.

    Which other tests use X-rays?

    The NHS lists several related tests, including angiography, which checks blood vessels using a dye injected into the blood. It also lists DEXA scans, which measure bone density, and CT scans, which give more detailed images. Studies of the digestive system use a barium drink or enema, a liquid that shows up on X-ray.

    How much radiation is involved?

    The NHS says a standard X-ray leaves no after-effects and the cancer risk is thought to be very small. The NHS says the risk of cancer from X-rays is thought to be very small. Trust leaflets say staff keep doses as low as possible and that a test goes ahead only when its benefit outweighs the small risk. GOV.UK safety advice says that when imaging is properly justified and optimised, the risks from X-ray examinations are far smaller than the risks of the conditions being investigated. GOV.UK safety advice says cancers linked to radiation can take decades to appear. The same advice says children have a higher lifetime risk from X-rays than adults, and that risk is lower for older patients because cancers take a long time to develop.

    Are X-rays safe in pregnancy?

    Trusts ask anyone who might be pregnant to tell the team before an X-ray. NHS trust leaflets say X-rays during pregnancy do not raise the risk of miscarriage and, at typical doses, do not cause birth defects. The main concern it names is a very small increase in the chance that the baby develops cancer in childhood. Trust leaflets say X-rays of the tummy or near the womb need special care during pregnancy. A trust leaflet says X-rays of parts of the body away from the tummy, such as the head, chest, arms or lower legs, are fine to have during pregnancy. A test may go ahead during pregnancy if the health benefit clearly outweighs the small radiation risk to the baby.

    How and when do results come back?

    A reporting radiographer or a radiologist, a doctor who specialises in imaging, reviews the X-ray. The report goes to the team that requested the test. The NHS says results may come on the same day or at an appointment a few weeks later, and that a clinician should explain them. Trusts quote different waits for the report, so timings differ between hospitals.
