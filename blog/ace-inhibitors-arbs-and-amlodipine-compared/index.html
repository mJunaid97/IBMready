    ACE inhibitors, ARBs and calcium-channel blockers: how do they differ?

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.

    Key points

    • The NHS says a doctor may recommend one or more blood pressure medicines, and the choice depends on blood pressure level, age and ethnicity.
    • A dry cough is a common side effect of ACE inhibitors such as ramipril and lisinopril, and ARBs such as losartan are a common alternative.
    • Ankle swelling is a common side effect of the calcium-channel blocker amlodipine.
    • ACE inhibitors and ARBs need regular blood tests to check the kidneys, and the NHS advises against losartan in pregnancy.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for a serious allergic reaction with a swollen throat or tongue, and NHS 111 for some other serious side effects.

    What do these medicines do?

    The NHS describes ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and calcium-channel blockers as medicines used to treat high blood pressure. Its examples of ACE inhibitors include enalapril, lisinopril, perindopril and ramipril. Examples of ARBs include candesartan, losartan and valsartan, and examples of calcium-channel blockers include amlodipine, felodipine and nifedipine. ACE inhibitors lower blood pressure by reducing a hormone called angiotensin-2, which narrows blood vessels. ARBs work in a similar way by blocking angiotensin-2. Calcium-channel blockers relax the muscle in the artery walls, which widens the vessels and lowers blood pressure. The NHS also describes amlodipine as a medicine to treat high blood pressure and angina, a type of chest pain.

    Which type is usually tried first?

    NICE guideline NG136 covers the diagnosis and management of high blood pressure in adults. The NHS says the choice of medicine depends on blood pressure level, age and ethnicity. In its first-step advice, NICE includes offering an ACE inhibitor or an ARB. If an ACE inhibitor is not tolerated, for example because of cough, NICE advises offering an ARB. NICE advises against combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB to treat high blood pressure. If blood pressure stays uncontrolled, the next step is a combination of an ACE inhibitor or ARB with a calcium-channel blocker or a thiazide-like diuretic, depending on the person's circumstances. The NHS says ARBs are often offered instead of ACE inhibitors because they do not typically cause a dry cough.

    What side effects are typical of each?

    For ramipril, the NHS lists a dry cough as a common side effect, along with dizziness on standing, headache, diarrhoea, vomiting and mild rash. It says dizziness is more likely when starting the medicine or moving to a higher dose. If a dry cough does not go away, doctors may suggest an ARB such as candesartan, irbesartan, losartan or valsartan. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) lists a dry cough, dizziness, headache and tiredness among the most commonly reported ACE inhibitor effects. For losartan, the NHS lists dizziness, tiredness, headache, tummy pain, feeling or being sick, and swelling in the legs, ankles or feet. The BHF lists dizziness, headache and feeling weak or tired as ARB side effects. For amlodipine, the NHS lists ankle swelling, headaches, dizziness and tiredness among the common effects, and says they are usually mild and last a short time. The NHS says to avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice with amlodipine because they can make side effects worse.

    What about the kidneys and potassium?

    The NHS says blood tests are done before ramipril is started and when the dose changes, to check the kidneys and potassium. It also says regular blood tests are needed with ACE inhibitors to check that the kidneys are working properly. For losartan, the NHS says long-term use can sometimes stop the kidneys working as well as they should, so a doctor checks kidney function with regular blood tests. The BHF also says regular blood tests check kidney function after starting an ARB. The NHS ramipril page says salt substitutes such as Lo-Salt are high in potassium, which can make blood potassium too high with ramipril. It says possible signs of kidney, liver, blood or pancreas problems with ramipril call for a doctor or NHS 111.

    What do they mean for pregnancy?

    The NHS advises against losartan in pregnancy. It says losartan can reduce the level of fluid around the baby, particularly in the second and third trimesters. The NHS says a doctor can usually switch to another option if pregnancy happens, and that any pregnancy should be planned carefully with a doctor or specialist. The NHS losartan pages differ on breastfeeding: one says it is not usually used, while another says it might be possible after talking to a doctor or pharmacist. For amlodipine, the NHS says it is not usually recommended in pregnancy because similar medicines are safer. It may still be used when other options fail and it is important that the condition is treated during pregnancy. The NHS advises telling the doctor about a pregnancy or a possible pregnancy.

    Who can advise about side effects or a missed dose?

    For a missed dose of amlodipine, the NHS advice is to leave it out and take the next dose as usual, without taking two doses to make up for it. It also says not to stop amlodipine unless a doctor says so. The NHS says to call 999 for a serious allergic reaction, with signs such as a swollen throat, lips or tongue and difficulty breathing. It lists angioedema, which is swelling of the lips, tongue and face, among the rare serious side effects of lisinopril. For lisinopril, the NHS says NHS 111 is the route for signs such as yellowing skin or eyes, unexplained bleeding or bruising, or severe stomach pain. This guide describes what the sources say and does not replace advice from the person who prescribes the medicine.
