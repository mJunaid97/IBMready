    Troponin blood test: what it shows

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    A troponin test measures proteins released when heart muscle is damaged, and a raised result does not always mean a heart attack.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Key points

    • Troponin T and troponin I are proteins released into the blood when heart muscle is damaged.
    • The more damage, the more troponin reaches the blood, and levels rise over hours, so the test is often repeated.
    • A raised troponin may point to a heart attack, but other conditions can raise it too.
    • The NHS says to call 999 for chest pain that is tight or squeezing, or that spreads to the arms, neck or jaw.

    What is troponin?

    Troponin T and troponin I are proteins found in heart muscle. They are released into the blood when the heart muscle is damaged, for example in a heart attack. The more damage there is, the more troponin reaches the blood.

    Why is a troponin test done?

    Clinicians mainly use the test to check whether a heart attack is happening or has recently happened. It is typically ordered when someone has chest pain or other signs of a heart attack. An NHS laboratory describes troponin as also helping to predict short-term and longer-term outcomes in acute coronary syndrome.

    How is it used in hospital?

    The NHS treats an ECG as an important early test in a suspected heart attack, and says it should be done within 10 minutes of arrival at hospital. Blood tests look for troponin, which leaks slowly into the blood after damage to the heart. Levels usually rise over hours, so testing is often repeated over several hours. NICE describes taking a second troponin sample after the first, with timing that depends on the test and the first result. High-sensitivity tests can detect much smaller amounts of troponin than older tests.

    What does a normal or raised result mean?

    MedlinePlus says normal levels hours after symptoms start make a heart attack unlikely, but doctors read results alongside symptoms and the ECG. Very high levels most often mean a heart attack has occurred. Raised levels do not always mean a heart attack, because other heart conditions can raise troponin. A UK study reported by University Hospital Southampton found raised troponin in some people with unrelated conditions, so results need careful interpretation. After a heart attack, levels may stay high for one to two weeks.

    How do the results fit with the ECG?

    The ECG and troponin results together help to identify the type of heart attack. A STEMI shows particular changes on the ECG. An NSTEMI is found when the ECG does not show those changes but blood tests show heart damage.

    When is chest pain an emergency?

    The NHS advises calling 999 for chest pain that feels tight or squeezing, or that spreads to the arms, neck or jaw. The NHS also advises calling 999 for someone who has passed out. The BHF advises calling an ambulance rather than travelling to hospital yourself, because paramedics can start care and tests on the way.

    What do NICE recommendations say about newer tests?

    NICE recommends high-sensitivity troponin tests as options for ruling out a type of heart attack called NSTEMI in emergency departments. NICE describes strategies using a single sample on arrival or a second sample after the first, depending on the test and result. A review notes that troponin levels tend to be lower in women than in men because of smaller heart size. NICE material describes the ECG as the first-choice test for a suspected STEMI, with the diagnosis based on changes in the ST segment.
