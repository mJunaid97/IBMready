    Vitamin D blood test: who needs one?

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    NHS guidance does not recommend routine vitamin D tests for people without symptoms, and testing is kept for specific symptoms or conditions.

    Key points

    • NHS guidance says not to test routinely for vitamin D deficiency in people without symptoms.
    • Testing is considered for bone pain, muscle weakness, or abnormal calcium, phosphate or parathyroid hormone results.
    • A lack of vitamin D can cause rickets in children and bone pain from osteomalacia in adults.
    • The NHS says everyone can consider a daily supplement in autumn and winter, and people at higher risk all year.

    What does a vitamin D blood test show?

    The test measures the level of vitamin D in the blood, usually as 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The body makes vitamin D under the skin when it is exposed to sunlight. In the UK, the sun is too low in the sky for the skin to make vitamin D from about October to early March. The routine test measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D because it is the best marker of overall vitamin D status and stays in the blood longer than the active form. The liver makes 25-hydroxyvitamin D first, and the kidneys then convert it into the fully active form.

    Who is tested and who is not?

    NHS guidance says not to test routinely for vitamin D deficiency in people without symptoms. Testing is generally considered for bone pain or softness in adults, a condition called osteomalacia, and for unexplained muscle weakness. It is also considered after abnormal calcium, phosphate or parathyroid hormone results, and before some bone treatments. People at higher risk who have no symptoms do not need routine testing but should be advised on a maintenance supplement. Higher risk applies to people over a certain age, those who get little sun and those with darker skin.

    What does a low level cause?

    A lack of vitamin D can cause rickets in children. In adults it can cause bone pain through osteomalacia. Some people notice tiredness or general aches and pains, which are easy to put down to other causes. In some cases the bones can become softer.

    How are the results read?

    Lab Tests Online UK describes results as deficient, possibly inadequate for some people, or sufficient for almost everyone. In the middle band, one NHS guideline says treatment may be appropriate for some patients. Local guidance varies, so the report shows the range used by the laboratory. A doctor interprets the result in the context of symptoms, bone health and any risk factors.

    Who is advised to take a supplement?

    UK public health guidance advises that everyone should consider a daily vitamin D supplement in autumn and winter. Scottish advice is similar and applies from age five upwards. People at higher risk are advised to take a supplement all year, including pregnant and breastfeeding women, children under five, and people with little sun exposure. People of African, African-Caribbean and South Asian origin are also in the higher-risk group. Clinical guidance warns that very high doses of vitamin D can be toxic and raise blood calcium. Local NHS guidance generally says supplements for prevention should be bought over the counter, with prescribing kept for diagnosed deficiency. The NHS advises that children aged 1 to 4 should take a daily vitamin D supplement throughout the year. NHS Inform lists people who are housebound, stay indoors for long periods or live in a care home as being at higher risk because they get little sun exposure.

    Where else does vitamin D come from?

    Sunlight on the skin is the main source in the warmer months. Few foods contain much vitamin D, so food alone is usually not enough.

    What are rickets and osteomalacia?

    Low vitamin D often causes no symptoms. Some people have symptoms such as tiredness and aches. Rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults are described as the best understood consequences of vitamin D deficiency. Darker skin and covering most of the body with clothing are recognised risk factors for vitamin D deficiency. Low levels can reflect limited sun exposure, low dietary intake or a problem absorbing vitamin D from the gut.
