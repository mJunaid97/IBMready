    Pelvic pain in women: common causes, red flags and which NHS route to use

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious. The NHS says to call 999 for severe or worsening pain.

    Open in the 3D explorerUterus & cervix anatomy

    Key points

    • The NHS lists period pain, ovarian cysts, endometriosis and pregnancy-related pain among the common causes of pelvic pain in women.
    • Rarer causes include ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease and womb or ovarian cancer.
    • The NHS advises calling 999 or going to A&E if the pain is severe, getting worse, or hurts when you move or touch the area.
    • Pain that comes and goes or does not go away is a reason for a routine GP appointment.

    What causes pelvic pain?

    The NHS says pelvic pain is more common in women. Common causes include period pain, an ovarian cyst, endometriosis and pain related to pregnancy. Rarer but more serious causes include ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, womb cancer and ovarian cancer.

    What is pelvic inflammatory disease?

    Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) is an infection of the female reproductive organs. PID is often caused by a sexually transmitted infection such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea. Bacteria that normally live in the vagina cause some cases.

    When is a routine GP appointment appropriate?

    The NHS suggests booking a GP appointment if pelvic pain comes and goes or does not go away. Bloating that does not go away, weight loss or changes in bowel habit are further reasons. Pain during sex is also a reason to see a GP.

    When should you call NHS 111 or an urgent GP?

    The NHS advises asking for an urgent GP appointment or getting help from NHS 111 for pelvic pain with difficulty peeing or pooing, blood in urine or stool, unusual vaginal discharge, or a very high temperature. The same advice applies if the person is or may be pregnant. For an ovarian cyst, sudden severe pelvic pain is a reason for an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111.

    When is it an emergency?

    The NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if pelvic pain is severe, getting worse, or hurts when you move or touch the area. Fainting or feeling dizzy, pain at the tip of the shoulder, difficulty breathing, heavy vaginal bleeding and sudden confusion are also on the 999 list. For PID, the NHS says to call 999 or go straight to A&E for severe pelvic pain or heavy vaginal bleeding, and not to drive yourself.

    Which tests might follow?

    If PID is suspected, a GP may take a sample of vaginal discharge to test, and an ultrasound scan may be used. Some people with PID have a negative swab result, so a negative swab does not rule it out.
