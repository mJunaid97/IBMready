    Vitamins and supplements: who the NHS says may need them

    Guide Published 3 November 2026

    The NHS says a varied and balanced diet should provide enough of several vitamins, but advises supplements for some people, including vitamin D in autumn and winter and folic acid in early pregnancy.

    Open in the 3D explorerSmall intestine anatomy

    Key points

    • NHS pages advise that everyone should consider taking a vitamin D supplement in autumn and winter.
    • Some groups, including people who are rarely outdoors and people with dark skin, are advised to take vitamin D all year.
    • The NHS advises anyone who is pregnant or could become pregnant to take a folic acid supplement until the twelfth week of pregnancy.
    • More is not always better: the NHS warns that very high vitamin D intakes could be harmful.

    Can a balanced diet provide everything?

    NHS pages say that a varied and balanced diet should provide all that is needed of some vitamins, such as vitamin A, while supplements are advised for some groups. The NHS says that from about late March or early April to the end of September, most people can make the vitamin D they need from sunlight on the skin.

    Who is advised to take vitamin D?

    The NHS says everyone should consider a daily vitamin D supplement in autumn and winter. In England the NHS advises all babies and children aged 1 to 4 to take vitamin D throughout the year, and NHS inform in Scotland describes babies and children under 5 as a higher-risk group. Some groups are advised to take it all year: people who are not often outdoors, such as those who are frail or housebound or living in care homes, people who usually cover most of their skin outdoors, and people with dark skin from African, African-Caribbean or South Asian backgrounds. For breastfed babies the NHS advises a daily vitamin D supplement throughout the year, and children aged 1 to 4 are also advised to take it year-round. Healthy Start vitamin products are free for eligible families.

    What does the NHS say about pregnancy?

    Anyone who is pregnant or could become pregnant is advised to take a daily folic acid supplement until the twelfth week of pregnancy. The NHS says some people need a higher dose, for example those with diabetes or taking medicine for epilepsy, and that this is arranged with a doctor.

    Can too much be harmful?

    For vitamin D, the NHS says there is an upper daily limit for vitamin D supplements above which they could be harmful, and this applies to adults, pregnant and breastfeeding women and children aged 11 to 17. For folic acid, the NHS says low supplement amounts are unlikely to cause harm, and that higher amounts can mask the symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency.

    What do some common nutrients do?

    For vitamin A, the NHS says most people should be able to get all they need from a varied and balanced diet. The NHS says vitamin D helps regulate the amount of calcium and phosphate in the body. It says that from about late March or early April to the end of September most people can make all the vitamin D they need from sunlight on the skin. Iron is important for making red blood cells, which carry oxygen around the body. Liver is very rich in vitamin A; the NHS says liver or liver products such as pâté should not be eaten more than once a week, and advises people who are pregnant to avoid them.
