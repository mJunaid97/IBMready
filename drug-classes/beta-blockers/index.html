    Beta blockers

    Also known as: beta-adrenergic blockers, the -olols

    Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand. Core treatment for angina, after heart attacks, in heart failure and for controlling fast rhythms. Selective (cardioselective) agents such as bisoprolol, atenolol and metoprolol are used mainly for heart problems, while non-selective ones such as propranolol also treat the physical symptoms of anxiety, tremor, migraine and an overactive thyroid, and eye-drop forms lower pressure in glaucoma. For high blood pressure on its own they are now reserved for people in whom other medicines have not worked. They are prescription-only, tiredness is their commonest drawback, and once started they are reduced gradually rather than stopped abruptly.

    Mechanism of action

    Adrenaline and noradrenaline speed and strengthen the heart through beta-1 receptors. Beta blockers occupy the receptors so the heart beats more slowly and less forcefully, uses less oxygen and lets fewer atrial impulses through the AV node. Cardioselective members (bisoprolol, metoprolol, atenolol) mostly spare the beta-2 receptors in the airways; non-selective ones (propranolol) block both and can provoke asthma. In heart failure, protecting the heart from chronic adrenaline drive improves survival when started low and increased slowly. Blocking beta-1 receptors lowers contractility, heart rate and the speed of conduction through the nodes; metoprolol, for example, flattens the slow depolarisation of pacemaker cells so they fire less often. Blood pressure falls through several routes, including less renin release from the kidney. Propranolol has additional membrane-stabilising properties that contribute to its antiarrhythmic effect, and because it is non-selective it also blunts the tremor, palpitations and sweating driven by adrenaline in anxiety and thyrotoxicosis. Beyond the heart the class is used for essential tremor, portal hypertension, aortic dissection, long QT syndrome and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; short-acting intravenous agents such as esmolol are used in intensive care for fast rhythms, and labetalol mainly for hypertensive emergencies. The same receptor blockade explains the drawbacks: beta-2 blockade can tighten airways and peripheral vessels, and by damping the adrenaline response the drugs can hide the shaking and racing heart that warn of hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes.

    Class effects

    • Tiredness, cold hands and feet, slow pulse
    • Vivid dreams, erectile dysfunction
    • Masking of hypoglycaemia warning signs
    • Dizziness, insomnia and nightmares; breathlessness in some people
    • Low blood pressure, especially combined with other blood-pressure medicines, causing dizziness or faintness

    Cautions

    • Never stop suddenly: rebound angina and fast rhythms
    • Asthma is a relative contraindication, especially for non-selective agents
    • Not combined with verapamil or diltiazem
    • Second- or third-degree heart block
    • Stopping suddenly increases heart rate and the force of contraction, which can bring on angina or let the underlying condition worsen; doses are reduced gradually
    • Diabetes: blood sugar control may be affected and the warning symptoms of a hypo hidden
    • Dizziness after a dose means no driving, cycling or operating machinery until it passes
    • Alcohol and some other medicines increase side effects
    • In asthma or COPD a cardioselective agent is preferred if a beta blocker cannot be avoided

    Class warnings

    • Can provoke bronchospasm in asthma (cardioselective agents only with specialist advice); avoid abrupt withdrawal after long use because of rebound angina and arrhythmia; mask the warning symptoms of hypoglycaemia in diabetes. UK
    • Not used in people with a history of asthma or bronchospasm unless there is no alternative and under specialist advice (cardioselective beta blockers carry the least risk), nor with marked bradycardia, second- or third-degree heart block, cardiogenic shock or unstable heart failure. UK

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.

    Guides and news

    • Underactive and overactive thyroid An underactive thyroid does not make enough thyroid hormone, while an overactive thyroid makes too much; the symptoms, causes and treatments differ.
