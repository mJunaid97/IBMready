    Loop diuretics

    Also known as: water tablets, furosemide-type diuretics

    Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour. Used to clear fluid in heart failure, kidney and liver disease. Furosemide, bumetanide and torasemide are the 'high-ceiling' diuretics whose effect keeps growing with the dose, so they are chosen when fluid must be shifted quickly or in large amounts. Their power is also their hazard: the same salt and water loss can cause dehydration, low blood pressure and disturbed potassium, sodium and magnesium, so kidney function and electrolytes are checked after starting and the medicine may be paused during vomiting, diarrhoea or fever.

    Na-K-2Cl cotransporter (NKCC2)

    Mechanism of action

    They block the sodium-potassium-chloride co-transporter in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, the segment that normally reabsorbs a quarter of filtered sodium. Furosemide competes with chloride for its binding site on the transporter, and because less salt is reabsorbed the medullary interstitium becomes less concentrated, so the kidney also loses much of its ability to reclaim free water. Salt that stays in the tubule carries water with it, so urine output rises several-fold. Potassium, magnesium and calcium are lost through the same blockade, and the resulting hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, metabolic alkalosis and raised uric acid are the predictable laboratory consequences, with hyponatraemia and dehydration when intake does not keep pace. Loop diuretics also reach the inner ear, where they alter the fluid of the stria vascularis and lower the electrical potential the cochlea depends on; the hearing loss this causes is usually temporary, but it can be permanent in severe kidney failure or when combined with other ototoxic drugs such as aminoglycoside antibiotics, whose blood levels furosemide can also raise. Thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics (bendroflumethiazide; indapamide) act further along the nephron and are the gentler class used for blood pressure.

    Class effects

    • Frequent urination, thirst, cramps
    • Low potassium, sodium and magnesium; raised uric acid and glucose
    • Dizziness from low blood pressure
    • Postural hypotension and fainting
    • Signs of serious dehydration: passing little urine, dark urine, light-headedness, tiredness, sunken eyes, confusion
    • Tinnitus and hearing loss, usually reversible, chiefly with high intravenous doses or other ototoxic drugs
    • Metabolic alkalosis; photosensitivity, interstitial nephritis and, at high doses in kidney failure, muscle pains

    Cautions

    • Kidney function and electrolytes within a week of starting
    • Hearing damage with rapid high intravenous doses
    • Pause on sick days
    • Interactions with lithium, digoxin, aminoglycosides and NSAIDs
    • Vomiting, diarrhoea or fever risk dehydration; the prescriber may advise pausing for a day or two until eating and drinking normally
    • Not suitable, or used with care, in low blood pressure, existing dehydration, liver disease, diabetes, difficulty passing urine, Addison's disease and gout
    • Lower doses are usual in older people
    • Alcohol adds to dizziness, light-headedness and dehydration
    • Combination with SGLT2 inhibitors such as empagliflozin increases dehydration and low blood pressure
    • High doses can cause profound diuresis with water and electrolyte depletion, so they are given under supervision

    Class warnings

    • Loop diuretics cause dehydration, low potassium, sodium and magnesium, and can precipitate acute kidney injury and gout; electrolytes and kidney function are monitored, and high doses can damage hearing. UK
