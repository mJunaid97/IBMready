    Opioids

    Also known as: opiates, narcotic analgesics

    Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl. Essential for severe acute pain and cancer pain; harmful when used long term for chronic non-cancer pain.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy
    Biological target
    Mu-opioid receptor
    Also called
    opiates, narcotic analgesics

    Mechanism of action

    Opioids bind mu-opioid receptors in the spinal cord, brainstem and brain, closing the gate on pain signals entering the cord and dampening the emotional response to pain. The same receptors in the brainstem slow breathing, in the gut cause constipation, and in the reward pathways produce euphoria and, with repeated use, tolerance and dependence. Naloxone displaces opioids from the receptors and reverses overdose within minutes.

    Class effects

    • Constipation, nausea, drowsiness, itching
    • Respiratory depression in overdose or with sedatives and alcohol
    • Tolerance, dependence, addiction

    Cautions

    • Start low, review early, plan the stop
    • Reduce doses in kidney failure and the elderly
    • Never crush modified-release tablets
    • No driving while drowsy

    Class warnings

    • Respiratory depression, sedation, constipation, dependence and overdose are class hazards; risk rises with other sedatives, alcohol, older age, kidney or liver disease and lung disease. Naloxone reverses overdose. UK
    • Long-term use for chronic non-cancer pain has limited evidence of benefit and substantial harm; UK guidance advises against starting opioids for chronic primary pain. UK

    Class interactions

    WithEffectSource
    Benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, alcohol and other CNS depressantsAdditive sedation and respiratory depression, potentially fatal. UK
    Serotonergic drugs (with tramadol, pethidine, fentanyl)Serotonin syndrome. UK
    Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (with fentanyl, oxycodone) and CYP2D6 variation (codeine, tramadol)Altered opioid levels and effect. UK
    Therapeutic duplication. Two opioids together (for example codeine in a combination product with a prescribed strong opioid) add sedation and respiratory depression; combinations are prescribed deliberately, never added over the counter. UK

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.

    Medications

    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.

    Guides and news

    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
