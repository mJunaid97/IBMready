    Combined hormonal contraceptives

    Also known as: CHC, combined hormonal contraception

    Combined hormonal contraceptives contain both an oestrogen and a progestogen, and include the combined pill, a skin patch and a vaginal ring.

    Open in the 3D explorerOvaries & fallopian tubes anatomy
    Also called
    CHC, combined hormonal contraception

    Mechanism of action

    They work mainly by stopping ovulation, the release of an egg from the ovaries. For the combined pill, NHS sources add that the mucus made by the cervix becomes thicker.

    Cautions

    • They carry a risk of blood clots in the leg or lung, heart attack and stroke, which the NHS describes as very small.
    • The UK medicines regulator found a higher relative risk of clots with drospirenone, gestodene and desogestrel products than with levonorgestrel, norethisterone or norgestimate products.
    • A history of blood clots, migraine with aura, breast cancer, liver cancer or severe cirrhosis, heart problems or complications of diabetes can make the combined pill unsuitable.
    • Some medicines, including some for TB, epilepsy or HIV and the herbal remedy St John's wort, can make the combined pill less effective.
    • The NHS notes that some hormonal contraceptives can change the effect of other medicines, such as the epilepsy treatment lamotrigine.

    Class warnings

    • Blood clots: for a possible clot in the lung, sudden difficulty breathing, chest or upper back pain, a very fast heartbeat or fainting needs a 999 call or A&E; for a possible clot in the leg, the NHS advises getting medical help as soon as possible, for example an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111. UK
    • The combined pill does not protect against sexually transmitted infections. UK

    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
