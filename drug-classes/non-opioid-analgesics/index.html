    Non-opioid analgesics

    Also known as: simple painkillers, paracetamol and relatives

    First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all, safe at normal doses for most people and most types of mild to moderate pain. NHS information describes paracetamol as a common painkiller and fever reducer that very rarely causes side effects when taken correctly; it can take up to an hour to work and keeps working for about five hours, and regular use over many years is regarded as safe provided the recommended dose is never exceeded. Unlike the NSAIDs it has no peripheral anti-inflammatory action and causes far fewer stomach problems, which is why it sits at the first step of most pain ladders and is combined with, rather than replaced by, other painkillers when pain is more severe.

    Mechanism of action

    Paracetamol acts mainly in the central nervous system, weakly inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase in the brain and spinal cord and, through its metabolite AM404, engaging cannabinoid and TRPV1 pathways that raise the pain threshold and lower the fever set-point. It has almost no effect on inflammation or the stomach lining. Its danger is the liver: in overdose a toxic metabolite overwhelms the liver's glutathione and destroys hepatocytes. The exact mechanism is still debated. StatPearls notes that paracetamol is historically grouped with the NSAIDs because it inhibits the cyclo-oxygenase pathway, yet it appears to do so in the central nervous system rather than in peripheral tissues, so reduced prostaglandin synthesis in the brain and spinal cord accounts for the analgesic and antipyretic effect without the anti-inflammatory, gastric and platelet effects of the NSAIDs. Most of a dose is handled by the liver through phase II glucuronidation and sulphation; only a small fraction passes through cytochrome P450 enzymes, chiefly CYP2E1, forming the reactive intermediate NAPQI. At therapeutic doses NAPQI is swiftly conjugated with hepatic glutathione into harmless cysteine and mercaptate compounds that leave in the urine. In overdose the conjugation pathways are saturated, more drug is diverted through CYP450, the limited glutathione stores are exhausted and NAPQI binds covalently to cysteine groups on liver proteins, particularly mitochondrial proteins, causing oxidative injury, failure of mitochondrial ATP synthesis and necrosis of the centrilobular (zone 3) hepatocytes. N-acetylcysteine, which restores glutathione, is the only approved antidote and is close to fully protective when started early after ingestion, which is why hospitals treat on the basis of the dose and timing rather than waiting for symptoms.

    Class effects

    • Very few at normal doses
    • Liver failure in overdose
    • Allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis with a swollen throat or tongue, a raised itchy rash and difficulty breathing, are rare but serious
    • Liver problems may first show as nausea or yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (jaundice)
    • Rarely, blood cell problems such as easy bruising and nosebleeds
    • Far fewer stomach effects than NSAIDs; constipation, nausea or vomiting are occasionally reported early in treatment

    Cautions

    • Maximum 4 g a day for adults, less at low body weight, with alcohol excess or liver disease
    • Count paracetamol hidden in cold remedies and combination products
    • Any overdose is an emergency even without symptoms
    • Early signs of overdose can be stomach pain, nausea or vomiting, but a person who has taken too much may feel entirely well, so medical advice is sought promptly regardless of symptoms
    • A single accidental extra dose is unlikely to cause problems, but advice is still sought if more than the recommended amount has been taken
    • Alcohol may be unsafe with paracetamol in people with liver problems
    • A pharmacist or doctor is consulted before combining paracetamol with other medicines, including some anti-sickness medicines
    • N-acetylcysteine is the antidote for overdose and is most effective when given early

    Class warnings

    • Paracetamol is safe at recommended doses but hepatotoxic in overdose; the maximum daily dose from all products is 4 g for adults, lower at low body weight, in liver disease and in alcohol dependence. UK

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).

    Guides and news

    • Ibuprofen and paracetamol together: NHS view The NHS says adults can take paracetamol and ibuprofen together if needed, but children should not be given them at the same time.
    • Migraine and tension headache compared Tension headache is usually a dull, tight pressure on both sides of the head, while migraine often throbs and brings nausea or sensitivity to light.
