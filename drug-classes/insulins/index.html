    Insulins

    Also known as: insulin therapy, insulin analogues

    Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2 diabetes when tablets are not enough. Regimens are built from insulins of different speeds: rapid-acting analogues taken with meals, intermediate and long-acting insulins taken once or twice daily as background, and ready-mixed (biphasic) insulins taken before meals; a once-weekly basal insulin (icodec) now also exists. Insulin is given by pen, syringe or pump into the fat under the skin, and the dose is adjusted continually against glucose readings, food, activity and illness. Hypoglycaemia is the price of effective treatment, so recognising and treating a 'hypo' is part of every insulin user's education.

    Mechanism of action

    Insulin binds receptors on muscle, fat and liver cells, moving glucose transporters to the cell surface so glucose leaves the blood, switching the liver from making glucose to storing glycogen, and stopping fat breakdown and ketone production. Modern analogues are engineered to act fast at meals (aspart, lispro, glulisine) or flat over 24 hours or more as background (glargine, detemir, degludec); mixed and pump regimens mimic the natural pattern of a basal trickle with mealtime bursts. Rapid-acting analogues (lispro, aspart, glulisine) are absorbed quickly after injection and lower glucose mainly by increasing uptake into skeletal muscle and fat. Glargine takes the opposite approach: it is soluble in the acidic solution of the pen but precipitates at the neutral pH of tissue, forming a depot that releases insulin slowly over about a day; detemir and degludec achieve a similarly flat profile by other means. In type 1 diabetes the usual pattern is a basal insulin once or twice daily plus a rapid-acting insulin before meals, the basal dose controlling the glucose the liver releases between meals and overnight; in type 2 diabetes treatment may start with a basal insulin alone, add mealtime doses later, or use a mixed insulin before meals. Insulin is injected into the abdomen, outer thighs or buttocks with a fresh needle each time, moving each injection at least a centimetre from the last, because repeated injection into one spot causes fatty lumps (lipohypertrophy) from which absorption is unreliable.

    Class effects

    • Hypoglycaemia: sweating, trembling, a fast heartbeat, tingling lips, dizziness, hunger and pallor; untreated it can progress to confusion, seizures and coma
    • Weight gain
    • Lumps at injection sites (lipohypertrophy), most likely when sites are not rotated or the same small area is used
    • Pain, bleeding, bruising, itching or redness at the injection site
    • Serious allergic reaction, rarely

    Cautions

    • Doses are individual and adjusted for food, activity and illness; 'units' written in full to avoid tenfold errors
    • Never stop basal insulin in type 1 diabetes, even when not eating
    • Driving rules: check glucose before and every two hours
    • Fast-acting sugar (glucose tablets, sugary sweets, fruit juice) is carried at all times; glucagon is used for a severe hypo in someone who is unconscious or cannot take sugar by mouth
    • Mealtime (rapid-acting) insulin is not taken until a low glucose has been treated; basal insulin is not simply omitted, and the care team gives a plan for low readings
    • Frequent or night-time hypos prompt a review of the regimen rather than being accepted
    • Overdose of a long-acting insulin can cause prolonged hypoglycaemia that outlasts a single treatment; older people are especially vulnerable
    • Insulin is brought to room temperature before injection, sites are rotated, and lumps are left alone until they soften
    • Hypoglycaemia impairs driving and other skilled tasks

    Class warnings

    • Hypoglycaemia is the main hazard, more likely with missed meals, exercise, alcohol, kidney impairment and beta blockers (which mask the warning signs); people on insulin are taught to recognise and treat it and must inform the driving licence authority. UK

    Conditions

    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
