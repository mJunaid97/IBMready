    Statins

    Also known as: HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, cholesterol-lowering drugs

    The main cholesterol-lowering medicines. They cut LDL cholesterol by 30-55% and reduce heart attacks and strokes in anyone at raised cardiovascular risk.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy
    Biological target
    HMG-CoA reductase
    Also called
    HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, cholesterol-lowering drugs

    Mechanism of action

    Statins block HMG-CoA reductase, the enzyme that controls how much cholesterol the liver makes. The liver compensates by displaying more LDL receptors, pulling LDL out of the blood. Lower LDL means less cholesterol deposited in artery walls, and statins also stabilise existing plaques so they are less likely to rupture and cause a clot. High-intensity statins (atorvastatin 40-80 mg, rosuvastatin 20-40 mg) are used after heart attacks and strokes.

    Class effects

    • Muscle aches in a minority
    • Headache, digestive upset
    • Small rise in blood glucose

    Cautions

    • Interactions with clarithromycin, some antifungals and ciclosporin; grapefruit with simvastatin
    • Unexplained muscle pain with dark urine needs a same-day creatine kinase test
    • Not in pregnancy or active liver disease

    Class warnings

    • Muscle toxicity (myopathy, rarely rhabdomyolysis) is the main class hazard; unexplained muscle pain, weakness or dark urine needs a creatine kinase test. Risk rises with dose, interacting drugs, hypothyroidism, kidney impairment and older age. UK
    • Contraindicated in active liver disease and in pregnancy and breastfeeding; liver function is measured before, within 3 months and at 12 months. UK

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).

    Medications

    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Simvastatin Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes.

    Guides and news

    • Cholesterol test results explained A cholesterol test reports total, HDL and non-HDL cholesterol plus triglycerides, and doctors read them with other risk factors.
    • Statins: common questions answered The NHS says statins lower the cholesterol the body makes, to reduce the risk of heart attacks and strokes, and they are usually taken long term.
