    Anticoagulants

    Also known as: blood thinners, DOACs, vitamin K antagonists

    Medicines that slow the clotting cascade to prevent and treat clots in veins and the heart: strokes in atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy
    Biological target
    Factor Xa, Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1)
    Also called
    blood thinners, DOACs, vitamin K antagonists

    Mechanism of action

    Direct oral anticoagulants block a single clotting enzyme: factor Xa (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban) or thrombin (dabigatran), with a predictable effect that needs no blood-level monitoring. Warfarin instead starves the liver of usable vitamin K, so factors II, VII, IX and X are made in inactive form; its effect builds over days and varies with diet and other drugs, so the INR test tunes the dose. Heparins, given by injection, activate antithrombin and are used in hospital and pregnancy.

    Conditions treated

    Class effects

    • Bruising, nosebleeds, heavier periods
    • Bleeding from the gut or, rarely, into the brain

    Cautions

    • Avoid NSAIDs and aspirin unless specifically advised
    • Doses adjusted for kidney function, weight and age
    • Stopped before surgery on a plan; missed DOAC doses lose protection within a day
    • Warfarin needs a steady vitamin K intake and INR checks

    Class warnings

    • Bleeding is the shared major hazard; signs such as blood in urine or stools, prolonged nosebleeds, coughing or vomiting blood, severe headache or unusual bruising need urgent assessment. Interacting drugs, kidney impairment and older age raise the risk. UK
    • Warfarin needs regular INR monitoring and is affected by diet, alcohol, illness and many medicines; DOACs are dosed by kidney function, weight and age and need periodic renal function checks. UK

    Aspirin + Anticoagulants (class)

    Specialist supervision or close monitoring
    Why it matters
    Aspirin with any anticoagulant (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran, heparins) increases bleeding.
    Mechanism
    additive bleeding risk
    What official information says
    The BNF advises that the combination increases the risk of bleeding and is used only when specifically indicated.
    General management information
    Combined only for a specific indication under specialist direction.
    What may be monitored
    Signs of bleeding · Haemoglobin

    Ibuprofen + Anticoagulants (class)

    Official guidance: avoid the combination
    Why it matters
    Ibuprofen with any anticoagulant increases bleeding, particularly from the stomach.
    Mechanism
    additive bleeding risk
    What official information says
    The BNF advises avoiding NSAIDs with anticoagulants because of increased bleeding risk.
    General management information
    Official guidance: avoid; paracetamol preferred.
    What may be monitored
    Signs of bleeding
    Basis of the status
