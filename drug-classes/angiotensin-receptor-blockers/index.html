    Angiotensin receptor blockers

    Also known as: ARBs, sartans, angiotensin II receptor antagonists

    Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on. They do the same job as ACE inhibitors without the cough, and are the usual alternative when an ACE inhibitor is not tolerated.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy
    Biological target
    Angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor
    Also called
    ARBs, sartans, angiotensin II receptor antagonists

    Mechanism of action

    Angiotensin II raises blood pressure by binding AT1 receptors on arterial muscle, the adrenal cortex and heart muscle. ARBs occupy those receptors so the hormone cannot act: arteries relax, aldosterone falls and the heart and kidneys are spared its growth-promoting effects. Because ACE is untouched, bradykinin is not raised and cough and angio-oedema are rare.

    Class effects

    • Dizziness, tiredness
    • Raised potassium, small rise in creatinine
    • Rarely angio-oedema

    Cautions

    • Never in pregnancy
    • Not combined with an ACE inhibitor
    • Sick-day rules as for ACE inhibitors
    • Monitor kidney function and potassium after starting and after dose changes

    Class warnings

    • Kidney function and potassium are monitored as for ACE inhibitors; contraindicated in pregnancy; not combined with an ACE inhibitor or, in diabetes, with aliskiren. UK

    Interaction records that name this class

    Lisinopril + Angiotensin receptor blockers (class)

    Contraindicated or avoidOfficial guidance: avoid the combination

    Lisinopril with any angiotensin receptor blocker (losartan, candesartan, valsartan, irbesartan, telmisartan) duplicates renin-angiotensin blockade.

    Why it can occur
    hyperkalaemia risk · Additive blockade of the renin-angiotensin system: more hyperkalaemia, low blood pressure and kidney injury
    What official information says
    NICE: do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB to treat hypertension.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official UK guidance: not recommended.
    What may be monitored
    Blood pressure · Potassium · Kidney function
    Basis of the status
    NICE NG136 1.4: do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Angiotensin receptor blockers.

    Conditions

    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.

    Medications

    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.

    Guides and news

    • ACE inhibitors, ARBs and amlodipine compared ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.
    • High blood pressure: what happens next High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms and is found by measuring it; treatment starts with lifestyle changes and may include medicine.
    • NIH-funded study: losartan and brown fat An NIH-funded study reports that losartan, a blood pressure medicine, raised brown fat activity in mice and cells.
