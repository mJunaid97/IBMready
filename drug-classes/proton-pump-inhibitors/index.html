    Proton pump inhibitors

    Also known as: PPIs, acid suppressants

    The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole), which heal ulcers and reflux by shutting down the acid pumps in the stomach lining. They are used for heartburn and acid reflux, indigestion, stomach and duodenal ulcers, and alongside antibiotics to clear Helicobacter pylori. Relief begins within a few days but full effect can take weeks. Most adults can take them, and children can when prescribed; low-dose products are sold over the counter for short courses in adults, with persisting symptoms a reason to see a doctor. Because the serious risks (low magnesium, weakened bones and fractures, gut infections, vitamin B12 deficiency) rise with duration, long-term use is reviewed regularly and stopped or stepped down when it is no longer needed.

    Mechanism of action

    PPIs irreversibly block the hydrogen-potassium ATPase, the proton pump in parietal cells that secretes acid as the final common step of every stimulus. Acid output falls by over 90% and recovers only as new pumps are made, so the effect outlasts the drug. Taken 30-60 minutes before breakfast when the pumps are most active. H2 blockers (famotidine) are the milder alternative. PPIs are prodrugs: absorbed in the proximal small bowel and carried in the blood to the parietal cells, they are activated only by acid-catalysed cleavage inside the acidic secretory canaliculi, which is why they act so selectively on the pump that makes acid. They are metabolised in the liver mainly by CYP2C19, the route behind their interactions and the variation in exposure between individuals. Suppressing acid has consequences beyond the ulcer: stomach acid normally kills swallowed microbes, so profound suppression allows microbial overgrowth and a higher susceptibility to gut and respiratory infections, including Clostridioides difficile diarrhoea. Absorption of some nutrients also depends on acid, which accounts for the vitamin B12 and iron deficiency seen with long use; hypomagnesaemia is rare, is most likely in people also taking a diuretic, and appears to reflect reduced intestinal absorption rather than renal loss. Acute interstitial nephritis is a recognised but uncommon reaction. Many of the other long-term associations reported, such as kidney, liver and cardiovascular disease and dementia, rest on observational data of low grade, so the practical response is the smallest effective dose for the shortest time rather than avoidance of a very effective class.

    Class effects

    • Headache, nausea, bowel changes
    • Low magnesium and B12 with long use
    • Small increase in gut infections and fractures
    • Stomach pain, constipation or diarrhoea and more wind than usual; headaches usually fade within the first week
    • Rash and dizziness
    • Low magnesium with longer use: tiredness, dizziness, muscle twitches and an irregular heartbeat
    • Rarely, allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) with a swollen throat or tongue or trouble breathing
    • Liver problems (yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, unusual tiredness) and severe or persistent diarrhoea signalling an inflamed bowel are uncommon reasons to seek help
    • Acute interstitial nephritis is a rare kidney reaction

    Cautions

    • Lowest dose, regular review; rebound acid after stopping
    • Alarm features (weight loss, swallowing difficulty, bleeding) need endoscopy first
    • Omeprazole reduces the activation of clopidogrel
    • A previous allergic reaction to omeprazole or another PPI, liver or kidney problems and osteoporosis are declared before starting
    • The prescriber is told if an endoscopy is planned, because acid suppression can hide problems that would otherwise be seen
    • Over-the-counter use is for adults only and for short courses; symptoms that persist need a doctor's assessment
    • Long-term use is reviewed by the doctor and not stopped suddenly without advice, because rebound acid can bring symptoms back
    • Pharmacist advice is sought about interactions with some blood thinners and seizure medicines

    Class warnings

    • PPIs can mask gastric cancer; long-term use is associated with low magnesium, fractures, C. difficile infection, vitamin B12 deficiency and subacute cutaneous lupus. The lowest effective dose is used and the need reviewed regularly. UK

    Conditions

    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.

    Medications

    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.

    Guides and news

    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
