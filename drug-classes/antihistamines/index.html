    Antihistamines

    Also known as: H1 antagonists, allergy tablets

    Medicines that block histamine's H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and itching. Newer members (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine) cause little drowsiness; older ones (chlorphenamine, promethazine) sedate. Second-generation agents are recommended first for allergic rhinitis and urticaria because they work as well with far fewer effects on the brain. The sedating first-generation drugs (chlorphenamine, promethazine, hydroxyzine, diphenhydramine) still have a place when itching or a streaming nose stops sleep, and some are used for nausea and travel sickness, but their anticholinergic actions make them a poor choice for older people.

    Mechanism of action

    When mast cells release histamine in an allergic reaction, it makes capillaries leak (swelling, runny nose), stimulates itch nerves and constricts airways. Antihistamines occupy the H1 receptors so these effects are muted. Second-generation drugs cross the blood-brain barrier poorly, which is why they are much less sedating. They are adjuncts only in anaphylaxis, where adrenaline is the treatment. The difference between the generations is largely physical chemistry: the first-generation drugs are lipophilic enough to enter the brain, where blocking H1 receptors produces sedation and slows psychomotor function, and they also block muscarinic receptors, causing dry mouth, blurred vision and difficulty passing urine. Second-generation drugs act mainly on peripheral H1 receptors and penetrate the brain poorly, so they relieve sneezing, itch, hives and watery eyes with little sleepiness, although drowsiness can still appear, particularly at higher-than-usual doses. Because of this profile, current guidelines favour second-generation agents as first-line treatment for allergic rhinitis and urticaria, and they are also preferred when an antihistamine is needed during breastfeeding. Alcohol, sleeping tablets and some antidepressants add to the sedation of the older drugs, and in older adults the first-generation agents are linked to confusion, falls and fractures.

    Class effects

    • Drowsiness (marked with older agents), dry mouth, headache
    • Sedating agents: sleepiness with reduced co-ordination, reaction speed and judgement; blurred vision and difficulty passing urine
    • Non-sedating agents: occasional headache, dry mouth or feeling sick; drowsiness can still occur, especially at higher doses
    • In older adults the first-generation drugs carry a higher risk of confusion, falls and fractures

    Cautions

    • Check driving response; avoid alcohol
    • Older agents cause confusion and falls in the elderly and urinary retention with prostate enlargement
    • Not a substitute for adrenaline in anaphylaxis
    • No driving, cycling or operating machinery after a sedating antihistamine, and alcohol makes the sedation stronger
    • Two sedating antihistamines are not taken together
    • Sleeping tablets and some antidepressants enhance the sedative effect
    • Chlorphenamine may not be suitable for people with epilepsy or primary angle closure glaucoma, who check with a pharmacist or doctor first
    • Liquid doses are measured with the syringe or spoon supplied, never a kitchen teaspoon
    • Second-generation agents are preferred if an antihistamine is needed while breastfeeding
    • If a non-drowsy antihistamine still causes sleepiness, a different non-drowsy agent is tried; taking tablets with food helps nausea

    Class warnings

    • Sedating (first-generation) antihistamines cause drowsiness, impair driving and interact with alcohol and other sedatives; non-sedating antihistamines are preferred for daytime use and for older people. UK

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Medications

    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Chlorphenamine A first-generation antihistamine, sold over the counter, that relieves hay fever.
    • Fexofenadine Fexofenadine is a non-drowsy antihistamine that relieves allergy symptoms such as those of hay fever and hives by blocking the effects of histamine.
    • Loratadine A non-drowsy antihistamine that blocks the effects of histamine to relieve allergy symptoms.

    Guides and news

    • Sedating and non-sedating antihistamines The NHS lists chlorphenamine as sleep-inducing and cetirizine, loratadine and fexofenadine as non-drowsy.
