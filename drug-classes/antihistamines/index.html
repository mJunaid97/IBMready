    Antihistamines

    Also known as: H1 antagonists, allergy tablets

    Medicines that block histamine's H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and itching. Newer members (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine) cause little drowsiness; older ones (chlorphenamine, promethazine) sedate.

    Open in the 3D explorerNose anatomy
    Biological target
    Histamine H1 receptor
    Also called
    H1 antagonists, allergy tablets

    Mechanism of action

    When mast cells release histamine in an allergic reaction, it makes capillaries leak (swelling, runny nose), stimulates itch nerves and constricts airways. Antihistamines occupy the H1 receptors so these effects are muted. Second-generation drugs cross the blood-brain barrier poorly, which is why they are much less sedating. They are adjuncts only in anaphylaxis, where adrenaline is the treatment.

    cetirizineloratadinefexofenadinechlorphenaminepromethazine

    Members with a page on this site are linked.

    Conditions treated

    AsthmaSepsis

    Class effects

    • Drowsiness (marked with older agents), dry mouth, headache

    Cautions

    • Check driving response; avoid alcohol
    • Older agents cause confusion and falls in the elderly and urinary retention with prostate enlargement
    • Not a substitute for adrenaline in anaphylaxis

    Class warnings

    • Sedating (first-generation) antihistamines cause drowsiness, impair driving and interact with alcohol and other sedatives; non-sedating antihistamines are preferred for daytime use and for older people. UK

    Class interactions

    WithEffectSource
    Alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines and other sedatives (sedating antihistamines)Additive drowsiness and impaired coordination. UK
    Antimuscarinic drugs (sedating antihistamines)Additive dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention and confusion in older people. UK
