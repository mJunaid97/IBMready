    Xanthine oxidase inhibitors

    Also known as: urate-lowering therapy

    Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day. Allopurinol is the first choice; it is also used to prevent uric acid kidney stones and to protect the kidneys from the surge of uric acid released when some cancer treatments destroy large numbers of cells. It takes several weeks to bring uric acid down, and the dose is built up gradually because starting high, or stopping for a few days and restarting at the old dose, raises the risk of a severe skin reaction. Febuxostat is the alternative for people who cannot take allopurinol or do not reach target on a fully adjusted dose; in the United States it carries a boxed warning for cardiovascular death and is restricted to those situations.

    Mechanism of action

    Xanthine oxidase converts hypoxanthine and xanthine into uric acid at the end of purine breakdown. Blocking it drops blood urate below its saturation point, so no new crystals form and existing deposits slowly dissolve. Attacks can increase for the first months as crystals mobilise, so an NSAID or colchicine is given as cover, and the dose is raised until the urate target is reached. Allopurinol itself is cleared from the blood within hours, but the liver rapidly oxidises it to oxypurinol, an active metabolite that also blocks xanthine oxidase and persists for most of a day, which is why once-daily dosing works and why the drug accumulates when kidney function is poor. A second, smaller contribution comes from the incorporation of hypoxanthine and xanthine into nucleic acids, which feeds back to suppress new purine synthesis. Oxypurinol is also central to the class's most feared harm: in people carrying the HLA-B*58:01 allele, which is most common in those of Korean, Han Chinese or Thai descent, it is thought to bind the HLA molecule and alter its shape so that CD8 T cells attack the skin, producing Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis or the broader allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome (fever, rash, eosinophilia and organ involvement), which carries a substantial mortality. Reduced kidney clearance lets oxypurinol accumulate, which is why the starting dose is cut in kidney disease and increased slowly. Febuxostat is a non-purine inhibitor of the same enzyme; a large cardiovascular outcome trial in people with gout and established cardiovascular disease found more cardiovascular deaths, most often sudden cardiac death, than with allopurinol, which led the US FDA to add a boxed warning, require a patient medication guide and limit its use to people with an inadequate response to a maximally adjusted allopurinol dose, intolerance to allopurinol, or in whom allopurinol is not advisable.

    Class effects

    • Rash, nausea
    • Gout flares in the first months
    • Nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea
    • Drowsiness, dizziness or poor co-ordination, so driving waits until these have passed
    • Stevens-Johnson syndrome: flu-like symptoms followed by a spreading red or purple rash that blisters and peels, most likely in the first weeks or after a dose increase that was too quick
    • Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome: fever, rash and involvement of liver, kidneys or blood
    • Rarely, liver problems (yellow skin or eyes) or falls in white blood cells (fever with sore throat, unexplained bruising)
    • Febuxostat: higher rate of cardiovascular death than allopurinol in people with established cardiovascular disease

    Cautions

    • Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome, rare but severe; HLA-B*58:01 screening in higher-risk populations
    • Interaction with azathioprine and mercaptopurine
    • Febuxostat: cardiovascular caution
    • Any rash is a reason to stop and seek medical advice straight away
    • Treatment is started low and increased slowly, particularly in kidney disease where oxypurinol accumulates; a lapse of several days is not followed by restarting at the previous dose
    • Severe skin reactions are more common in children, in people who reacted to an epilepsy medicine, in those allergic to trimethoprim and in those taking sodium valproate
    • Amoxicillin and ampicillin increase the chance of a rash
    • New medicines or supplements in the first months of treatment are checked with a pharmacist first
    • Febuxostat is reserved for people who cannot take allopurinol or do not reach target on it, and is used with particular care in established cardiovascular disease

    Class warnings

    • Allopurinol can cause severe hypersensitivity (rash, fever, organ involvement), more often with kidney impairment, high starting doses and the HLA-B*58:01 allele common in some East Asian populations; any rash is a reason to stop. Starting treatment can provoke gout flares, so cover with an anti-inflammatory is given. UK

    Conditions

    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
