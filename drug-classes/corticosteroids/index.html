    Corticosteroids

    Also known as: steroids, glucocorticoids, cortisone

    Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases, and as creams, drops and joint injections for local use. Short courses are generally well tolerated, but with high doses, long courses or several steroid products used together the hazards mount: bone loss, raised blood sugar, infection, eye and mood changes, and suppression of the body's own cortisol. In the UK, people on longer or higher-dose treatment carry a blue steroid card, and those judged at risk of adrenal insufficiency carry a red emergency card, so that any clinician treating them knows to continue the medicine and cover illness or surgery rather than let the dose lapse.

    Mechanism of action

    Glucocorticoids enter cells and bind receptors that move to the nucleus, where they switch off genes for inflammatory cytokines and switch on anti-inflammatory ones, shrinking the whole inflammatory response regardless of its trigger. The same receptors in liver, bone, skin, eye and brain produce the side effects of systemic use, and prolonged dosing suppresses the pituitary-adrenal axis, so the body stops making its own cortisol. Inhaled and topical forms deliver tiny doses where they are needed and largely avoid these effects. Alongside this slow genomic route, a faster non-genomic effect appears within minutes of a dose, including inhibition of phospholipase A2, which starves the inflammatory cascade of arachidonic acid. The synthetic agents (prednisolone, methylprednisolone, dexamethasone, betamethasone) tend to produce more Cushingoid change and more suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis than hydrocortisone; dose and duration are surprisingly poor predictors of who will be suppressed, and even inhaled and topical preparations can do it. Suppression may persist for up to a year after stopping, which is why doses are tapered and why hydrocortisone, whose short action lets the axis recover between doses, is the replacement used if adrenal insufficiency develops. Recovery of the axis is assessed with a morning cortisol and, where needed, a short Synacthen (ACTH stimulation) test. The symptoms of steroid-induced adrenal insufficiency are non-specific and overlap with both the disease being treated and the withdrawal syndrome itself, so clinicians keep a low threshold for suspecting it.

    Class effects

    • Systemic: raised glucose and blood pressure, weight gain, mood change, thin skin, bone loss, infection risk, cataracts
    • Inhaled: oral thrush and hoarseness
    • Short courses: dizziness, headache, swollen ankles, weakness or tiredness, and a greater likelihood of picking up infections
    • Months or years of tablets: thinner bones and fractures, poorly controlled or new diabetes (corticosteroids are the commonest cause of drug-induced diabetes), cataracts and glaucoma, muscle weakness, mood and sleep disturbance, and slower growth in children
    • Cushingoid change: weight gain on the upper back and abdomen, a rounded face, thin skin, slow wound healing
    • Adrenal suppression, so the body cannot mount its own cortisol response to illness, injury or surgery

    Cautions

    • Courses over three weeks must be tapered; carry a steroid card
    • Sick-day and surgery doses; adrenal crisis if stopped abruptly
    • Bone protection and a proton pump inhibitor for long courses
    • Live vaccines avoided on high doses
    • Blue steroid card for courses longer than three weeks or at high dose; red emergency card when the prescriber judges a risk of adrenal insufficiency; children receive an adrenal insufficiency card
    • Warning signs of adrenal insufficiency: nausea or vomiting, severe dizziness or fainting, muscle weakness, profound tiredness, mood change, loss of appetite and weight loss
    • Stopping suddenly after prolonged use causes severe tiredness, weakness, joint and muscle pain and risks adrenal crisis; doses are reduced gradually
    • Any infection, and contact with chickenpox, shingles or measles, is reported promptly because the immune response is blunted
    • Dental and medical staff are told about steroid treatment before any procedure
    • Using several steroid products at once (tablets, inhalers, creams, injections) increases the risk of adrenal suppression

    Class warnings

    • Systemic corticosteroids raise blood glucose and blood pressure, thin bone and skin, increase infection risk, disturb mood and sleep, and suppress the adrenal glands; courses over three weeks are tapered, and a steroid card is carried. Chickenpox and measles exposure needs urgent advice in non-immune people. UK
    • Inhaled corticosteroids at high doses can also suppress the adrenal glands and affect growth and bone; the lowest dose that controls asthma is used and the mouth is rinsed after use. UK

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Medications

    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.

    Guides and news

    • Reliever and preventer inhalers explained A reliever inhaler eases symptoms when they happen, while a preventer is taken every day to reduce airway inflammation.
