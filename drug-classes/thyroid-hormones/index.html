    Thyroid hormones

    Also known as: thyroid replacement

    Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate; the dose is tuned by the TSH blood test. Because the tablet replaces a hormone the body lacks rather than adding a foreign drug, it rarely causes side effects at the right dose: the symptoms that do appear (insomnia, headache, anxiety, sweating, hair loss, loose stools, shaky hands) are the signs of too much, and many years of over-replacement can contribute to osteoporosis and heart trouble. Treatment is usually lifelong; doctors start low, adjust the dose on blood tests, and warn that some people feel better within weeks while others take months. Most adults and children can take it, it is continued through pregnancy with closer blood-test monitoring, and it is usually compatible with breastfeeding.

    Mechanism of action

    Levothyroxine is identical to the thyroid's own T4. Tissues convert it to active T3, which enters cell nuclei and switches on genes controlling metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate, gut motility, growth and brain development. As levels normalise, the pituitary's TSH falls back into range, which is how the dose is checked. Liothyronine (T3) is used rarely, on specialist advice. The thyroid itself releases mostly T4 and only a minority of the body's T3; the rest is made in the tissues by deiodinase enzymes, especially type 2, and in someone whose thyroid has been removed these enzymes are the only source of T3. T3 enters the nucleus and alters the transcription of genes that set the pace of metabolism, which is why the effect on symptoms lags weeks behind a dose change. The TSH test is the preferred monitor because the pituitary is so sensitive to small shifts in free T4 that TSH moves out of range before free T4 does; a normal TSH is taken as evidence the dose is right. A small group of people have persistent symptoms despite a normal TSH, and a normal TSH does not guarantee a normal T3 in every patient, perhaps because peripheral conversion is impaired; this is the rationale for the specialist-only use of liothyronine (synthetic T3), which in the NHS is started by endocrinologists under shared-care arrangements. Absorption from the gut is incomplete and is reduced by food, coffee, calcium and iron, so the tablet is taken on an empty stomach at the same time each day, and the dose is reviewed when any interacting medicine starts or stops.

    Class effects

    • None at the right dose
    • Palpitations, sweating, weight loss and insomnia if the dose is too high
    • Signs of too much: difficulty sleeping, headaches, anxiety, sweating, hair loss, nausea or vomiting, diarrhoea and shaking hands
    • Rarely, an allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) with a swollen throat or tongue, raised itchy rash and difficulty breathing
    • A sudden rise in thyroid hormone levels can provoke a thyroid crisis, which needs urgent care
    • Many years of over-replacement may contribute to osteoporosis and heart problems

    Cautions

    • Take on an empty stomach, 4 hours apart from calcium, iron, antacids and proton pump inhibitors
    • Start low in the elderly and in heart disease
    • Dose often rises in pregnancy: check TSH early
    • Not for weight loss in people with a normal thyroid
    • A previous allergic reaction to levothyroxine, an overactive thyroid, diabetes, an adrenal gland condition or a heart condition are declared before starting, because the dose or timing may need to change
    • Coffee as well as food, calcium and iron reduces absorption, so the tablet is taken on an empty stomach and at the same time every day; an irregular routine distorts blood-test results
    • Pregnancy: levothyroxine is continued and the dose often needs to rise, so thyroid blood tests are repeated more often
    • Breastfeeding: usually compatible, checked with a pharmacist or doctor
    • Children's doses differ by age and are set by a clinician
    • Some antidepressants and calcium or iron supplements interact, so all other medicines are reported to the prescriber
    • Liothyronine (T3) is a specialist-initiated medicine prescribed under shared-care arrangements in the NHS

    Class warnings

    • Over-replacement causes palpitations, atrial fibrillation, weight loss, anxiety and bone loss; under-replacement leaves hypothyroid symptoms. The dose is adjusted by TSH, rechecked 6-8 weeks after any change. UK
    • Started at a low dose in cardiac disease and older people, because a sudden rise in metabolic rate can precipitate angina or arrhythmia. UK

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Thyroid hormones.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.

    Medications

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.

    Guides and news

    • US recall of one lot of thyroid tablets Vitruvias Therapeutics announced a US recall of one lot of Thyroid Tablets, USP 30 mg on 21 August 2026 because of possible superpotency.
    • Levothyroxine: timing and other medicines The NHS says levothyroxine replaces a missing thyroid hormone and is taken at the same time each day on an empty stomach.
    • Thyroid blood tests: TSH and T4 explained Thyroid tests read TSH and free T4 together, because free T4 helps to interpret an abnormal TSH.
    • Underactive and overactive thyroid An underactive thyroid does not make enough thyroid hormone, while an overactive thyroid makes too much; the symptoms, causes and treatments differ.
