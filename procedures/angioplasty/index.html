    Coronary angioplasty and stenting

    Also known as: PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention, primary PCI, stent, balloon angioplasty

    A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery, as emergency treatment for a heart attack or to relieve angina that medicines do not control.

    Open in the 3D explorerCoronary vessels anatomy

    What it is

    A narrowed or blocked coronary artery is opened from inside. A fine wire is steered across the narrowing through a catheter from the wrist or groin, a balloon is inflated to crush the plaque against the wall, and a drug-coated metal mesh stent is expanded to hold the artery open. As primary PCI it is the emergency treatment for a heart attack; electively it relieves angina. It is a non-surgical procedure carried out by a cardiologist in a cardiac catheter laboratory, with X-ray screening and an iodine-based contrast agent to show the arteries. Only local anaesthetic and light sedation are needed, so the person is awake, and the puncture needs no stitches. Several balloon inflations, and sometimes more than one stent, may be needed to achieve a good result. The stent stays in the artery permanently as a scaffold; antiplatelet medicines are then taken to stop a clot forming on it.

    Why it is done

    • ST-elevation heart attack (STEMI): open the artery within 120 minutes of diagnosis. Reopening the blocked artery quickly limits the amount of heart muscle that dies
    • Non-ST-elevation heart attack and unstable angina, usually within days of admission, once the coronary anatomy has been mapped by angiography
    • Stable angina not controlled by medication, or with a large area of ischaemia on testing. Angina symptoms usually improve after stenting, although the procedure does not cure the underlying coronary artery disease
    • Where many arteries are badly narrowed, or the left main stem is involved, bypass surgery is usually advised instead of stents

    Before the procedure

    • ECG, troponin, kidney function, clotting; a loading dose of aspirin and a second antiplatelet. Kidney function matters because the contrast agent can reduce it, especially where the kidneys are already impaired
    • Any previous reaction to contrast dye, iodine or medicines is recorded, because an allergic reaction to the dye is a recognised though uncommon risk
    • An explanation of the procedure, its risks and the alternatives (medicines alone or bypass surgery), and written consent
    • Nil by mouth if elective; in an emergency straight to the catheter lab
    • Local anaesthetic to the wrist; mild sedation. A general anaesthetic is not used

    Step by step

    1. A sheath is placed in the radial (or femoral) artery under local anaesthetic and heparin is given to stop blood clotting on the equipment
    2. Catheters are passed under X-ray screening to the origin of each coronary artery and contrast injected to map the narrowings; a brief warm flush is often felt as the dye goes in
    3. A guidewire is steered across the lesion
    4. A balloon is inflated for seconds to open the narrowing, with the ECG watched for ischaemia; a few inflations may be needed
    5. A drug-eluting stent on a balloon is positioned and expanded to the artery's size; more than one stent is sometimes needed. A final angiogram confirms flow
    6. The sheath is removed and the wrist compressed with a band, or the groin held under pressure or closed with a plug; no stitches are needed

    Afterwards

    • Bed rest for a few hours (longer for femoral access); home the same or next day for elective cases, 2-3 days after a heart attack
    • Bruising and soreness at the puncture site and a tender chest are normal for a few days; mild chest discomfort in the first days is often the stent settling, but persistent or severe chest pain is checked urgently
    • Dual antiplatelet therapy for 6-12 months to stop clot forming on the stent, then aspirin for life. The second agent is clopidogrel, prasugrel or ticagrelor; it is important not to stop these medicines early without the cardiologist's advice
    • Statin, beta blocker and ACE inhibitor as indicated; cardiac rehabilitation
    • Press firmly on the wound and call 999 for bleeding that does not stop; call 999 for severe chest pain; contact the unit or NHS 111 the same day for increasing pain or swelling at the site, a high temperature, or a limb that becomes cold, numb or swollen

    Recovery

    Back to light activity within days and work in a week or two for elective procedures; after a heart attack, structured rehabilitation over 6-12 weeks. Heavy lifting and strenuous activity are avoided for about a week or until the wound has healed. After an emergency angioplasty for a heart attack it can take several weeks or months to recover fully, and the return to work and driving is guided by the cardiac rehabilitation team. Angina usually improves, but symptoms can return, more often than after bypass surgery, so the medicines and lifestyle changes that slow coronary disease remain essential.

    Risks

    • Bruising or bleeding at the puncture site, including a haematoma under the skin, is common and not usually serious; rarely the artery itself is damaged, which can affect the blood supply to the hand or leg
    • Contrast kidney injury and allergy; kidney function usually recovers but the risk is higher in people with diabetes or existing kidney disease
    • Heart attack, stroke or death during the procedure (well under 1% electively, higher in emergencies). A coronary artery can be damaged or blocked by the equipment; this is usually repaired during the procedure, but on rare occasions emergency bypass surgery is needed
    • Abnormal heart rhythms during the procedure (uncommon)
    • Stent thrombosis (rare, but serious if antiplatelets are stopped early); restenosis, where the treated segment narrows again within the stent over the following months and years, affects a minority and is reduced by drug-eluting stents and medicines
    • Emergency bypass surgery (rare)
    • Risks are greater when the procedure is done as an emergency or when complex blockages are treated

    Alternatives

    • Medication alone for stable angina (equally good for survival in many cases); angioplasty is reserved for angina that medicines do not control
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting for three-vessel or left main disease, especially with diabetes; after bypass, symptoms are less likely to return than after stenting
    • Thrombolysis (clot-busting drugs) when PCI cannot be delivered in time

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.
    • Peripheral arterial disease A long-term condition in which the arteries that supply blood to the legs become narrowed or blocked.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Clopidogrel An antiplatelet medicine that stops platelets clumping by blocking their P2Y12 receptor, taken to prevent clots after a heart attack, stent.
    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
