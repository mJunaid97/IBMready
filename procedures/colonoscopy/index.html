    Colonoscopy

    Also known as: lower endoscopy, bowel scope, flexible sigmoidoscopy, bowel cancer screening colonoscopy

    A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps, take biopsies and diagnose bleeding, inflammation and cancer.

    Open in the 3D explorerLarge intestine anatomy

    What it is

    A flexible camera is passed through the anus around the whole large intestine to the caecum and often into the last part of the small intestine. Polyps found on the way are removed, which prevents them growing into cancer; this is why screening colonoscopy reduces bowel cancer deaths. The value of the test depends almost entirely on the bowel being completely empty: residual stool hides the lining, lowers the chance of reaching the caecum and of detecting adenomas, and can mean the examination has to be repeated. The preparation instructions are therefore followed closely. Most other medicines, including aspirin, are usually continued, while iron tablets are stopped beforehand because they coat the bowel wall. The test is done with sedation and a painkiller, or with gas and air, and the whole examination is recorded with photographs of landmarks and any abnormality.

    Why it is done

    • A positive stool screening test (FIT) or bleeding from the bowel
    • Change in bowel habit, unexplained weight loss or iron-deficiency anaemia
    • Diagnosing and monitoring inflammatory bowel disease, with biopsies to confirm the type and extent of inflammation
    • Surveillance after polyps or bowel cancer, and in high-risk families
    • Removing polyps, stopping bleeding, dilating strictures, decompressing a twisted bowel

    Before the procedure

    • Low-fibre diet for a few days, then strong laxative bowel preparation the day before, drinking litres of clear fluid until the output runs clear; red or purple drinks are avoided because the dye can look like blood
    • Iron tablets stopped a week before; blood thinners and diabetes medicines adjusted. Aspirin is usually continued; anticoagulants may need to be paused if polyp removal is likely
    • The laxatives cause nausea, cramps, bloating and frequent watery stools; drinking plenty of clear fluid prevents dehydration and salt imbalance, which matter most in the frail and in people with kidney or heart disease
    • Sedation and painkiller through a cannula, or gas and air; a driver is arranged if sedation is used

    Step by step

    1. Lying on the left side; the lubricated scope is inserted and advanced with carbon dioxide inflating the bowel
    2. The scope is steered around the sigmoid, descending, transverse and ascending colon to the caecum, changing position or with abdominal pressure to straighten loops
    3. The lining is inspected carefully on the slow withdrawal (at least six minutes)
    4. Polyps are snared with a wire loop and cut with diathermy, or removed cold if small; larger lesions lifted and resected in pieces
    5. Biopsies are taken from any abnormal or inflamed areas; all specimens go to histology
    6. Blood pressure, pulse and oxygen are monitored throughout when sedation is used, because sedatives can lower blood pressure and breathing

    Afterwards

    • Wind and cramps for a few hours as gas passes
    • Eat and drink normally; a little blood after polypectomy is expected
    • No driving for 24 hours after sedation
    • Histology in one to two weeks decides surveillance intervals
    • Severe abdominal pain, heavy rectal bleeding, fever, or dizziness and reduced urine output suggesting dehydration are reported urgently; bleeding after polyp removal can be delayed by up to a week

    Recovery

    Normal activities the next day. The findings are usually explained before discharge: a normal examination with good preparation is reassuring, removed polyps are classified by histology to set the interval to the next check, and biopsies of inflamed areas guide treatment of inflammatory bowel disease. If the preparation was poor or the caecum could not be reached, a repeat colonoscopy or CT colonography is arranged.

    Risks

    • Bleeding after polyp removal (about 1 in 100 for large polyps, sometimes delayed by a week); the risk is higher on anticoagulants
    • Perforation (about 1 in 1000-2000), more after resection of large polyps; may need surgery
    • Missed lesions (some per cent, especially with poor preparation); poor preparation also lowers the chance of completing the examination
    • Reaction to sedation, including low blood pressure or oxygen; dehydration and salt imbalance from bowel preparation in the frail
    • Side effects of the preparation itself: nausea, vomiting, bloating, cramps, headache and anal soreness, usually mild; occasionally the procedure has to be abandoned because of them

    Alternatives

    • CT colonography (virtual colonoscopy): no sedation, but no biopsies and needs colonoscopy if abnormal
    • Flexible sigmoidoscopy: examines only the left colon with an enema
    • Capsule colonoscopy in selected cases

    Conditions

    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Diverticular disease and diverticulitis Diverticular disease is a condition in which small pouches in the wall of the large intestine cause symptoms.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
