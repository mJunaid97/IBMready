    Blood transfusion

    Also known as: red cell transfusion, platelet transfusion, plasma transfusion, FFP

    Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia, blood loss or clotting failure.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    What it is

    Donated blood components given into a vein: red cells for anaemia and blood loss, platelets for low counts with bleeding, fresh frozen plasma and cryoprecipitate for clotting factor deficiency. Each unit is matched to the recipient's ABO and RhD blood groups and screened for antibodies, and identity is checked at every step. Transfusion treats a shortage of a particular blood component rather than 'blood' as a whole, so only the component that is lacking is given. It takes place in hospital, through a cannula in a vein, and the person is monitored throughout. All donated blood is tested for serious infections such as hepatitis and HIV before it is released, and the recipient's own sample is tested so that compatible units are selected (cross-matching); this is done before every transfusion except in a life-threatening emergency, when group O blood may be given first.

    Why it is done

    • Major bleeding from trauma, surgery, the gut or childbirth
    • Symptomatic anaemia that cannot wait for iron or other treatment; haemoglobin below about 70 g/L in stable patients (80 in heart disease). A transfusion is not given simply to correct a number: the decision weighs symptoms, the cause of the anaemia and whether a slower treatment would work
    • Bone marrow failure and chemotherapy, where cancers or their treatment stop the marrow making enough red cells or platelets
    • Sickle cell disease and thalassaemia (regular transfusions), which carry the particular long-term problem of iron overload
    • Reversing clotting problems before surgery or in massive haemorrhage, using platelets, plasma or cryoprecipitate rather than red cells

    Before the procedure

    • A group-and-save or crossmatch sample, labelled at the bedside from a wristband check. The laboratory tests the sample against donor units so that the blood given is compatible; the most serious avoidable harm is giving the wrong unit to the wrong person, which is why sample labelling and the bedside identity checks are strict
    • Consent, explaining benefits, risks and alternatives; baseline temperature, pulse and blood pressure. Previous transfusions, pregnancies and any earlier reaction are recorded because they can leave antibodies that make matching harder
    • A cannula in a forearm vein

    Step by step

    1. Two staff check the unit against the patient's identity and the compatibility label; most incompatible transfusions result from a clerical or identification error rather than a laboratory failure, which is why the check is repeated at the bedside
    2. Red cells run over 1.5-3 hours (faster in emergencies); platelets over 30 minutes; plasma over 30-60 minutes
    3. Observations are repeated at 15 minutes and at the end of each unit
    4. Any fever, chills, rash, breathlessness, back or flank pain, dark urine, flushing or dizziness stops the transfusion immediately for assessment; a mild temperature or rash may be treated with paracetamol and by slowing the rate once a serious reaction has been excluded
    5. Haemoglobin is rechecked after; each unit of red cells raises it by about 10 g/L

    Afterwards

    • Most people feel better within hours if anaemia was symptomatic
    • A sore arm or bruise at the needle site for a few days is common
    • Delayed reactions (jaundice, falling haemoglobin) can occur a week or so later, when antibodies formed against the donor cells destroy them; new symptoms in the days after a transfusion are reported to the care team
    • Women of childbearing age receive RhD-matched and K-negative blood to protect future pregnancies

    Recovery

    Usually a day-case or in-patient treatment with no recovery period of its own. The benefit depends on the reason for transfusion: symptoms of anaemia such as breathlessness and tiredness often ease within hours, while the underlying cause still needs treatment so that the haemoglobin does not simply fall again. People who need transfusions regularly are monitored for iron overload and for antibodies that can make future matching more difficult.

    Risks

    • Mild fever or hives (about 1 in 100); itching, rash, fever and headache are the commonest reactions and are usually mild
    • Fluid overload in the elderly and heart failure (the commonest serious harm), prevented by slow rates and diuretics; it is most likely when a large volume is given quickly and can cause fluid on the lungs
    • Acute haemolytic reaction from a wrong-blood error (rare but can be fatal), why identity checks are strict: the immune system destroys the donated cells, which can cause kidney failure, shock and clotting problems
    • Severe allergic reactions with breathing difficulty, chest or back pain and a fast pulse (rare)
    • Transfusion-related acute lung injury, bacterial contamination of platelets, and iron overload with repeated transfusion, which can damage the liver and heart
    • Alloimmunisation: forming antibodies against donor red cells, which can cause delayed reactions and complicate future transfusions and pregnancies
    • Infection transmission is extremely rare with modern screening (HIV about 1 in several million)

    Alternatives

    • Iron (oral or intravenous), B12, folate and erythropoietin for anaemia with time to respond
    • Tranexamic acid and cell salvage in surgery to reduce the need
    • Patient blood management: correcting anaemia before planned surgery
    • Giving only the component that is lacking (platelets or plasma rather than red cells) so that whole-blood transfusion is rarely needed

    Conditions

    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.

    Procedures

    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.

    Guides and news

    • Blood transfusion: why and how it is done A blood transfusion gives red blood cells, plasma or platelets from a donor to someone who has lost blood or has a condition affecting their blood.
    • FDA licenses first freeze-dried plasma The US FDA licensed Ezplaz Freeze Dried Plasma on 29 July 2026, the first freeze-dried plasma product licensed in the US, for adults who need plasma.
