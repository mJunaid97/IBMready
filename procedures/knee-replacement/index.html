    Knee replacement

    Also known as: total knee arthroplasty, TKR, knee arthroplasty, partial knee replacement

    The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when non-surgical treatment has failed.

    Open in the 3D explorerKnee joint anatomy

    What it is

    The worn surfaces at the end of the femur and top of the tibia are cut away and capped with metal components, with a polyethylene insert between them as the new cartilage; the back of the kneecap is often resurfaced. A partial replacement treats one compartment only when the rest of the joint is healthy, through a smaller incision and usually with a quicker recovery. The operation is considered when arthritis pain is severe enough to limit walking and daily life and is no longer controlled by tablets, exercise and injections. It is a major operation: some blood loss is expected and a transfusion is occasionally needed, and the body's response to surgery and anaesthetic carries general risks to the heart, lungs and wound healing that are higher in smokers, in diabetes and when immunity is weakened. The implant is a mechanical device that wears over time; most last for many years, but younger and more active people are more likely to need a revision operation at some stage.

    Why it is done

    • Severe osteoarthritis (or rheumatoid arthritis) with pain that limits walking, sleep and daily life despite painkillers, physiotherapy, weight loss and injections
    • Post-traumatic arthritis after fractures
    • Rarely, tumours or failed previous surgery

    Before the procedure

    • Standing X-rays; blood tests, ECG, MRSA screen, dental check
    • 'Joint school' education, prehabilitation exercises, weight optimisation; stopping smoking and good diabetic control lower the risk of wound problems and infection
    • An explanation of the expected benefits, the risks and the alternatives, and consent; the operation is planned so that pain relief, early walking and clot prevention start on the day of surgery
    • Spinal or general anaesthetic with nerve blocks

    Step by step

    1. A vertical incision over the front of the knee; the kneecap is moved aside
    2. Guided cuts remove a few millimetres from the end of the femur and top of the tibia, and worn cartilage and the cruciate ligaments as required
    3. Trial components are fitted and the ligament balance and alignment checked through the full range of movement
    4. The final metal components are cemented (or press-fitted) and the plastic insert locked in
    5. The joint is washed, local anaesthetic infiltrated, and the layers closed

    Afterwards

    • Standing and walking with a frame the same day or next; getting the knee moving early with the therapy team improves the final result
    • Physiotherapy from day one: bending, straightening, stairs; home in 1-3 days
    • Blood-thinning injections or tablets for 2-6 weeks; ice and elevation; compression stockings and early walking also reduce the risk of clots
    • Pain and stiffness in the first weeks are normal and are managed with regular painkillers; difficulty passing urine in the first day after a spinal anaesthetic is common and usually settles
    • Wound check at two weeks; six-week and one-year reviews
    • Contact the surgical team or NHS 111 the same day for a hot, red or increasingly painful knee, oozing or pus from the wound, a high temperature, or throbbing calf pain; call 999 for chest pain or breathlessness, which may indicate a clot in the lung

    Recovery

    Walking without aids by about six weeks, driving at 6-8 weeks, most of the benefit by three months and continued improvement for a year. A knee replacement usually lasts 15-25 years. Many people manage most light daily activities within a few weeks, and most of the benefit builds over about three months, and daily life improves for the great majority. A minority are left with lasting problems: limited bending, ongoing pain or stiffness, or a knee that feels unstable when standing or walking, for which further physiotherapy or occasionally another operation is considered.

    Risks

    • Deep infection (about 1%), the most serious, sometimes needing revision; superficial wound infections are treated with antibiotics, while deep infection can appear early or years later
    • Blood clots in the leg or lung, among the commonest serious complications despite prevention
    • Stiffness needing manipulation under anaesthetic or arthroscopy; scar tissue can limit bending
    • Loosening or wear over time; a small number never feel quite right
    • Nerve or vessel injury, fracture around the implant, bleeding into the joint, kneecap dislocation (rare); nerve damage usually recovers over months
    • General risks of major surgery: breathing problems, heart attack or stroke, poor wound healing, allergic reactions to medicines, and very rarely death

    Alternatives

    • Exercise programmes, weight loss, walking aids, bracing
    • Corticosteroid injections for short-term relief
    • Partial knee replacement when only one compartment is worn, with a smaller incision and faster recovery
    • Osteotomy (realigning the bone) in younger patients with one-sided wear
    • Arthroscopy is not helpful for degenerative knees
    • Steroid or hyaluronic acid injections

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.

    Symptoms

    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.

    Guides and news

    • General anaesthetic: before, during, after A general anaesthetic puts a person into controlled unconsciousness for surgery.
    • Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis Osteoarthritis makes joints painful and stiff; rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the joint lining.
