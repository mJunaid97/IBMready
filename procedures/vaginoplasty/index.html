    Vaginoplasty

    Also known as: gender-affirming genital surgery, penile inversion vaginoplasty, vulvoplasty, genital reconstruction surgery, lower surgery

    Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people, and occasionally for a vagina absent from birth (as in MRKH syndrome) or lost to cancer surgery. In the commonest technique, penile inversion, the skin of the penis, usually extended with a graft of scrotal skin, lines a canal made between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind; the glans becomes the clitoris on its own nerve and vessels, the testes are removed and the urethra is shortened. Other techniques line the canal with peritoneum or a segment of sigmoid colon, or omit the canal altogether (vulvoplasty, also called zero-depth vaginoplasty). Gender incongruence is not a mental illness: the World Health Organization classifies it under conditions related to sexual health, and vaginoplasty is one option that some, not all, trans women choose.

    What it is

    Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people, and occasionally for a vagina absent from birth (as in MRKH syndrome) or lost to cancer surgery. In the commonest technique, penile inversion, the skin of the penis, usually extended with a graft of scrotal skin, lines a canal made between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind; the glans becomes the clitoris on its own nerve and vessels, the testes are removed and the urethra is shortened. Other techniques line the canal with peritoneum or a segment of sigmoid colon, or omit the canal altogether (vulvoplasty, also called zero-depth vaginoplasty). Gender incongruence is not a mental illness: the World Health Organization classifies it under conditions related to sexual health, and vaginoplasty is one option that some, not all, trans women choose.

    Why it is done

    • Gender incongruence in trans women and some non-binary people who want female genital anatomy, after assessment under the WPATH Standards of Care
    • Congenital absence or underdevelopment of the vagina, for example in MRKH syndrome, or loss of the vagina to cancer surgery
    • Vulvoplasty for people who want the external anatomy without a canal, or for whom lifelong dilation is not practical
    • Revision of an earlier vaginoplasty for stenosis, loss of depth, granulation tissue or an unsatisfactory appearance

    Before the procedure

    • Assessment by a gender identity service following the WPATH Standards of Care: a settled decision, capacity to consent, other physical and mental health conditions well managed, and usually a period of gender-affirming hormone therapy where hormones are wanted and suitable (WPATH SOC8 suggests at least six months, and UK NHS pathways have typically asked for about 12 months)
    • Discussion of fertility, with the option of storing sperm, because removal of the testes ends sperm production
    • Permanent hair removal (electrolysis or laser) from the penile and scrotal skin that will line the canal, completed over the preceding months
    • Stopping smoking, with weight and diabetes optimised; oestrogen may be paused for some weeks around surgery to lower the risk of venous thromboembolism, but practice varies and many teams now continue it, especially transdermal preparations; the decision is made with the surgical team
    • Bowel preparation, fasting, general anaesthetic, antibiotics at induction and clot-prevention measures (compression stockings, anticoagulant injections)

    Step by step

    1. Under general anaesthetic the legs are raised in the lithotomy position and a urinary catheter placed; the testes and spermatic cords are removed through a scrotal incision (orchidectomy) unless this has been done earlier
    2. The penile skin is freed from the shaft as a tube; the glans is reduced and kept on its dorsal neurovascular bundle to become the clitoris, the urethra is separated and shortened, and the corpora cavernosa are largely removed
    3. A canal is dissected from the perineum between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind, passing through the pelvic floor between the pubococcygeus muscles, with the rectal wall checked for injury
    4. The penile skin tube, extended with a hair-free scrotal skin graft if extra depth is needed, is turned inside out and drawn into the canal to become its lining
    5. The clitoris is set at the top of the vestibule with the shortened urethra opening below it; the labia majora are formed from scrotal skin and the labia minora and vestibule from penile or urethral skin
    6. The canal is packed with gauze to hold the lining against its walls, the wounds are dressed and the catheter is left in place
    7. In peritoneal or intestinal vaginoplasty a laparoscopic stage lines the canal with flaps of peritoneum or a segment of sigmoid colon, chosen where penile skin is scarce or as a revision

    Afterwards

    • Several days in hospital, with bed rest at first; the packing and catheter are removed and the wounds checked before discharge
    • Dilation begins once the packing is out and follows a schedule that is frequent at first and reduces over months to a lifelong maintenance routine; regular rinsing of the canal clears debris; the lining is skin and does not lubricate with arousal, so lubricant is used for dilation and for sex
    • No penetrative sex for about three months, and no cycling, swimming or strenuous exercise until the wounds have healed; if oestrogen was paused it is restarted once the person is mobile, as the team directs
    • Follow-up with the surgical team, and pelvic floor physiotherapy if dilation is painful; the prostate remains, so prostate care continues, including PSA testing when indicated, interpreted knowing that removing the testes and suppressing androgens lower PSA, so a result in the usual range can be falsely reassuring; there is no cervix, so cervical screening does not apply; breast screening depends on how the person is registered with their GP: trans women registered as female are invited routinely, and those registered as male are not invited automatically but can ask their GP or the local screening service
    • Urgent review is needed for fever, heavy bleeding, increasing pain or swelling, inability to pass urine, or faeces or gas appearing in the canal, and for calf pain or breathlessness, which can signal a clot

    Recovery

    Most people are back to normal daily activity within 6-12 weeks; swelling settles and the tissues soften over the following months, and full healing, with the final appearance and sensation, takes up to a year. Dilation continues for life, although the frequency can usually be reduced once healing is complete and for people who have regular receptive penetrative sex.

    Risks

    • Bleeding, haematoma, infection and wound breakdown along the labia or introitus, which usually heals with dressings
    • Granulation tissue, narrowing (stenosis) or loss of depth if dilation lapses, sometimes needing revision surgery
    • Injury to the rectum during the dissection and, rarely, a fistula between the canal and the rectum or urethra
    • Urinary problems (a spraying stream, retention, urinary tract infection) and altered or reduced clitoral sensation
    • Venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), guarded against with compression stockings, anticoagulant injections and early mobilisation, and sometimes by pausing oestrogen around surgery
    • Regret, which is reported to be rare

    Alternatives

    • Vulvoplasty (zero-depth vaginoplasty): the external anatomy without a canal, avoiding dilation
    • Orchidectomy alone, which removes the testes and the need for a testosterone-suppressing medicine
    • Hormone therapy, hair removal and other gender-affirming care without genital surgery; not everyone wants or needs surgery
    • Peritoneal or intestinal vaginoplasty where penile and scrotal skin are insufficient, or as revision after loss of depth
