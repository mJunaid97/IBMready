    Cataract surgery

    Also known as: phacoemulsification, lens replacement, cataract extraction

    The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens, usually under local anaesthetic as a day case.

    Open in the 3D explorerEye anatomy

    What it is

    The cloudy natural lens of the eye is broken up with ultrasound through a 2-3 mm incision at the edge of the cornea and sucked out, and a folded plastic lens is injected into the empty lens capsule where it unfolds. Done under drops or a local anaesthetic injection in 15-30 minutes, it is the commonest operation in the world. The technique is called phacoemulsification. The lens capsule, the thin bag that held the natural lens, is deliberately left in place to hold the implant, and the power of the implant is chosen from measurements of the eye taken beforehand. The person is awake and may be aware of light and movement but does not see the surgery itself; a steady flow of salty fluid over the eye during the operation is normal. Most people see better afterwards, although many still need glasses for reading or fine distance focus.

    Why it is done

    • A cataract that blurs vision or causes glare enough to affect daily life, driving or work; there is no fixed level of vision at which surgery becomes necessary, and the decision rests on how much the cataract interferes with what the person needs to do
    • Rarely, to see the retina for treating diabetic eye disease, or to lower eye pressure in glaucoma

    Before the procedure

    • Eye measurements (biometry) to choose the lens power, aiming for distance or near focus; these are usually taken at a pre-assessment clinic, and the choice of focus is discussed because most people need glasses for whatever distance the implant is not set for
    • Drops to dilate the pupil on the day; anaesthetic drops or a small injection. Dilating drops blur vision for several hours, so driving to and from the appointment is not possible
    • No need to fast for local anaesthetic; regular medicines continued

    Step by step

    1. A tiny self-sealing incision is made at the edge of the cornea
    2. A circular opening is torn in the front of the lens capsule (capsulorhexis), keeping the rest of the capsule intact to hold the new lens
    3. An ultrasound probe emulsifies the lens nucleus, which is aspirated along with the softer cortex; the empty capsule is left behind
    4. The folded intraocular lens is injected into the capsule and unfolds into place
    5. The incision seals without stitches; antibiotic is placed in the eye. A stitch is occasionally needed if the wound does not self-seal

    Afterwards

    • Home within an hour with a clear shield to wear at night for a week; someone else drives
    • Antibiotic and anti-inflammatory drops for about four weeks
    • Vision usually clears within days; new glasses after 4-6 weeks. Grittiness, a mild ache and redness are normal at first and fade over a few days, though the eye can take a month or more to settle fully; colours often look brighter or bluer than before
    • Avoid rubbing the eye, swimming and heavy lifting for a couple of weeks, and keep soap and shampoo out of the eye
    • Sudden worsening of vision, increasing pain, or new flashes and shadows are reported to the eye clinic at once because they can signal infection, inflammation or retinal detachment

    Recovery

    Most people see well within a few days and resume driving within one to two weeks once the eye meets the standard. The second eye is done weeks later. Discomfort is usually mild and short-lived, and the small incision heals on its own. Glasses are often still needed, almost always for reading and close work and sometimes to fine-tune distance vision. Months or years later the capsule behind the implant can thicken and cloud vision again; this is treated with a quick outpatient laser procedure and does not mean the cataract has returned.

    Risks

    • Posterior capsule opacification (cloudy membrane behind the lens) in up to a fifth over years, cleared with a quick laser
    • Infection inside the eye (endophthalmitis, about 1 in 1000), sight-threatening; sterile technique and antibiotics at the end of surgery reduce the risk
    • Tear of the capsule, retinal detachment, swelling of the retina, raised pressure. A tear in the back of the capsule is the main complication during surgery: it can let the gel inside the eye come forward, raises the later risk of retinal detachment and may mean the lens has to be placed differently
    • Bleeding inside the eye (rare) and inflammation after surgery that needs stronger treatment
    • Minor problems such as soreness, swelling or a mild surface infection are the commonest and settle with drops
    • A refractive surprise needing glasses or a lens exchange

    Alternatives

    • Stronger glasses and better lighting while the cataract is mild
    • No surgery, if vision is acceptable; cataracts do not damage the eye by waiting except when very advanced
