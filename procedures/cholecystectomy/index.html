    Cholecystectomy

    Also known as: gallbladder removal, laparoscopic cholecystectomy, lap chole

    Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile, which flows straight into the duodenum.

    Open in the 3D explorerGallbladder & bile ducts anatomy

    What it is

    Removal of the gallbladder, the small sac under the liver that stores bile. The commonest planned abdominal operation, almost always done by keyhole surgery. The liver keeps making bile, which then flows straight into the duodenum. Surgery is the usual treatment for gallstones because, once stones have caused symptoms, they tend to do so again; gallstones that cause no symptoms generally need no treatment, although symptoms may appear later. The operation is done under general anaesthetic and usually takes one to two hours. Compared with the open operation, the keyhole approach means smaller wounds, less pain, a shorter hospital stay, a faster return to work and fewer wound infections. An open cholecystectomy is chosen when the gallbladder is severely inflamed, infected or scarred from previous operations, and the surgeon may switch to open surgery if problems arise during a keyhole procedure. The gallbladder is not an essential organ and people live normally without it, though bile then enters the gut continuously rather than in response to meals.

    Why it is done

    • Gallstones causing repeated pain (biliary colic)
    • Acute cholecystitis (an inflamed, infected gallbladder)
    • Gallstone pancreatitis, once recovered, to prevent recurrence
    • Gallbladder polyps above 10 mm or a porcelain gallbladder (cancer risk)
    • A gallbladder that does not empty properly (biliary dyskinesia) causing typical pain without stones, in selected cases

    Before the procedure

    • Ultrasound confirming stones; liver function tests; MRCP or ERCP if stones are suspected in the bile duct. Deranged liver tests or a dilated duct raise the suspicion of duct stones, which are cleared endoscopically before or around the time of surgery
    • Fasting; general anaesthetic
    • Antibiotics at induction if inflamed
    • Explanation of the operation, the possibility of conversion to open surgery and the risk of bile duct injury, and consent

    Step by step

    1. Four ports: one at the navel for the camera, three under the right ribs; carbon dioxide inflates the abdomen to create working space
    2. The gallbladder is retracted upward to expose Calot's triangle
    3. The cystic duct and cystic artery are identified with a 'critical view of safety', clipped and divided
    4. The gallbladder is peeled off the liver bed with diathermy and removed in a bag through the navel port; the bag prevents stones spilling into the abdomen, where they can cause abscesses long afterwards
    5. An X-ray of the bile duct (cholangiogram) may be done during the operation
    6. If the anatomy cannot be seen safely because of inflammation, scarring or bleeding, the operation is converted to an open incision under the right ribs

    Afterwards

    • Home the same day or next morning
    • Shoulder-tip pain from residual gas for a day or two; some pain or discomfort at the wounds for a few days
    • Wounds are kept clean and dry; showering is usually allowed after a couple of days, with baths and swimming delayed until the wounds have healed
    • Normal diet resumed; some people notice looser stools after fatty meals
    • Worsening pain, fever, redness or discharge from a wound, persistent vomiting or yellowing of the skin or eyes are reported promptly, because they can indicate infection, a bile leak or a retained duct stone

    Recovery

    Most people return to normal activities within two weeks; heavy lifting avoided for a few weeks. After an uncomplicated keyhole operation many people are back to normal physical activity in about a week, while recovery from open surgery takes longer. Digestion adjusts to the continuous flow of bile; a minority have ongoing loose stools or intolerance of fatty food, and a small number develop persistent pain, bloating or diarrhoea (post-cholecystectomy syndrome) that needs further assessment.

    Risks

    • Bile leak from the liver bed or cystic duct stump (1-2%), which can cause bile peritonitis or an abscess and may need a drain or endoscopic stent
    • Injury to the common bile duct (about 1 in 300), the serious complication, which may need specialist reconstructive surgery
    • Bleeding, infection of a wound or inside the abdomen, retained stones in the duct, pancreatitis
    • Stones spilled during surgery, which rarely cause abscesses or pain months or years later
    • Conversion to open surgery (a few per cent, more in acute inflammation), which is a safety decision rather than a complication
    • General anaesthetic risks and blood clots in the legs or lungs

    Alternatives

    • Watchful waiting for stones with no symptoms, which usually need no treatment
    • Antibiotics and a drainage tube (cholecystostomy) in patients too unwell for surgery
    • Bile-acid tablets for tiny stones (slow, often recur); non-surgical treatments are only an option for cholesterol stones, not pigment stones

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Cholecystectomy.

    Conditions

    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
