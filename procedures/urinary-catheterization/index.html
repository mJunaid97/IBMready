    Urinary catheterisation

    Also known as: urinary catheter, Foley catheter, indwelling catheter, intermittent self-catheterisation, suprapubic catheter

    A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be measured, for hours, days or long term.

    Open in the 3D explorerUrinary bladder & urethra anatomy

    What it is

    A soft tube passed along the urethra into the bladder to drain urine, held in place by a small balloon inflated inside the bladder. Used for hours during surgery, days on a ward, or long term when the bladder cannot empty. A suprapubic catheter enters through the lower abdominal wall instead; intermittent catheterisation passes a tube several times a day and removes it each time. Intermittent catheters are usually single-use and are passed either at set times or when the bladder feels full; they avoid the continuous presence of a foreign body in the bladder and so carry fewer long-term problems. An indwelling catheter drains into a leg bag or, with a valve, is emptied directly into the toilet. A suprapubic catheter is chosen when the urethra is damaged or blocked, or when intermittent catheterisation is not possible. Catheter-associated urinary infection is one of the commonest healthcare-associated infections, and the risk rises the longer a catheter is in place, so catheters that are not needed are avoided and removed as soon as possible. Most people with a long-term catheter can live a relatively normal life, including work, exercise, swimming, travel and sex, with guidance from a continence nurse.

    Why it is done

    • Acute urinary retention (usually from an enlarged prostate) or chronic retention with kidney damage
    • Measuring urine output hour by hour in critical illness and major surgery
    • Neurological bladder problems (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis), often by self-catheterisation
    • Bladder irrigation after prostate or bladder surgery, or with clots
    • Incontinence with skin breakdown when other measures fail (last resort)

    Before the procedure

    • Explanation and consent; checking for latex allergy and previous difficult catheters. A history of prostate enlargement, urethral stricture or earlier failed attempts predicts a difficult insertion, when a urologist or specialist nurse is involved early rather than after repeated tries
    • Sterile technique: hand hygiene, gloves, cleaning the urethral opening
    • Anaesthetic lubricant gel instilled into the urethra and left for a few minutes
    • The smallest catheter that will drain adequately is chosen, because larger tubes put more pressure on the lining of the urethra

    Step by step

    1. The catheter (usually 12-16 Fr) is advanced gently along the urethra; in women the short urethra opens just above the vagina, and in men the penis is held upright to straighten the first bend, then lowered to pass the prostate
    2. Urine flows once the tip is in the bladder; the catheter is advanced further before the balloon is inflated with 10 mL of sterile water. The balloon is never inflated until urine has been seen, because inflating it in the urethra causes serious injury
    3. It is drawn back until the balloon rests at the bladder neck and connected to a drainage bag or valve
    4. The volume drained is recorded; very large volumes are drained in stages if chronic retention is suspected
    5. The catheter is secured to the thigh to prevent traction; the insertion, catheter size and planned review date are documented, often in a catheter passport the person keeps
    6. If the catheter will not pass, force is not used: repeated failed attempts cause urethral trauma, false passages, bleeding and later strictures, so a different technique or specialist help is sought

    Afterwards

    • Daily cleaning of the meatus, keeping the bag below bladder level and changing bags on schedule
    • Removal (trial without catheter) as soon as it is no longer needed, usually within days; every day in place adds to the infection risk
    • Long-term catheters are changed every 4-12 weeks; bacteria in the urine are expected and not treated unless there are symptoms
    • Leaking around the catheter (bypassing) can result from bladder spasms or straining at stool, and can also be the first sign that the catheter is blocked; severe or persistent spasm, blockage or no drainage is reported promptly to the community nurse
    • Fever, rigors, loin pain, confusion or feeling generally unwell with a catheter in place suggest infection spreading to the kidneys or blood and need same-day assessment

    Recovery

    Slight stinging on first passing urine after removal is normal; a failed trial without catheter leads to re-catheterisation and urology review. People discharged with a catheter are taught bag changes, hygiene and what to do about leakage or blockage, and are given a date for removal or change. Adjusting to a long-term catheter takes time, and discomfort, urgency, bladder spasm and blood in the urine are commonly reported in the first weeks.

    Risks

    • Urinary tract infection, rising with each day in place, and catheter-related sepsis; most bacteria in the urine cause no symptoms, but a proportion progress to infection needing treatment
    • Urethral trauma, bleeding and false passage, especially in men with strictures or enlarged prostates, and after repeated insertion attempts
    • Blockage by debris or clots; bypassing; bladder spasm; pain and discomfort, and in men painful erections
    • Rarely, misplacement of the catheter or balloon in the urethra or a ureter, or injury to the bladder wall, more likely in people with reduced sensation from neurological disease
    • Long term: strictures, stones, bladder cancer risk after many years; pressure damage to the urethra and penis from a poorly secured catheter; effects on sexual function and self-image

    Alternatives

    • Bladder scan and watchful measures for mild retention
    • Alpha-blocker tablets before a trial without catheter in prostatic retention
    • Intermittent self-catheterisation or suprapubic catheter for long-term needs; sex is usually easier with these than with an indwelling urethral catheter
    • Sheath (condom) drainage or pads for incontinence

    Conditions

    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.
    • Pelvic organ prolapse Descent of one or more of the pelvic organs (the bladder, uterus, rectum, small bowel or, after hysterectomy.
    • Enlarged prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia) An enlarged prostate is a common condition in which the prostate gland grows larger and can squeeze the urethra, the outlet tube for urine.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Vaginoplasty Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
