    Gastroscopy

    Also known as: upper endoscopy, OGD, oesophago-gastro-duodenoscopy, EGD, upper GI endoscopy

    A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment, with throat spray or light sedation.

    Open in the 3D explorerOesophagus anatomy

    What it is

    A thin flexible camera is passed through the mouth down the oesophagus, into the stomach and the first part of the small intestine, giving a direct view of the lining and allowing biopsies and treatment. It takes 5-15 minutes with throat spray or light sedation. Before the test a nurse reviews the medical history and medicines, explains the risks and takes written consent. The choice between throat spray alone and sedation is the person's: with throat spray they are fully awake and can leave soon afterwards, while sedation makes the test more comfortable but requires monitoring of breathing, pulse and blood pressure and an escort home. Gagging as the scope passes the back of the throat is normal and does not interfere with breathing. Air put into the stomach to open up the view causes bloating and belching and is sucked out at the end. Biopsies are painless because the lining of the gut has no pain sensation to the forceps. The examination can miss a small lesion, particularly if food remains in the stomach, which is why fasting beforehand matters.

    Why it is done

    • Difficulty or pain on swallowing, persistent indigestion, especially over 55 or with weight loss
    • Vomiting blood or black stools (urgent, to find and treat the bleeding point)
    • Unexplained iron-deficiency anaemia
    • Diagnosing coeliac disease, H. pylori, Barrett oesophagus; surveillance of ulcers and Barrett
    • Treatment: stretching strictures, removing polyps, banding varices, placing feeding tubes and stents

    Before the procedure

    • Nothing to eat for 6 hours and clear fluids only until 2 hours before, so that the stomach is empty for a clear view and to lower the risk of inhaling stomach contents
    • If testing for H. pylori is planned, the unit may ask you to pause acid-suppressing tablets for about two weeks beforehand; do this only on the unit's instruction
    • Blood thinners managed; a driver if sedated. All medicines are declared, including aspirin, anticoagulants, diabetes and blood pressure tablets, iron and anti-inflammatories, because some affect bleeding risk or need their timing adjusted around fasting
    • Dentures, loose teeth, crowns and bridgework are noted because the scope and mouthguard can occasionally damage them

    Step by step

    1. Lying on the left side with a mouthguard; throat spray numbs the gullet, or sedation is given through a cannula in the arm or hand
    2. The scope is swallowed over the back of the tongue and advanced under direct vision; retching at this point is expected and passes
    3. Air inflates the stomach for a clear view; the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum are inspected in turn
    4. Biopsies are taken painlessly with forceps through the scope; polyps removed, bleeding treated with clips, injections or heat
    5. The air is sucked out, the scope is withdrawn, and the whole procedure is recorded; pulse, oxygen and blood pressure are monitored throughout if sedation has been given

    Afterwards

    • Sore throat and bloating for a day; mild stomach and throat discomfort passes within hours
    • Eat and drink once the throat spray has worn off (about an hour)
    • No driving, alcohol or important decisions for 24 hours after sedation; an adult escort is needed to go home
    • Biopsy results in one to two weeks, sometimes several weeks
    • Black, tarry stools, vomiting blood, severe or worsening abdominal or chest pain, or persistent bloating are reported to the emergency department because they may indicate bleeding or a perforation

    Recovery

    Normal activities the same day (next day after sedation). The immediate findings are usually explained before discharge; biopsy results decide treatment for coeliac disease, H. pylori, Barrett oesophagus or suspected cancer and are given at a follow-up appointment or by letter.

    Risks

    • Sore throat, bloating
    • Bleeding after biopsy or polyp removal (small), higher in people taking anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs
    • Perforation of the oesophagus or stomach (about 1 in 3000, higher with stretching); a tear may need admission or surgery, and the risk is higher after therapeutic procedures such as dilatation than after a diagnostic test
    • Reaction to sedation, which can slow breathing, lower blood pressure or disturb heart rhythm, more so in older people and those with other illnesses; chest infection from aspiration (rare)
    • Damage to teeth, crowns or bridgework (slight risk)
    • A small lesion may be missed, particularly if the stomach has not emptied

    Alternatives

    • Barium swallow or CT (no biopsies)
    • Capsule endoscopy for the small bowel
    • A trial of acid suppression in younger patients without alarm features
    • Non-invasive tests for H. pylori (breath or stool test) when no view of the lining is needed

    Conditions

    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.

    Medications

    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.

    Guides and news

    • Gastroscopy: what happens and what to expect A gastroscopy uses a thin camera to look at the gullet and stomach.
    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
