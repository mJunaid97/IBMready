    Joint aspiration and injection

    Also known as: arthrocentesis, joint tap, steroid injection, knee aspiration, synovial fluid analysis

    A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection; it is the definitive way to diagnose septic and crystal arthritis.

    Open in the 3D explorerKnee joint anatomy

    What it is

    A needle is passed into a swollen joint to draw off fluid for analysis and to relieve pressure, often followed by an injection of corticosteroid and local anaesthetic through the same needle to settle inflammation. The knee is the commonest site; the shoulder, wrist, elbow, ankle and hip (with ultrasound guidance) are also done. Analysis of the fluid is the definitive way to diagnose infection in a joint and crystal arthritis, and helps sort out other causes of arthritis. The appearance of the fluid is itself informative: increasing inflammation goes with more fluid, lower viscosity, greater cloudiness and a higher cell count dominated by neutrophils, while frank blood points to injury or a bleeding disorder. Strict sterile technique protects the joint from infection and also keeps the sample uncontaminated so the laboratory results can be trusted. Ultrasound guidance improves the chance of obtaining fluid and reduces complications, particularly in small or deep joints. The only absolute reason not to aspirate is infected skin over the entry point; a suspected septic joint is aspirated even in someone who is unwell, because the fluid result changes management.

    Why it is done

    • A hot, swollen joint where septic arthritis must be ruled out (the fluid goes for urgent microscopy and culture); Gram stain is prioritised if only a little fluid is obtained, and culture confirms the diagnosis because the stain misses many early infections
    • Diagnosing gout and pseudogout by finding crystals under polarised light
    • Relieving a tense, painful effusion after injury or in osteoarthritis, which also restores movement
    • Joint pain or swelling of unknown cause, where the fluid cell count and composition separate inflammatory from non-inflammatory arthritis
    • Steroid injection for osteoarthritis flares, rheumatoid arthritis, frozen shoulder, bursitis and tendon sheath inflammation

    Before the procedure

    • Explain and consent; check for anticoagulants (not a contraindication for most joints), skin infection over the site, and allergy. Infected skin (cellulitis) over the planned entry point is the main contraindication to needle entry; bacteria in the bloodstream is a relative concern because the needle could seed the joint, and a corticosteroid is not injected if a joint infection is suspected
    • Position the joint: the knee slightly bent over a roll; the landmark chosen beside the patella
    • Skin cleaned with antiseptic; sterile gloves; local anaesthetic to the skin optional; ultrasound used for deep joints or when landmarks are uncertain

    Step by step

    1. A needle (18-21 gauge) on a syringe is advanced through the skin into the joint space using landmarks or ultrasound, with a giving-way as it enters
    2. Fluid is aspirated: its colour and clarity noted (clear yellow, cloudy, bloody, pus)
    3. Samples are sent for cell count, Gram stain and culture, and crystal examination; glucose, protein and lactate may be measured as supporting evidence of infection
    4. If indicated, the syringe is swapped and steroid (for example 40 mg methylprednisolone or triamcinolone) with lidocaine is injected
    5. The needle is withdrawn and a dressing applied; the fluid volume is recorded

    Afterwards

    • Rest the joint for 24-48 hours after a steroid injection; ice for discomfort; simple painkillers may be needed for the first day or two
    • Watch for a flare of pain in the first day (crystal reaction to the steroid) and for redness, fever or increasing pain suggesting infection; a marked increase in pain with swelling, restricted movement, fever and feeling unwell needs same-day assessment
    • Diabetics: blood glucose may rise for a few days
    • Results: Gram stain and cell count are available within hours and guide whether antibiotics are started; culture takes days; if the fluid is inconclusive a synovial biopsy may be considered

    Recovery

    Relief from an aspiration is immediate; steroid benefit builds over a few days and lasts weeks to months. The injection acts quickly and the effect can last for months, but it treats inflammation rather than the underlying cause, so the diagnosis still needs to be established. Repeated injections into the same joint are limited because of concern about cartilage and surrounding tissue.

    Risks

    • Introducing infection into the joint (about 1 in 10,000 with sterile technique)
    • Bleeding into the joint, especially on anticoagulants
    • Post-injection flare, skin thinning or pale patch at the site, facial flushing; thinning of the fat under the skin can leave a small dimple months later
    • Steroid deposits visible on later X-rays, which are harmless
    • Cartilage damage with repeated steroid injections (limit to 3-4 a year)

    Alternatives

    • Oral anti-inflammatories and rest
    • Physiotherapy and bracing
    • Ultrasound-guided injection for deep or difficult joints
    • Arthroscopy for mechanical problems, or synovial biopsy when fluid analysis is inconclusive

    Conditions

    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • ACL injury A sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, the band inside the knee that stops the tibia sliding forward on the femur and controls rotation.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Symptoms

    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Knee replacement The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when…

    Medications

    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
