    Coronary artery bypass grafting

    Also known as: CABG, bypass surgery, heart bypass, open heart surgery

    Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery, usually on a heart-lung machine.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    What it is

    Open-heart surgery that routes blood around blocked coronary arteries using the patient's own vessels: the internal mammary artery from inside the chest wall is joined beyond the blockage in the left anterior descending artery, and lengths of leg vein or radial artery bridge from the aorta to the other vessels. Usually done on a heart-lung machine with the heart stopped. The operation takes several hours under general anaesthetic. It is usually performed through a full-length incision in the breastbone; in selected cases it can be done through smaller cuts with a camera, which is quicker to recover from but not suitable for everyone. Bypass relieves angina and breathlessness and lowers the risk of a heart attack in people with extensive disease, but it does not cure coronary artery disease: new blockages can form in the native arteries and in the grafts, so medicines and lifestyle changes continue for life.

    Why it is done

    • Left main stem or three-vessel coronary disease, particularly with diabetes or a weak ventricle, where surgery improves survival compared with stents
    • Angina not controlled by medication where anatomy is unsuitable for stents
    • Failed or complicated angioplasty
    • Combined with valve surgery when both are needed

    Before the procedure

    • Coronary angiography and echocardiogram; carotid ultrasound; lung function, bloods, dental review. The risk of the operation is estimated from age, sex, diabetes, lung and kidney function and smoking, and discussed before consent
    • The surgical team says whether and when to pause clopidogrel before the operation (often several days) and aspirin is usually continued; neither is stopped without their instruction
    • General anaesthetic; arterial and central venous lines; urinary catheter

    Step by step

    1. The sternum is split lengthways and the internal mammary artery harvested from the chest wall while the vein is taken from the leg (or the radial artery from the arm)
    2. Heparin is given and the heart-lung machine takes over circulation; the aorta is clamped and the heart arrested with cold potassium solution
    3. Each graft is sewn with fine sutures to the coronary artery beyond its blockage; vein grafts are then joined to the aorta
    4. The heart is restarted, the machine weaned off, and temporary pacing wires and chest drains placed
    5. The sternum is closed with steel wires and the skin closed; the operation usually takes three to six hours

    Afterwards

    • Intensive care overnight on a ventilator for a few hours, then the ward after a day or two; 5-8 days in hospital in total
    • Chest drains out in a day or two; pain from the sternum and leg wound; the heart rhythm is monitored continuously and temporary pacing used if needed
    • Aspirin, statin, beta blocker; medicines to prevent clots and irregular rhythm in the early days; cardiac rehabilitation from about six weeks
    • Tiredness, poor sleep and appetite, constipation, itching of the healing wounds and low mood or poor concentration are common in the first weeks and usually improve
    • Follow-up at about six to eight weeks, with an ECG or echocardiogram to check how the heart is working

    Recovery

    Six to twelve weeks off work, no driving for four weeks, no heavy lifting for three months while the sternum knits; full benefit at about three months. Arterial grafts last decades; vein grafts about half are narrowed by ten years. Most people return to ordinary activities, including driving, work and sex, at about six weeks, later for heavy manual work. Recovery is quicker after minimally invasive bypass. Cardiac rehabilitation provides supervised exercise, education and psychological support, and most people notice a marked improvement in chest pain and breathlessness once they have recovered.

    Risks

    • Death (around 1-2% electively, higher in emergencies and with other illness, heart failure, lung or kidney disease)
    • Stroke (1-2%), heart attack, kidney injury (usually temporary, recovering over days or weeks)
    • Atrial fibrillation in a third (usually temporary and treated with a short course of medicine)
    • Wound infection, especially of the sternum in diabetes and obesity
    • Bleeding needing a return to theatre; memory and concentration changes for some months, which usually improve but can occasionally be permanent
    • Recurrence: the operation does not stop coronary disease, and new narrowings can develop in native arteries or grafts over the years

    Alternatives

    • Angioplasty and stents for suitable anatomy; symptoms are more likely to return than after bypass
    • Optimal medical therapy
    • Off-pump and minimally invasive bypass in selected cases

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Warfarin The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.
