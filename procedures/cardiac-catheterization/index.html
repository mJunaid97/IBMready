    Cardiac catheterisation

    Also known as: cardiac catheterization, coronary angiogram, heart catheterisation, right heart catheter, electrophysiology study, ablation

    Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and, in electrophysiology studies, map and ablate the source of an arrhythmia.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    What it is

    Thin tubes are passed through an artery or vein to the heart to measure pressures and oxygen levels in its chambers, inject contrast into the coronary arteries and chambers, and, in electrophysiology studies, map the electrical circuits and burn or freeze the tissue causing an arrhythmia (ablation). The catheter is guided to the heart under X-ray screening from a blood vessel in the wrist, arm, groin or neck. Coronary angiography, the commonest form, shows whether and where the coronary arteries are narrowed or blocked, and if a suitable blockage is found angioplasty and stenting can be done in the same sitting. The person is awake, with local anaesthetic at the puncture site and a sedative if wanted, and can follow instructions during the test. It carries a slightly higher risk than non-invasive heart tests but is very safe in experienced hands.

    Why it is done

    • Mapping coronary artery narrowing in angina or after a heart attack (before angioplasty or bypass)
    • Assessing valve disease and heart failure by measuring pressures, including pulmonary hypertension, through right heart catheterisation
    • Diagnosing and ablating arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, SVT and atrial flutter
    • Closing holes in the heart, replacing valves through a catheter (TAVI), biopsy of heart muscle

    Before the procedure

    • Blood tests including kidney function and clotting; ECG; sometimes echo or CT. A stress test or heart imaging may be done first to see how well the heart is working and to plan the procedure
    • Fasting 4-6 hours; metformin and blood thinners managed. Allergies, especially any previous reaction to contrast dye or iodine, and any possibility of pregnancy are declared because X-rays and iodine contrast are used
    • The procedure, its risks and alternatives are explained and written consent is taken
    • Local anaesthetic to the wrist or groin, light sedation; awake throughout

    Step by step

    1. A sheath is placed in the radial artery (coronaries), femoral artery (left heart) or femoral vein (right heart, electrophysiology)
    2. Catheters are advanced under X-ray screening to the coronary origins or heart chambers; some pressure or discomfort may be felt at the puncture site but not along the vessels
    3. Contrast injections film the arteries from several angles as the X-ray machine moves around; a brief warm flush lasting a minute or two is normal as the dye goes in. Pressures and oxygen levels are recorded through the catheter
    4. In electrophysiology, several electrode catheters map the rhythm; pacing provokes the arrhythmia and the culprit tissue is ablated
    5. Sheaths are removed and the site compressed or closed with a plug; a pressure device or band is applied, and after wrist access the person can usually walk around straight away

    Afterwards

    • Lie flat for 2-6 hours after femoral access; a wrist band for radial. Lying flat prevents bleeding from the groin artery and can cause some mild back discomfort
    • Home the same day for diagnostic studies; drink fluids to clear contrast. An overnight stay may be needed after treatment procedures or where other health problems make monitoring sensible
    • Results explained immediately with a plan for medication, stents or surgery
    • A bruise at the puncture site may last a few weeks; a small lump under the skin (haematoma) usually settles. Bleeding that does not stop, a cold or painful limb, chest pain or a fever are reported urgently

    Recovery

    Normal activities within a day or two; avoid heavy lifting for a week. Ablation for atrial fibrillation is followed by anticoagulation for at least two months. Soreness and bruising at the wrist or groin are the commonest after-effects and fade over days to weeks. The team advises when regular medicines, driving and work can resume, which depends on the access site and on whether a stent or ablation was performed.

    Risks

    • Bruising, bleeding or a false aneurysm at the puncture site; rarely damage to the artery in the arm or groin that affects the blood supply to the limb
    • Contrast kidney injury or allergy; the dye can reduce kidney function, particularly in people with diabetes or existing kidney disease, so kidney function is checked beforehand and fluids encouraged afterwards
    • For diagnostic angiography, stroke, heart attack, arrhythmia and perforation of the heart are rare (well under 1%); ablation and electrophysiology procedures carry their own risks, which the electrophysiology team explains; low blood pressure, blood clots, infection and, with neck access, a collapsed lung are also rare
    • For ablation: damage to the heart's normal conduction needing a pacemaker (rare)
    • A small radiation dose, which matters most for children and for people who need repeated procedures

    Alternatives

    • CT coronary angiography for stable chest pain (no catheter, but no treatment possible)
    • Echocardiography and cardiac MRI for structure and function
    • Stress testing to look for ischaemia without imaging the arteries directly
    • Medication for arrhythmias instead of ablation

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Ambulatory ECG monitoring A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during…
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.

    Guides and news
