    Appendicectomy

    Also known as: appendectomy, appendix removal, laparoscopic appendicectomy

    Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.

    Open in the 3D explorerLarge intestine anatomy

    What it is

    Removal of the inflamed appendix, the blind-ended tube hanging off the caecum at the start of the large intestine. Usually done through three small keyhole incisions with a camera (laparoscopically), occasionally through a single cut in the right lower abdomen. The appendix usually needs to come out as soon as appendicitis is diagnosed, because an inflamed appendix can burst and spread infection through the lining of the abdomen (peritonitis), which can be life-threatening. Keyhole surgery is the usual first choice; if the surgeon meets difficulties or judges it safer, the operation is converted to an open approach. With enhanced-recovery care many people with uncomplicated appendicitis go home within a day.

    Why it is done

    • Acute appendicitis, to prevent perforation and peritonitis; surgeons usually recommend removal even when antibiotics have settled an episode, because a proportion of people treated with antibiotics alone have another attack
    • An appendix mass or abscess after initial antibiotics and drainage, with the operation deferred until the inflammation has settled
    • Occasionally, an appendix tumour found on imaging or during other surgery

    Before the procedure

    • Blood tests, pregnancy test in women, sometimes ultrasound or CT. The diagnosis is mainly clinical; blood tests show inflammation and imaging is used when the picture is unclear
    • Nil by mouth; intravenous fluids and antibiotics
    • If an abscess has already formed, surgery is often postponed: antibiotics are given and the abscess may be drained through a small cut in the skin, with the appendix removed later
    • Consent and marking; general anaesthetic. Previous abdominal surgery is noted because scar tissue raises the chance of injury when the keyhole instruments are inserted

    Step by step

    1. Carbon dioxide inflates the abdomen through a port at the navel to create working space
    2. A camera and two instruments are introduced through 5-12 mm ports
    3. The appendix is lifted, its blood supply in the mesoappendix sealed, and its base tied or stapled at the caecum
    4. The appendix is removed in a bag; any pus is washed out
    5. If inflammation, scarring or bleeding makes the keyhole view unsafe, the surgeon converts to an open incision in the right lower abdomen
    6. Ports are removed and the small wounds closed with dissolvable stitches

    Afterwards

    • Home the same or next day for uncomplicated cases; a few days in hospital after open surgery, a perforated appendix or complications
    • Simple painkillers; walk early. Getting out of bed the day after surgery lowers the risk of blood clots in the legs and eases muscle soreness
    • Pain and bruising around the wounds for the first few days, and shoulder-tip pain from the gas used to inflate the abdomen, are expected; constipation or diarrhoea for a few days is common
    • Antibiotics continued for a few days if the appendix had perforated

    Recovery

    Back to desk work in about a week and to sport in two to four weeks after keyhole surgery; longer after open surgery or a perforated appendix. Most people recover from keyhole surgery within a couple of weeks, although studies of convalescence suggest full recovery often takes around two weeks on average and is slower when there were complications. After open surgery strenuous activity is avoided for several weeks.

    Risks

    • Wound infection (more after perforation)
    • Pelvic abscess, or an abscess where the appendix was removed
    • Bleeding, injury to bowel, bladder or blood vessels during instrument insertion (uncommon; more likely after previous abdominal surgery)
    • Stump leak, adhesions (scar tissue inside the abdomen, which can rarely cause a bowel blockage years later), hernia at the port site (rare)
    • Anaesthetic risks
    • All risks are higher when the appendix has already burst; peritonitis from a burst appendix can be life-threatening and needs urgent surgery and antibiotics

    Alternatives

    • Antibiotics alone for uncomplicated appendicitis: a course of tablets at home, or intravenous antibiotics in hospital for severe cases. It works in most people in the short term, but a proportion have another episode and about a third need surgery within a few years, so surgeons usually still recommend removal
    • Drainage and delayed surgery for an abscess

    Conditions

    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.

    Guides and news

    • General anaesthetic: before, during, after A general anaesthetic puts a person into controlled unconsciousness for surgery.
