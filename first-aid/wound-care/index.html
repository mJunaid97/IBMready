    Cuts, grazes and wound care

    Also known as: cuts, grazes, abrasion, laceration, puncture wound, animal bite, stitches, tetanus

    Most cuts and grazes heal on their own if they are cleaned, closed and kept covered. The aims are to stop bleeding, remove dirt and bacteria, bring the edges together so new skin can bridge the gap, and spot the wounds that need stitches, antibiotics or a tetanus booster.

    Get medical help when

    • Bleeding that does not stop after 10 minutes of pressure, or spurting blood
    • A wound that gapes, is longer than about 2 cm, is on the face, over a joint or on the hand with loss of movement or sensation (may need stitches within 12-24 hours)
    • Animal or human bites, deep puncture wounds, wounds contaminated with soil or glass, or something embedded
    • Signs of infection, or a tetanus vaccination history that is unknown or incomplete, especially with a deep, dirty or contaminated wound or a bite

    Recognise it

    • Graze: surface skin scraped off, oozing, dirty
    • Cut (laceration or incision): edges may gape; bleeding depends on depth and site (scalp and face bleed freely)
    • Puncture: small entry, deep track, high infection risk (nails, bites, splinters)
    • Infection developing days later: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus, increasing pain, fever, red streaks up the limb

    What to do

    1. Wash your hands and stop the bleedingApply pressure with a clean cloth for 10 minutes; raise the part above the heart.
    2. Clean the woundRinse under running tap water for several minutes to flush out dirt. Pat dry with a clean towel. Remove loose grit with clean tweezers; leave anything deeply embedded for a clinician.
    3. Close and coverBring the edges of a small cut together with adhesive strips. Cover with a sterile non-stick dressing or plaster; change it daily or if wet or dirty. Keep it covered until healed.
    4. Pain reliefParacetamol or ibuprofen if needed.
    5. Watch for infectionCheck daily. Seek care for spreading redness, pus, increasing pain or fever.

    Do not

    • Do not use antiseptic solutions, hydrogen peroxide or iodine inside a wound: they damage healing tissue; tap water is best
    • Do not pull out deeply embedded objects
    • Do not close dirty wounds, bites or wounds more than 12-24 hours old with strips or glue: they need cleaning and often antibiotics
    • Do not pick scabs

    Get medical help when

    • Bleeding that does not stop after 10 minutes of pressure, or spurting blood
    • A wound that gapes, is longer than about 2 cm, is on the face, over a joint or on the hand with loss of movement or sensation (may need stitches within 12-24 hours)
    • Animal or human bites, deep puncture wounds, wounds contaminated with soil or glass, or something embedded
    • Signs of infection, or a tetanus vaccination history that is unknown or incomplete, especially with a deep, dirty or contaminated wound or a bite

    Why it works: the anatomy

    The skin's epidermis regrows from the edges and from hair follicles at about a millimetre a day; the dermis beneath rebuilds with collagen, which is why deep cuts scar. Keeping a wound moist and covered speeds cell migration, and closing the edges shortens the distance new skin must cross. Bacteria in soil and animal mouths (Clostridium tetani, Pasteurella) thrive in deep, oxygen-poor puncture tracks, which is why those wounds are cleaned open and given antibiotics rather than sealed.

    Conditions

    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.

    Medical tests

    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.

    Guides and news

    • Burns and scalds: first aid and when to get help Cooling a burn under running water for 20 minutes is the first step; the NHS says large, deep, facial, chemical or electrical burns need A&E or 999.
