    Severe bleeding

    Also known as: haemorrhage, major bleeding, tourniquet, pressure dressing, shock

    An adult has about 5 litres of blood and can lose a fifth before the body's compensation fails; a severed artery can do that in minutes. Direct pressure on the wound is the single most effective measure, with a tourniquet for limb bleeding that pressure cannot control.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Emergency

    Call emergency services (999 UK · 112 Europe · 911 North America) as soon as you recognise this, or have someone call while you act.

    Recognise it

    • Blood soaking through clothing or dressings, pooling, or spurting (arterial)
    • Signs of shock as blood is lost: pale, cold, clammy skin, fast breathing, rapid weak pulse, thirst, confusion, then collapse
    • Amputations and deep wounds of the neck, armpit or groin bleed catastrophically

    What to do

    1. Protect yourself and callWear gloves if available. Call emergency services or have someone call while you act.
    2. Apply firm direct pressurePress hard on the wound with a pad, clean cloth or your hands, directly over the bleeding point. Do not lift it to look. If blood soaks through, add more on top and press harder.
    3. Pack deep woundsFor a deep wound in the neck, armpit or groin where a tourniquet cannot go, pack gauze or clean cloth tightly into the wound and keep pressing.
    4. Apply a tourniquet if a limb keeps bleedingIf direct pressure fails for a limb, or the bleeding is catastrophic, place a tourniquet (commercial, or improvised from a wide band and a stick to twist) 5-7 cm above the wound, not over a joint, and tighten until bleeding stops. Note the time. Do not loosen it.
    5. Treat for shockLie them down, raise the legs if injuries allow, keep them warm, and do not give food or drink. Keep pressing on the wound until help arrives.
    6. If they become unresponsiveCheck breathing; start CPR if not breathing normally.

    Do not

    • Do not remove a large embedded object: press around it instead
    • Do not release pressure to check or change dressings
    • Do not apply a tourniquet loosely or over a joint, and never remove one before medical care
    • Do not give anything to drink

    Get medical help when

    • Any bleeding that soaks through a dressing, spurts, or comes from a deep or large wound
    • Any wound with signs of shock, or in someone on blood thinners

    Why it works: the anatomy

    Pressure closes the vessel against underlying tissue and lets a clot form; arteries have muscular walls that contract but keep pumping at systolic pressure, so only sustained force stops them. A tourniquet compresses the artery against bone: the femoral and brachial arteries lie close to the femur and humerus, which is why limb tourniquets work. Shock is the failure to deliver oxygen once blood volume falls; the body diverts blood from skin (pale, cold) and speeds the heart to compensate.
