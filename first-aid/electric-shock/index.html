    Electric shock

    Also known as: electrocution, electrical injury, lightning strike

    Electricity passing through the body can stop the heart, paralyse breathing muscles, burn tissue along its path far more deeply than the skin marks suggest, and throw the person against objects. The first rule is not to become the second casualty: break contact safely before touching them.

    Emergency

    Call emergency services (999 UK · 112 Europe · 911 North America) as soon as you recognise this, or have someone call while you act.

    Recognise it

    • Person in contact with a live source, unable to let go, or found collapsed near one
    • Burns at the entry and exit points (often hand and foot), which may look small
    • Unconsciousness, absent or abnormal breathing, irregular pulse
    • Muscle pain, numbness, confusion, seizures; injuries from falls or being thrown
    • Lightning: linear or feathery burns, ruptured eardrums, temporary paralysis
    • After the contact is broken, signs of shock: pale, cold, clammy skin, sweating, and fast shallow breathing
    • Signs that an unresponsive person is recovering: coughing, opening the eyes, speaking, or starting to breathe normally

    What to do

    1. Make it safeThe rescuer's own safety comes first: do not touch the person while they are still in contact with the electrical source, because the current would pass to the rescuer as well. Low voltage (household): switch off at the mains or unplug, which breaks the contact between the electricity and the casualty. If the supply cannot be switched off, stand on dry insulating material such as a plastic mat or wooden box and use a broom handle or wooden pole to push the person's limb away from the source; do not touch them directly. If a wooden object will not work, St John Ambulance describes looping a rope under the casualty's arms or ankles to pull them clear. High voltage (power lines, railways, substations): stay at least 18 metres away and call emergency services; do not approach until the supply is confirmed off.
    2. Call emergency servicesOnce the contact is broken, call 999 or 112 (UK) for any shock beyond a minor tingle, and always for loss of consciousness, burns, or a high-voltage or lightning injury. If someone else is present, ask them to call and to fetch a defibrillator (AED) while the first aider checks the casualty.
    3. Check response and breathingCheck for a response and for normal breathing. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR at once and send a helper for a defibrillator; electrical cardiac arrests respond well to defibrillation, and an untrained bystander can use an AED by following its voice prompts. If they show signs of becoming responsive, such as coughing, opening their eyes or speaking, and start to breathe normally, place them in the recovery position and keep checking their breathing until help arrives.
    4. Treat burnsCool entry and exit burns with running water for 20 minutes and cover loosely. Look for both points: current enters at one site and leaves at another, and both marks can be small even when deep tissue along the path has been injured.
    5. Treat other injuriesSuspect spinal injury if thrown; keep them still. Treat for shock: help them lie down and raise their legs, supporting them on a chair, cover them with a coat or blanket to keep them warm, keep them calm, and give nothing by mouth. Keep monitoring breathing and responsiveness while waiting for the ambulance.

    Do not

    • Do not touch someone still in contact with electricity
    • Do not use anything wet or metal to move them
    • Do not approach high-voltage sources or a downed cable
    • Do not assume a small skin burn means a small injury
    • Do not go near or touch the person until the electrical supply has definitely been switched off or they have been pushed clear with a dry, non-conducting object

    Get medical help when

    • Any electric shock with unconsciousness, burns, chest pain, palpitations, breathing difficulty, or in pregnancy
    • All high-voltage and lightning injuries
    • Any shock in a child, or any household shock that caused more than a brief tingle (heart rhythm monitoring may be needed)

    Why it works: the anatomy

    Current follows the path of least resistance through wet, conductive tissues: blood vessels, nerves and muscle. Alternating current at mains frequency causes muscles to contract and hold on, and if the path crosses the chest it can throw the heart into ventricular fibrillation or stop the diaphragm. Heat generated along the path cooks deep tissue, and muscle breakdown can clog the kidneys. Lightning is a direct-current flashover that more often causes cardiac and respiratory arrest than deep burns.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Symptoms

    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.

    Guides and news

    • Burns and scalds: first aid and when to get help Cooling a burn under running water for 20 minutes is the first step; the NHS says large, deep, facial, chemical or electrical burns need A&E or 999.
