    Stroke: recognise and act FAST

    Also known as: FAST test, stroke first aid, mini-stroke, TIA

    A blood vessel in the brain has blocked or burst. Around two million neurons die every minute the blockage persists, but clot-busting drugs and clot retrieval can reverse the damage if given within hours. There is nothing to do at the scene except recognise it and get an ambulance.

    Emergency

    Call emergency services (999 UK · 112 Europe · 911 North America) as soon as you recognise this, or have someone call while you act.

    Recognise it

    • Face: has it drooped on one side? Can they smile? Has their mouth or eye drooped?
    • Arms: can they raise both arms fully and keep them there? Someone having a stroke may manage only one
    • Speech: is it slurred, garbled or absent? Can they say a simple sentence, or answer a simple question such as their name, and understand you?
    • Time: call emergency services at once if any one of these signs is present; the FAST test picks up the three most common signs of stroke but not every stroke
    • Also: sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body including the leg, hand or foot; difficulty finding words or speaking in clear sentences; sudden blurred vision or loss of sight in one or both eyes; sudden memory loss or confusion; dizziness or a sudden fall; a sudden, severe headache
    • A transient ischaemic attack (TIA, 'mini-stroke') has the same signs, which then go away; it is treated as the same emergency

    What to do

    1. Call emergency servicesCall 999 or 112 (UK) and say the word 'stroke', giving the time the symptoms started (or when they were last seen well). Any one FAST sign is enough to call: do not wait for all three, and do not wait to see whether the symptoms pass. The time decides which treatments are possible, and prompt action gives the best chance of survival and recovery.
    2. Keep them safe and comfortableHelp them lie down with head and shoulders slightly raised, supported with cushions or pillows, or on their side if drowsy or vomiting. Loosen tight clothing and keep them comfortable and supported while waiting for the ambulance.
    3. Give nothing to eat or drinkSwallowing is often impaired after a stroke and fluid can enter the lungs; they may also need surgery or anaesthesia. This includes their usual tablets.
    4. Reassure and monitorTalk calmly and reassure them even if they cannot reply; they may understand more than they can express. Keep checking their level of response, breathing and any change in the FAST signs, and note the times. If they stop responding, treat them as an unresponsive casualty: open the airway, check breathing, and start CPR if they are not breathing normally.

    Do not

    • Do not give aspirin: a bleeding stroke looks identical and aspirin would worsen it
    • Do not let them 'sleep it off' or wait for symptoms to pass, even if they improve (a transient ischaemic attack is a warning of an imminent stroke)
    • Do not give food, drink or their usual tablets
    • Do not wait for all three FAST signs: any one of them means it is time to call 999

    Get medical help when

    • Any FAST sign, however brief, even if it has resolved
    • Any of the other sudden signs: one-sided weakness or numbness, loss of vision, severe headache, confusion, dizziness or a sudden fall
    • A suspected TIA (mini-stroke) whose symptoms have already gone: it is a medical emergency the same as a stroke

    Why it works: the anatomy

    The middle cerebral artery supplies the face, arm and speech areas of the cortex, which is why FAST catches most strokes. Neurons deprived of oxygenated blood stop firing in seconds and die in minutes, but the surrounding 'penumbra' survives for hours on collateral flow and is what treatment saves. A thrombectomy retrieves the clot through a catheter from the femoral artery up to the brain.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.

    Medications

    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.

    Guides and news

    • Which NHS service to use: 999, 111, GP, A&E The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies and NHS 111 for urgent problems or when unsure, with pharmacies.
