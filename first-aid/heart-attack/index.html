    Heart attack

    Also known as: myocardial infarction first aid, chest pain first aid

    A coronary artery has blocked and heart muscle is dying. Survival and the amount of muscle saved depend on how fast the artery is reopened, so the first-aid job is recognition and a fast call, keeping the person still and calm, and being ready to start CPR.

    Emergency

    Call emergency services (999 UK · 112 Europe · 911 North America) as soon as you recognise this, or have someone call while you act.

    Recognise it

    • Central chest pain or pressure, like a tight band or heavy weight, that does not ease with rest
    • Pain spreading to the arms (usually left), jaw, neck or back
    • Sweating, grey or pale skin, nausea, breathlessness, light-headedness
    • Women, older people and people with diabetes more often have breathlessness, indigestion-like discomfort or extreme tiredness without classic pain

    What to do

    1. Call emergency services immediatelySay you suspect a heart attack. Do not drive them to hospital yourself unless there is no ambulance service: an ambulance brings a defibrillator and can start treatment and alert the catheter lab.
    2. Sit them down and keep them stillA half-sitting position with knees bent and back supported (the 'W' position) eases the heart's work. Loosen tight clothing. Reassure them.
    3. Give aspirin if available and not allergicOne 300 mg tablet chewed slowly (or 4 x 75 mg). It begins to stop platelets clumping in the blocked artery. Do not give it if they are allergic or have been told not to take aspirin.
    4. Help with their own GTN sprayIf they have angina medication, help them take it as prescribed.
    5. Watch and be readyStay with them. If they become unresponsive and stop breathing normally, start CPR and send for a defibrillator.

    Do not

    • Do not let them walk about, eat, or 'see if it passes'
    • Do not give aspirin to someone with a known allergy or bleeding problem, or to children
    • Do not leave them alone

    Get medical help when

    • Call 999 straight away for chest pain or discomfort that does not ease with rest, or that comes with breathlessness, sweating, collapse or a known heart condition; do not wait to see if it passes

    Why it works: the anatomy

    The coronary arteries branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve and run across the heart's surface. A ruptured plaque in one of them triggers a clot that blocks flow; muscle downstream is ischaemic within seconds and starts dying within 20-30 minutes. Chewed aspirin reaches platelets within minutes; angioplasty within 90-120 minutes of the call saves the most muscle.

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.

    Guides and news

    • Which NHS service to use: 999, 111, GP, A&E The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies and NHS 111 for urgent problems or when unsure, with pharmacies.
