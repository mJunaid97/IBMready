    Heat exhaustion and heatstroke

    Also known as: heat illness, hyperthermia, sunstroke, heat cramps

    Heat exhaustion is the body struggling to shed heat: sweaty, weak and dizzy, but still thinking clearly, and it recovers with cooling and fluids within 30 minutes. Heatstroke is the failure of that system: core temperature above 40°C with confusion or collapse, which damages the brain, kidneys and muscle and kills without rapid cooling.

    Emergency

    Call emergency services (999 UK · 112 Europe · 911 North America) as soon as you recognise this, or have someone call while you act.

    Recognise it

    • Heat exhaustion: headache, dizziness, nausea and loss of appetite, heavy sweating, pale clammy skin, cramps in the arms, legs and stomach, fast weakening pulse and rapid breathing, temperature 38-40°C, thirst, weakness
    • Heatstroke: temperature over 40°C, hot skin (often dry, but may be sweaty), confusion, unusual behaviour, slurred speech, agitation, seizures, decreased level of consciousness or collapse; a full, bounding pulse
    • The key difference: in heat exhaustion the person is still thinking clearly and sweating; in heatstroke the level of consciousness falls and the skin is hot
    • At risk: babies, older people, athletes and outdoor workers, heart disease, some medicines (diuretics, antipsychotics), alcohol

    What to do

    1. Move to a cool placeShade, air conditioning or a cool room, out of the sun. Lie them down, raise the feet slightly, remove excess clothing. If heatstroke is suspected from the outset (very high temperature with confusion or reduced consciousness), call 999 or 112 first, then move them into the shade and remove outer clothing.
    2. Cool activelySpray or sponge with cool water and fan them, or wrap them in a cool, wet sheet; place cold packs wrapped in cloth to the armpits, neck and groin. For a collapsed athlete, immersion in cold water to the neck is the fastest method if it can be done safely. Cooling the skin with water and fanning lowers body temperature far faster than drinking does, so in suspected heatstroke cooling takes priority over fluids. If they start to overheat again, repeat the cooling.
    3. Give fluids if fully alertCool water, sipped steadily; an isotonic sports drink or oral rehydration solution helps replace the salt and fluid lost through sweating. Do not add table salt to a drink, which makes the condition worse. Nothing by mouth if confused or drowsy, because swallowing is unsafe.
    4. Reassess after 30 minutesHeat exhaustion should be improving: keep checking breathing, pulse and level of response. If they are still unwell after 30 minutes in a cool place, have a high temperature, cannot keep fluids down, become confused, have a seizure, or stop responding, treat it as heatstroke: call emergency services and continue cooling until temperature is below 39°C. Even someone who recovers from heat exhaustion should be seen by a medical professional.
    5. If unresponsiveRecovery position if breathing, continuing to cool them and to monitor breathing and responsiveness; CPR if not breathing normally. Be ready to protect them from injury if a seizure occurs.

    Do not

    • Do not give paracetamol or aspirin: they do not lower heatstroke temperatures and can harm the liver and kidneys
    • Do not give alcohol or caffeine
    • Do not leave anyone in a parked car
    • Do not wait to see if a confused person improves
    • Do not add table salt to drinks given for heat exhaustion
    • Do not rely on drinks alone in suspected heatstroke: cooling the skin with water and fanning, and calling for emergency help, come first

    Get medical help when

    • Any confusion, unusual behaviour, seizure, loss of consciousness or temperature over 40°C
    • No improvement after 30 minutes of cooling and rest, or inability to keep fluids down
    • Heat illness in a baby, young child or frail older adult
    • After any episode of heat exhaustion, even one that has recovered, so that a medical professional can check them

    Why it works: the anatomy

    Muscles and metabolism generate heat that blood carries to the skin, where sweat evaporates to shed it; the hypothalamus runs this thermostat. High humidity stops evaporation, dehydration reduces sweat, and once core temperature passes about 40°C proteins begin to denature: brain cells swell, muscle breaks down (releasing myoglobin that clogs the kidneys), and clotting fails. Cooling the large vessels of the neck, armpits and groin, and evaporative cooling with water and fanning, drop core temperature fastest.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.

    Guides and news
