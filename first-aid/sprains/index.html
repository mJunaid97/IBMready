    Sprains and strains

    Also known as: sprained ankle, pulled muscle, RICE, PRICE, soft tissue injury

    A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament (the bands that hold a joint together); a strain is a stretched or torn muscle or tendon. Most heal with protection, early gentle movement and time. The first days aim to limit swelling and pain, the following weeks to restore movement and strength.

    Get medical help when

    • Go to A&E if the joint looks deformed, you heard a crack, cannot bear weight at all, or numbness or pale cold skin develops
    • Seek medical advice if the pain is severe or the person cannot move the injured part
    • See a clinician if it is no better after a week, or the joint gives way repeatedly

    Recognise it

    • Pain, swelling and bruising around a joint (sprain) or in a muscle belly (strain) after a twist, fall or sudden effort; sports activities are the usual setting
    • Difficulty putting weight through the joint or using the muscle
    • A 'pop' at the time of injury with rapid swelling suggests a more serious ligament tear
    • Suspect a fracture instead if there is bony tenderness, deformity, or inability to take four steps
    • Severe pain or being unable to move the injured part at all means medical advice is needed rather than home care

    What to do

    1. ProtectStop the activity. Help the person sit or lie down and support the injured area in a comfortable position, raised if possible, padding it with blankets, cushions or similar soft material. Use crutches or a brace for a day or two if weight-bearing is painful.
    2. Rest, then load graduallyRest for the first 24-72 hours, then begin gentle movement and progressive weight-bearing: complete rest slows recovery. If the pain is severe or the person cannot move the injured part, seek medical advice before trying to use it.
    3. IceA cold pack, or a bag of frozen vegetables wrapped in a towel, applied for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours during the first 2-3 days. Cooling eases swelling, pain and bruising, but a pack should stay on for no more than 20 minutes at a time.
    4. CompressionAn elastic bandage snug but not tight, removed at night, to limit swelling. For an elbow, knee or wrist, St John Ambulance describes bending the joint slightly and applying a roller bandage in figure-of-eight turns. After bandaging, check circulation beyond the bandage: press the skin and confirm the colour returns within two seconds; if it does not, loosen and reapply, and recheck every 10 minutes.
    5. ElevationRaise the limb above heart level when resting, propped on pillows, to help limit swelling and bruising.
    6. Pain relief and rehabilitationParacetamol or ibuprofen can help with pain; aspirin is never given to anyone under 16. Once pain allows, range-of-motion, balance and strengthening exercises to prevent re-injury. Most ankle sprains improve in 2 weeks and heal in 6-12.

    Do not

    • Do not apply ice directly to skin or for more than 20 minutes
    • Do not apply heat, drink alcohol, run or massage in the first 72 hours (they increase bleeding and swelling)
    • Do not immobilise for weeks: stiffness and weakness follow
    • Do not leave a bandage on that is too tight: if skin colour beyond it does not return within two seconds of pressing, loosen and reapply
    • Do not give aspirin to anyone under 16

    Get medical help when

    • Go to A&E if the joint looks deformed, you heard a crack, cannot bear weight at all, or numbness or pale cold skin develops
    • Seek medical advice if the pain is severe or the person cannot move the injured part
    • See a clinician if it is no better after a week, or the joint gives way repeatedly

    Why it works: the anatomy

    Ligaments are dense collagen with a poor blood supply, so they heal slowly and by scar; the lateral ankle ligaments tear when the foot rolls inward, the knee's cruciate and collateral ligaments with twisting. Ice numbs pain nerves and slows local bleeding; elevation and compression use gravity and pressure to limit fluid leaking from damaged capillaries. Controlled loading aligns the new collagen fibres along lines of stress, which is why early movement gives stronger healing than rest.

    Conditions

    • Anterior cruciate ligament injury A sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, the band inside the knee that stops the tibia sliding forward on the femur and controls rotation.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.

    Guides and news

    • Sprains and strains: treatment and recovery The NHS describes PRICE care for the first 2 to 3 days, and most sprains and strains feel better after about 2 weeks.
