    Pelvis Anatomy

    Also known as: pelvis, hip, hip bones

    The bony pelvis is made up of the two hip bones together with the sacrum and the coccyx. It links the spine and the lower limbs, and it carries the weight of the trunk down to the legs during standing and walking. The pelvic cavity inside the bony ring houses the urinary bladder, rectum and internal reproductive organs, which the pelvis helps to protect.

    The hip bone

    The hip bone, also called the coxal bone, is one of two bones that together with the sacrum and coccyx make up the pelvis. Each adult hip bone is formed by three separate bones, the ilium, ischium and pubis, that fuse together during the late teenage years. The ilium is the upper, fan-shaped and largest part, and its curved upper margin is the iliac crest. The ischium forms the posteroinferior part and bears the ischial tuberosity, a large roughened area that carries the weight of the body when sitting. The pubis forms the anterior part and curves medially to join the pubis of the opposite hip bone at the pubic symphysis. The three bones converge to form the acetabulum, a deep, cup-shaped cavity on the lateral side of each hip bone that is part of the hip joint.

    Landmarks and muscle attachments

    The iliac crest ends at the rounded anterior superior iliac spine in front and the posterior superior iliac spine behind, and the anterior inferior and posterior inferior iliac spines lie below them. On the posterior margin of the ilium the greater sciatic notch, a large U-shaped indentation, lies above the ischial spine, the bony projection that separates it from the lesser sciatic notch. The obturator foramen is the large opening at the front of the hip bone, between the ischium and pubis. The ischial tuberosity also serves as the attachment for the posterior thigh muscles.

    Sacrum, coccyx, joints and ligaments

    The midline sacrum and coccyx complete the bony pelvis. At the front the two hip bones meet at the pubic symphysis, and at the back each hip bone unites with the sacrum at a sacroiliac joint. The coccyx attaches to the sacrum at the sacrococcygeal joint. The anterior, posterior and interosseous sacroiliac ligaments each contribute to stabilising the sacroiliac joint, and the interosseous ligament forms the main connection between the sacrum and ilium. The sacroiliac joint is supplied by several arteries, including the iliolumbar, superior gluteal and lateral sacral arteries. The sacrotuberous ligament runs from the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity, and the sacrospinous ligament is a triangular band from the ischial spine to the sacrum and coccyx. Together these two ligaments form the greater and lesser sciatic foramina, and the pudendal nerve lies between them.

    The true and false pelvis

    The pelvic brim, also called the pelvic inlet, separates the true pelvis from the false pelvis above it. It begins at the sacral promontory behind and the pubic symphysis in front, and its boundary landmarks also include the pectineal and arcuate lines. The broad false (greater) pelvis lies above the brim, is formed by the wings of the ilium and belongs to the abdomen. The narrower true (lesser) pelvis lies below the brim and is bounded below by the pelvic outlet, which is bounded by the coccyx, the ischial tuberosities and the pubic symphysis. The true pelvis holds the urinary bladder, rectum and part of the colon, and also the uterus, fallopian tubes and ovaries in people who have them.

    Shape differences between pelvises

    OpenStax describes general differences between the female and male pelvis, and says the female pelvis is wider to accommodate childbirth. It describes the female pelvis as having a larger subpubic angle, a rounder pelvic brim and a wider, shallower lesser pelvic cavity. The female sacrum is described as wider, shorter and less curved, with a sacral promontory that projects less into the pelvic cavity, which gives the inlet a rounder or oval outline. The greater sciatic notch is described as broader in the female pelvis, and in general the bones of the male pelvis are thicker and heavier.

    Function and blood supply

    The main role of the pelvic girdle is to support the upper body and transfer body weight to the lower limbs. StatPearls describes the hip bone as forming a sturdy basin that supports the trunk and provides attachment for lower limb muscles and ligaments. In females, the pelvic outlet also offers support and guidance for a foetus through the birth canal. The internal iliac artery divides into anterior and posterior divisions: the anterior supplies the pelvic organs, the perineum and the gluteal and adductor areas, and the posterior supplies the parietal structures of the pelvis and the gluteal region.

    Pelvic and hip fractures

    A fracture of the pubic rami, which are parts of the pubis at the front of the pelvis, most often follows a fall from standing. Falls are common in older people, and people are more prone to these fractures if their bones are thin (osteoporosis). A Frimley Health NHS leaflet says that pain usually increases with movement and weight bearing, and NHS trust leaflets say that these fractures are usually managed without surgery. In injuries that disrupt the pelvic ring, the posterior pelvic venous plexus is the main source of haemorrhage.

    A hip fracture is a different injury, a break in the top of the thigh bone close to the hip joint, usually caused by a fall or an injury to the side of the hip.

    Pelvic girdle pain in pregnancy

    Healthier Together, an NHS Wales site, describes pelvic girdle pain in pregnancy as a collection of uncomfortable symptoms caused by stiffness of the pelvic joints, or the joints moving unevenly, at the back or front of the pelvis. Pain can be felt over the pubic bone at the front and across one or both sides of the lower back, and it can spread into the thighs. The NHS advises speaking to a midwife or GP if pelvic pain makes it hard to move around.

    How the pelvis is investigated

    A hip fracture is usually first suspected from the symptoms, a physical examination and the history of the injury, and imaging such as an X-ray, an MRI scan or a CT scan is used to confirm it. For pelvic injuries, StatPearls notes that CT helps to assess the pelvis and detect bleeding, and cautions clinicians not to assume that a pelvic ring injury is the only source of bleeding, with chest and abdominal bleeding excluded first. An X-ray examination usually takes only a few minutes and nothing is felt during the exposure; the person needs to keep still so that the image is not blurred, and several X-rays may be taken from different angles.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
