    Ureter Anatomy

    Also known as: ureter

    The ureters are a pair of muscular tubes that carry urine from the renal pelvis of each kidney down the posterior abdominal wall and across the pelvis to the trigone of the bladder. Urine does not simply drain by gravity: waves of peristalsis in the ureteric wall propel it towards the bladder, and the oblique entry of each ureter through the bladder wall acts as a valve that stops urine flowing back.

    Location and course

    Each ureter begins at the pelviureteric junction, roughly at the level of the second lumbar vertebra, where the renal pelvis funnels down and narrows into the ureter. From the apex of the renal pelvis at the kidney hilum the ureter descends on the anterior surface of the psoas major muscle, remaining retroperitoneal throughout its abdominal course, and crosses the pelvic brim at the bifurcation of the common iliac artery, where it enters the pelvis. It then runs down the lateral pelvic wall, lying on the internal iliac artery, before turning forwards and medially to pierce the posterolateral wall of the bladder. The two ureters open into the bladder at the upper corners of the trigone. On an abdominal radiograph the ureter follows the tips of the lumbar transverse processes, a useful guide when looking for stones.

    Parts and relations

    The ureter is divided into abdominal and pelvic portions at the pelvic brim. In the abdomen it is crossed anteriorly by the gonadal vessels and, on the right, lies behind the second part of the duodenum and the vessels of the right colon, while on the left it passes behind the left colic vessels and the sigmoid mesocolon. In the pelvis its relations differ between the sexes. In the female the ureter runs beneath the uterine artery close to the cervix, so it is at risk when the artery is tied during hysterectomy; in the male the vas deferens crosses above it just before it reaches the bladder. The ureter is narrowest at three points: the pelviureteric junction, the crossing of the pelvic brim and the vesicoureteric junction, where it passes obliquely through the bladder wall. These are the sites where stones lodge.

    Blood supply and innervation

    The arterial supply is segmental. The abdominal ureter receives branches from the renal, gonadal and common iliac arteries and directly from the abdominal aorta; the pelvic ureter is supplied by branches of the internal iliac artery, including the vesical and, in the female, uterine arteries. These vessels run along the ureter within its outer coat and join to form an anastomotic plexus, so the ureter can survive division of one or two feeders, but it cannot be stripped or resected proximally and distally at the same time without compromising its blood supply. Veins follow the arteries, and lymph drains to the lumbar and iliac nodes. The ureter is supplied by autonomic fibres from the renal, aortic and hypogastric plexuses; its pain fibres return to the lower thoracic and upper lumbar segments, which is why ureteric colic is felt in the loin and radiates to the groin.

    Structure and function

    The ureteric wall has three layers: a mucosa of transitional epithelium that can stretch without tearing, a thick muscular coat of longitudinal and circular smooth muscle, and an outer adventitia that carries the vessels and nerves. Urine does not passively drain into the bladder but is propelled by waves of peristalsis that begin in the smooth muscle of the renal pelvis and travel down the ureter, each wave pushing a bolus of urine ahead of a contraction that almost closes the lumen. The oblique passage of the ureter through the bladder wall means that as the bladder fills and its wall tightens, the intramural ureter is compressed, forming a valve that prevents urine refluxing towards the kidney. Peristaltic frequency rises when urine production increases, and at very high flows the ureter can remain open and conduct urine as a continuous column.

    Variation

    Because the ureter develops from the ureteric bud, an outgrowth of the mesonephric duct that grows towards the metanephric kidney, early branching of the bud produces a duplicated system: two ureters may drain one kidney, joining before the bladder or opening separately, in which case the upper-pole ureter opens lower and more medially than expected. A ureter that fails to join the bladder properly opens ectopically into the urethra or genital tract. A retrocaval ureter passes behind the inferior vena cava and may be compressed by it. Where the kidney itself is abnormal, as in a horseshoe kidney, the ureters pass in front of the fused isthmus and may be kinked. Congenital narrowing at the pelviureteric junction or incompetence of the vesicoureteric valve, allowing reflux of infected urine, are both recognised causes of kidney damage in childhood.

    Clinical relevance

    Stones are the commonest ureteric problem. Many cause no symptoms while in the kidney, but when a stone moves into the ureter it stretches the tube and causes renal colic, a severe cramping pain in the flank that comes and goes and may travel to the groin; blood in the urine is common. A stone blocking the ureter can lead to kidney infection, and fever is a warning sign that requires urgent treatment. Non-contrast CT is the standard first test, with ultrasound preferred in pregnancy and in children; blood tests check kidney function and signs of infection. Small stones often pass on their own, while stones that do not may be removed or fragmented through a thin telescope passed up the ureter (ureteroscopy). The ureter is also at risk of injury in pelvic surgery, particularly where it crosses deep to the uterine artery, and of compression by tumours or fibrosis.

    The urinary in brief

    The two kidneys filter about 180 litres of plasma a day, returning almost all of it to the blood and excreting the rest as urine. Urine drains through the ureters into the bladder, a muscular reservoir that empties through the urethra. The kidneys also regulate blood pressure, red cell production and vitamin D activation.
