    Thymus Anatomy

    The thymus is a soft, two-lobed lymphoid organ lying behind the upper sternum in the superior and anterior mediastinum, in front of the great vessels and the heart. It is the primary lymphoid organ in which T lymphocytes mature: immature cells arriving from the bone marrow are tested against the body's own tissues and most are destroyed, so that only cells able to recognise foreign antigen without attacking self are released. The gland is largest in childhood and is gradually replaced by fat with age.

    Location and relations

    The thymus lies in the superior part of the retrosternal mediastinum, immediately behind the manubrium and the upper part of the body of the sternum, and extends down into the anterior mediastinum in front of the pericardium. Behind it lie the left brachiocephalic vein, the arch of the aorta and its branches, and the trachea; on each side are the pleurae and the phrenic nerves. In children the upper poles of the two lobes often reach up through the thoracic inlet into the neck as far as the lower edge of the thyroid, following the gland's embryological path of descent. StatPearls notes that variation in the number, size and position of the lobes is common, and accessory thymic tissue may be found anywhere along the line of descent from the pharynx to the mediastinum.

    Lobes and structure

    The thymus is bilobed, and the two lobes, though pressed together and joined by connective tissue, keep separate blood supplies, lymphatic drainage and innervation throughout life. Each lobe is enclosed in a capsule whose septa divide it into lobules. OpenStax describes a densely packed outer cortex, which stains intensely because it is crowded with developing lymphocytes, and a paler inner medulla, where the surviving thymocytes gather before leaving the organ. The framework of both zones is a network of epithelial cells, with macrophages and dendritic cells among them; the medulla also contains whorled Hassall's corpuscles of keratinised epithelium that are characteristic of the thymus under the microscope. Tight capillary walls and an epithelial sheath form a blood-thymus barrier in the cortex that shields developing cells from circulating antigens.

    Blood supply, drainage and nerves

    The thymus has no single named artery. StatPearls describes its blood as coming mainly from the inferior thyroid arteries above and branches of the internal thoracic (internal mammary) arteries on each side, whose lateral branches to the gland are sometimes called the lateral thymic arteries, with minor contributions from the pericardiacophrenic and anterior intercostal arteries. The veins follow the arteries, and a large posterior thymic vein drains into the left brachiocephalic (innominate) vein that passes directly behind the gland; this vein is at risk during surgery on the thymus. Lymph leaves through the parasternal, brachiocephalic and tracheobronchial nodes; the thymus has no afferent lymphatics, which fits its role as a site of lymphocyte production rather than antigen filtering. Its sympathetic and vagal nerve supply is small and reaches it with the vessels.

    Function

    OpenStax identifies the bone marrow and thymus as the two primary lymphoid organs. B lymphocytes complete their development in the marrow, but immature T cells, the thymocytes, leave the marrow and migrate to the thymus to mature. In the cortex they meet cortical epithelial cells and are tested: those that cannot recognise the body's own MHC molecules, and those that bind self-antigens too strongly, are eliminated, so that only a small fraction of the cells that enter leave as mature, functional T cells. Survivors emerge as single-positive cells carrying either CD4 or CD8 and pass through the medulla into the blood to populate the lymph nodes and spleen. The process teaches T cells to recognise foreign antigen presented on self-MHC while remaining tolerant of self. Thymic epithelial cells also secrete hormones such as thymosin that support this maturation.

    Development and involution

    The thymus develops from the third pharyngeal pouches on each side; the two primordia begin to form around the sixth week of gestation, descend into the chest and fuse in the anterosuperior mediastinum around the eighth week, which is why the lobes remain functionally separate. Lymphoid precursors from the bone marrow colonise the epithelial framework and the gland is already active before birth. Relative to body size it is largest in infancy and reaches its greatest absolute weight around puberty, after which it involutes: lymphoid tissue is slowly replaced by fat so that the adult thymus is a fatty remnant that still produces a trickle of new T cells. Because of this, the thymus is easy to see on a child's chest radiograph but is usually inconspicuous in adults, and a prominent mediastinal mass in an adult is treated as abnormal.

    Clinical relevance

    Thymoma, a tumour of the thymic epithelium, is the commonest tumour of the anterior mediastinum in adults and frequently occurs together with myasthenia gravis, an autoimmune disorder of the neuromuscular junction. The NHS describes weakness of the eye, eyelid, facial, chewing and swallowing muscles that worsens after activity and improves with rest. Investigation includes blood tests for the responsible antibodies, nerve conduction studies and, for the thymus itself, a CT or MRI scan of the chest to check whether the gland is larger than usual or contains a tumour. Removal of the thymus (thymectomy) is part of the treatment for thymoma and for some people with myasthenia. Failure of the thymus to develop, as in DiGeorge syndrome, leaves a child without T cells and prone to severe infections, and a large normal thymus in an infant is a common cause of a widened mediastinum on X-ray.

    The lymphatic & immune in brief

    Lymphatic vessels return tissue fluid to the blood and carry it through lymph nodes where immune cells inspect it. The spleen filters blood, recycles old red cells and mounts immune responses; the thymus, large in childhood, trains T lymphocytes. This release models the spleen and the two lobes of the thymus; lymph nodes and lymphatic vessels are not included.
