    Hand Bones Anatomy

    Also known as: wrist, fingers, carpal bones

    Each hand consists of 27 bones: eight carpal bones in the wrist, five metacarpal bones forming the palm and fourteen phalanges in the thumb and fingers. The carpal bones lie in two rows of four, and only the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum contribute to the radiocarpal (wrist) joint. The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone, often as a result of a fall onto the hand.

    Bones of the hand

    The eight carpal bones make up the wrist, the five metacarpal bones form the palm and the fourteen phalanges form the thumb and fingers. The metacarpals are numbered one to five starting from the thumb side. The first metacarpal is freely mobile, moving independently of the others, which join together to make up the palm. The thumb has only two phalanges, a proximal and a distal, while each of the other four digits, from the index finger to the little finger, has a proximal, middle and distal phalanx. The expanded distal end of each metacarpal articulates with the proximal phalanx of the thumb or one of the fingers at the metacarpophalangeal joint, and also forms the knuckles of the hand.

    Carpal bones

    The eight carpal bones are arranged in two rows of four, a proximal row and a distal row. Counting from the thumb side, the proximal row is the scaphoid, lunate, triquetrum and pisiform, and the distal row is the trapezium, trapezoid, capitate and hamate. Several names describe shape: scaphoid means boat-shaped, lunate moon-shaped, triquetrum three-cornered, pisiform pea-shaped, capitate head-shaped and hamate hooked. The pisiform can be felt as a bony bump at the medial base of the hand, and the hamate has a prominent bony extension on its anterior side, the hook of the hamate. Only the scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum contribute to the radiocarpal joint: the scaphoid and lunate articulate directly with the distal end of the radius, whereas the triquetrum articulates with a fibrocartilaginous pad.

    The carpal tunnel

    The flexor retinaculum is a fibrous connective tissue band that forms the anterior roof of the carpal tunnel at the wrist, and many experts consider it synonymous with the transverse carpal ligament. Posterior to the flexor retinaculum, within the carpal tunnel, lie the four flexor digitorum superficialis tendons, the four flexor digitorum profundus tendons, the flexor pollicis longus tendon and the median nerve. Carpal tunnel syndrome is compression of the median nerve at the wrist, causing hand pain, numbness and tingling in the area the nerve supplies. As the condition advances there can be weakness and eventual wasting of the thenar muscles at the base of the thumb. Raised pressure within the carpal tunnel is the primary factor in the onset of the condition, although in most cases the underlying cause is unknown.

    Fractures of the wrist and scaphoid

    The scaphoid is the most commonly fractured carpal bone, and NHS trust leaflets describe it as a small bone of the hand on the thumb side of the wrist. Scaphoid fractures and wrist fractures usually result from a fall onto an outstretched hand. A scaphoid injury typically causes pain and tenderness near the base of the thumb, and the wrist may swell, but most scaphoid fractures leave the wrist looking normal and cause only mild symptoms. Some people also have difficulty gripping or twisting the wrist or thumb. One NHS trust page on distal radial fractures, which are breaks of the forearm bone next to the wrist joint, describes immediate severe pain, swelling and often deformity of the wrist, painful movement, and sometimes tingling or numbness in the fingers. Wrist fractures are common in young children and older people.

    How scaphoid injuries are investigated

    A scaphoid fracture does not always show on the first X-ray, and in some people it is not obvious until some days after the injury. If the first X-ray does not show a fracture but one is still suspected, the person is examined again and the X-ray repeated at the fracture clinic, and an MRI scan is requested if suspicion continues. One NHS trust leaflet explains that, until a fracture can be ruled out, the wrist is treated as if the bone were broken, because the scaphoid has a poor blood supply. An X-ray is a quick, painless procedure that produces images of the inside of the body. The person needs to keep still during the exposure so the picture is not blurred, and more than one X-ray may be taken from different angles.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
