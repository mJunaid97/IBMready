    Cerebellum Anatomy

    The cerebellum, the 'little brain', sits in the posterior cranial fossa behind the brainstem and below the back of the cerebrum. It does not start movement itself; instead it compares what the cerebrum intends with what the body is actually doing and smooths the result, so that posture, gait and skilled movements are steady and well timed.

    Location and relations

    The cerebellum occupies the posterior cranial fossa, the lowest part of the back of the skull. It lies behind the fourth ventricle, the pons and the medulla oblongata, and is attached to the posterior surface of the brainstem by three pairs of thick white-matter stalks, the cerebellar peduncles. The pons is its main connection with the rest of the brainstem, and the two structures develop from the same embryonic vesicle, the metencephalon. Above, a fold of dura mater, the tentorium cerebelli, separates the cerebellum from the occipital lobes of the cerebrum. Because the cerebellum is packed into a tight bony compartment next to the brainstem and the outflow of cerebrospinal fluid, swelling or bleeding here can compress vital centres quickly, which is why cerebellar strokes and tumours are treated as emergencies.

    Lobes, cortex and deep nuclei

    Seen from above, the cerebellum has two lateral hemispheres joined by a narrow midline ridge, the vermis. Deep fissures divide it into three lobes: the V-shaped primary fissure separates the anterior lobe from the posterior lobe, and the posterolateral fissure separates the posterior lobe from the small flocculonodular lobe on the under-surface. The cerebellar cortex is a thin sheet of grey matter folded into many narrow accordion-like folds (folia), which greatly increases its surface area; its grey matter is arranged in three layers. Beneath the cortex lies a core of white matter containing four pairs of deep cerebellar nuclei, the dentate, emboliform, globose and fastigial nuclei. The cortex processes incoming information, but it is these deep nuclei that send almost all of the cerebellum's output to the rest of the nervous system.

    Peduncles and functional zones

    Each peduncle carries a different kind of traffic. The inferior cerebellar peduncle brings input from the medulla, notably from the inferior olive, carrying sensory feedback from the periphery and the spinal cord. The middle cerebellar peduncle, the largest, arises from the pons and is the main route by which copies of the cerebrum's motor commands reach the cerebellar cortex. The superior cerebellar peduncle carries the output of the deep nuclei upwards to the red nucleus of the midbrain and, via the thalamus, back to the motor cortex. Functionally the cerebellum has three zones: the vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe), which works with the balance organs of the inner ear to steady the eyes and trunk; the spinocerebellum (vermis and nearby cortex), which controls posture and gait; and the cerebrocerebellum (lateral hemispheres), which refines skilled, planned movement.

    Blood supply

    Arterial blood reaches the cerebellum through three paired arteries (the superior cerebellar artery, SCA, the anterior inferior cerebellar artery, AICA, and the posterior inferior cerebellar artery, PICA), which arise from the vertebrobasilar circulation at the back of the brain and wrap around the brainstem to reach the cerebellar surface. The posterior inferior cerebellar artery supplies the under-surface of the hemispheres and part of the medulla as it passes, so an occlusion here commonly produces a combined cerebellar and brainstem picture rather than a purely cerebellar one. The anterior inferior cerebellar artery supplies the front and lower part of the cerebellum and closely accompanies the nerves of hearing and balance, which is why its blockage can cause sudden deafness and vertigo alongside incoordination. Venous blood drains into the dural venous sinuses around the posterior fossa.

    Function

    The cerebellum coordinates gait, maintains posture, regulates muscle tone and refines voluntary muscle activity, but it cannot itself initiate a muscle contraction. Its working principle is comparison: the cerebellar cortex receives a copy of the motor command descending from the cerebrum and, at the same time, sensory feedback about what the limbs and trunk are actually doing. Where the two do not match, the cerebellum adjusts the descending signals to correct the error, so that a reaching hand slows smoothly as it nears its target and the body stays upright on uneven ground. With repetition these corrections are stored, which is why the cerebellum is central to motor learning: riding a bicycle becomes automatic because the cerebellum has learned the timing. The vestibulocerebellum also keeps the gaze steady while the head moves.

    Clinical relevance

    Damage to the cerebellum produces ataxia: loss of coordination with a wobbly, wide-based gait, clumsiness and frequent falls. Midline (vermis) lesions mainly disturb gait and trunk balance, whereas a lesion in one hemisphere causes limb ataxia on the same side of the body. Other signs are nystagmus, slurred speech, intention tremor and dysmetria, tested with finger-to-nose and heel-to-shin manoeuvres. Causes include multiple sclerosis, head injury, lack of oxygen, long-term heavy alcohol use, stroke and inherited conditions such as Friedreich's ataxia; in some late-onset cases no cause is found. Investigation begins with history and examination; MRI excludes stroke, tumour, infection or malformation and shows cerebellar shrinkage, often most marked in the vermis. Blood tests (for example vitamin B12, vitamin E and certain antibodies) and genetic testing follow when the cause is unclear.

    About

    The 'little brain' behind the brainstem, with two hemispheres and a central vermis.

    Function. Coordinates balance, posture and the timing and smoothness of movement; also contributes to learning and language.

    Clinical note. Damage causes unsteady gait, tremor and slurred speech; alcohol acts on it acutely.

    The nervous system in brief

    The nervous system gathers information, decides and commands. The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system; the cranial and spinal nerves with their ganglia form the peripheral nervous system. This model is richest in the head: the cerebral gyri and lobes, the basal ganglia, thalamus and hypothalamus, the brainstem and cerebellum, the ventricles, the optic pathway and the nerves of the orbit. Peripheral nerves of the limbs are not part of this BodyParts3D release.
