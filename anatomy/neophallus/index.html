    Neophallus Anatomy

    Also known as: phalloplasty anatomy, metoidioplasty, phalloplasty

    A neophallus is a penis constructed by surgery, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people, and also after loss of the penis through injury or cancer. Phalloplasty builds the phallus from a flap of skin, fat and fascia taken with its own vessels and nerves, usually from the forearm or thigh, while metoidioplasty releases the clitoris enlarged by testosterone to stand free as a small phallus. Neither has erectile tissue beyond that of the clitoris, so structure, sensation and function depend on the tissue used and the stages completed. The 3D model is schematic: a phalloplasty phallus with its glans, a lengthened urethra and a scrotum with testicular implants, drawn to typical dimensions on the reference pelvis.

    Key facts

    Commonest donor siteRadial forearm free flap: skin, fat and fascia on the radial artery, cephalic vein and antebrachial cutaneous nerves
    AlternativeAnterolateral thigh flap on the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, usually kept on its pedicle
    UrethraWhen urethral lengthening is chosen, a tube within a tube of flap skin is joined to a urethra lengthened with labia minora and vaginal mucosa
    SensationFlap nerves joined to a dorsal clitoral nerve and the ilioinguinal nerve; returns over months to a year or more
    ErectionNo erectile tissue in a phalloplasty; an inflatable or malleable implant is added at a later stage
    MetoidioplastyReleases the testosterone-enlarged clitoris, typically several centimetres long, in one stage with no donor site
    Retained organsIn people assigned female at birth there is no prostate; the cervix, uterus and ovaries remain unless removed and still need care

    Location and relations

    The neophallus is attached to the pubic region where the clitoris and labia were, its base over the pubic symphysis between the hip bones and above the urethral opening. In phalloplasty the clitoris is usually stripped of its surface skin and buried at the base of the new shaft, and the labia majora are joined below to form a scrotum. If the urethra is lengthened, the new urethra runs from the original meatus through a lengthened perineal segment and along the underside of the shaft to the tip; the vagina is usually removed, because a lengthened urethra beside a retained vagina is prone to fistula. Behind lie the perineal body, external anal sphincter and rectum. The urethra passes through the urogenital hiatus of the levator ani, between the pubococcygeus of each side, and then through the deep perineal pouch, where the external urethral sphincter surrounds it, and on through the perineal membrane; the puborectalis sling lies behind, around the anorectal junction.

    Structure

    A phalloplasty shaft is a rolled flap: an outer tube of skin over fat and deep fascia, and a glans shaped later by a ridge and skin graft (glansplasty). The radial forearm free flap is a rectangle of skin taken with the radial artery, the cephalic vein and the lateral and medial antebrachial cutaneous nerves; a narrower strip of the same flap is rolled skin-inward to form the urethra. The anterolateral thigh flap, raised on the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, is thicker, usually stays on its pedicle and often needs a separate urethral lining. A metoidioplasty phallus is the clitoris itself, released from its suspensory ligament and the labia minora, with a urethra optionally built along its underside from labial and vaginal mucosa. Scrotoplasty from the labia majora with testicular implants completes either operation.

    Blood supply and lymph

    A free flap lives only through its vascular pedicle. In radial forearm phalloplasty the radial artery is joined under the microscope to a recipient vessel in the groin, usually the deep inferior epigastric artery or a branch of the femoral artery, and the cephalic and accompanying veins to the great saphenous or femoral veins; the joins are watched closely for the first days, since thrombosis threatens the whole phallus. The hand keeps its supply through the ulnar artery and the palmar arches, confirmed before surgery. A pedicled anterolateral thigh flap keeps its own artery and vein from the lateral circumflex femoral system, so no microsurgery is needed. A metoidioplasty phallus keeps the clitoral branches of the internal pudendal vessels, so its erectile tissue still engorges. Lymph from the shaft skin drains to the superficial inguinal nodes.

    Innervation

    Protective sensation comes from the flap's own cutaneous nerves, the antebrachial cutaneous nerves of the forearm or the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve of the thigh, which are joined at the base of the phallus to the ilioinguinal nerve and to one of the dorsal clitoral nerves. Erotic sensation comes from the clitoris, whose dorsal nerves are branches of the pudendal nerve (S2 to S4) and are preserved when it is buried at the base of the shaft. Regrowth is slow: the shaft is numb at first, sensation returns from base towards tip over months to a year or more, and the tip may never be as sensitive as the base. In metoidioplasty the clitoral nerves are preserved, so erotic sensation is usually retained from the outset. The forearm donor site is left with altered sensation.

    Function and hormonal influence

    The aims are standing urination, sexual function and a body that matches the person's gender. A phalloplasty shaft has no erectile tissue, so it cannot become erect on its own; rigidity for penetration comes from an erectile device, inflatable or malleable, placed at a later stage once protective sensation has returned so the skin can guard against pressure damage. Orgasm comes from the buried clitoris and the reinnervated shaft. A metoidioplasty phallus keeps the natural erection and full sensation of clitoral tissue but is small, so penetrative sex is usually not possible; it allows standing urination if the urethra is lengthened. Testosterone maintains libido, clitoral size and the oiliness and hair of the skin; the shaft skin behaves as forearm or thigh skin, growing hair unless removed beforehand, and does not engorge with arousal.

    Changes through the stages and over life

    Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty are options in gender-affirming care that some, not all, trans men and non-binary people choose; gender incongruence is not a mental illness, and the World Health Organization classifies it under conditions related to sexual health. A neophallus is built in stages over a year or more. Before surgery testosterone has usually enlarged the clitoris, and hair is removed from the donor skin that will line the urethra; hysterectomy and oophorectomy often precede the first stage, and vaginectomy is done when the urethra is lengthened. Later stages add glansplasty, scrotoplasty with testicular implants and, after sensation returns, an erectile implant. Swelling settles over months and the forearm scar matures over a year. The shaft is skin and fat, so unlike the clitoris it does not enlarge or shrink with hormone levels, although its skin ages and grows hair as forearm or thigh skin does, and implants have a finite life.

    Clinical relevance

    Urethral complications are the commonest problems after phalloplasty with urethral lengthening: a fistula, with urine leaking from the perineal or shaft urethra, and a stricture, with scar narrowing the channel and slowing the stream, which may need dilatation, urethrotomy or graft repair. Flap loss from thrombosis is rare but serious and needs an urgent return to theatre. Erectile implants can become infected or erode through the skin, since there is no corporal tissue to anchor them. The forearm donor site is covered with a skin graft that can heal slowly or fail in part, and leaves a visible scar and altered sensation; hand function is usually preserved but the arm is splinted at first. Urinary infections and retention can follow the urethral stages. Screening follows the anatomy present: a retained cervix still needs cervical screening, which has to be requested from the GP by anyone registered as male, because invitations are issued on the gender marker held by the practice; a retained uterus or ovaries still need gynaecological care, there is no prostate, and residual breast tissue after chest surgery still needs assessment if a lump appears. Anyone catheterising a neophallus should know which stages are complete, as the urethra may be tortuous.

    The gender-affirming surgery in brief

    No open 3D scan of anatomy after gender-affirming surgery exists, so this layer is schematic: simple solids drawn to the typical dimensions and positions described in the surgical literature, placed in the reference pelvis, and labelled so that every piece opens the same clinical pages as the rest of the atlas. It shows the results of the two main genital operations together. After penile-inversion vaginoplasty a canal about 12 to 15 cm deep lined with penile and scrotal skin runs up and back between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind, a clitoris fashioned from part of the glans sits under the pubic arch, and the scrotal and shaft skin form the labia; the prostate is kept. After phalloplasty a phallus about 13 to 15 cm long built from a forearm or thigh flap hangs from the pubic region, often with a lengthened urethra running to its tip, and the labia majora are joined into a scrotum that later receives testicular implants; an erectile implant may be added later. Metoidioplasty, which releases the clitoris enlarged by testosterone instead of using a flap, is described on the neophallus page.
