    Testicle Anatomy

    Also known as: testis, testicle, testicles, epididymis

    The testes, also called the testicles, are a pair of egg-shaped glands that lie in the scrotum, the sac of loose skin below the penis. They make sperm and the hormone testosterone. Sperm pass from each testis into the epididymis, a coiled tube in which they mature, and then along the deferent duct, which joins the duct of the seminal vesicle near the prostate to form the ejaculatory duct. The seminal vesicles lie behind the prostate and add fluid to the semen.

    Location and relations

    Each testis lies in its own compartment of the scrotum, separated from the other testis by the scrotal septum. The dartos muscle forms the muscle layer beneath the scrotal skin and continues inwards to form that septum. Each testis is covered on the outside by the tunica vaginalis, and beneath this lies the tough, white tunica albuginea. The deferent duct is bundled with blood vessels and nerves in the spermatic cord, which supports the testis in the scrotum.

    Structure of the testis

    The tunica albuginea folds inwards to form septa that divide the testis into hundreds of lobules, and at the back of the testis it folds in to form the mediastinum testis, an incomplete septum. The tightly coiled seminiferous tubules form the bulk of each testis, and sperm are made within them. The seminiferous tubules join straight tubules, the tubuli recti, which lead to the rete testis, a network in the mediastinum testis, and efferent ductules then connect the rete testis to the epididymis.

    Leydig cells, which lie between the tubules, make testosterone. Sertoli cells help control the survival of the developing germ cells, and tight junctions between them form the blood-testis barrier. This barrier keeps bloodborne substances away from the germ cells and stops surface antigens on developing germ cells from escaping into the bloodstream and provoking an autoimmune response.

    Epididymis, deferent duct and seminal vesicle

    The epididymis is a coiled tube with a head, a body and a tail. Sperm continue to mature as they pass through it and are stored in the tail until ejaculation. The deferent duct is also known as the vas deferens or ductus deferens. It continues from the tail of the epididymis, and during ejaculation contraction of smooth muscle pushes sperm from the tail into the duct.

    The deferent duct ascends through the inguinal canal into the pelvic cavity, looping behind and to the side of the prostate. Its ampulla joins the duct of the seminal vesicle at the base of the prostate to form the ejaculatory duct, which enters the prostate and empties into the prostatic urethra. Each seminal vesicle lies in the pelvis behind the prostate, below the fundus of the bladder and above the rectum, beside the ampulla of the deferent duct.

    Blood supply, nerves and lymph

    The testicular arteries arise from the front and side of the abdominal aorta, just below the origin of the renal arteries, with additional supply from the artery of the vas deferens and the cremasteric artery. Venous blood leaves through the pampiniform plexus, a network of veins that lies in front of the deferent duct. The testes receive both sympathetic and parasympathetic nerve fibres. Lymph from the testes travels with the testicular vessels and drains to the para-aortic lymph nodes, which lie beside the aorta. The arteries to the seminal vesicles come from the middle and inferior vesical arteries and the middle rectal artery.

    Function: sperm, testosterone and temperature

    The testes make sperm and the androgen testosterone, and luteinising hormone binds to receptors on the Leydig cells and increases their production of testosterone.

    Sperm production proceeds more efficiently when the testes are kept a few degrees below core body temperature. In cold weather the dartos and cremaster muscles contract to lift the testes towards the body and reduce the surface area of the scrotum, which retains heat; when the temperature rises the scrotum relaxes, which increases its surface area and promotes heat loss.

    During ejaculation sperm travel through the deferent duct and mix with fluid from the associated seminal vesicle.

    Clinical relevance: torsion and infection

    In testicular torsion the spermatic cord twists, which cuts off the blood supply to the testicle and can cause lasting damage. Sudden, severe pain in one testicle is the most common sign, and NHS advice is to go straight to A&E or call 999. Epididymitis is swelling and pain of the epididymis, the tube behind the testicle, and the NHS says it is usually caused by a sexually transmitted infection or a urine infection, with other causes including mumps, tuberculosis and a groin injury. Depending on the cause, there may also be difficulty passing urine or a discharge from the penis.

    Clinical relevance: swellings and undescended testes

    Most testicular lumps and swellings are not cancer, although the NHS advises seeing a GP about a lump or swelling in a testicle. Cystic swellings are usually caused by a hydrocele, a cyst in the epididymis or a varicocele, and these are all benign and need treatment only if they cause significant symptoms. A hydrocele is fluid that collects between the layers of the tunica vaginalis, and a varicocele is an abnormal widening of the veins of the pampiniform plexus that usually occurs on the left.

    An undescended testicle has not moved into the scrotum as it usually does, and the condition is common in young boys. Often the testicle moves into place on its own, but sometimes surgery is needed, and the problem is usually found at the newborn examination. Left untreated, an undescended testicle can lead to fertility problems later in life and an increased risk of testicular cancer, and it can also twist (testicular torsion), which can be painful.

    Testicular cancer and how it is investigated

    Testicular cancer is a disease in which malignant cells form in the tissue of one or both testicles. The NHS lists a lump or swelling in a testicle, a testicle getting bigger, an ache or pain in the testicle or scrotum, and a heavy, firm or hard scrotum as the main symptoms. Having these symptoms does not definitely mean testicular cancer, but the NHS advises seeing a GP about any of them. A scrotal ultrasound scan will usually distinguish between benign and cancerous swellings. When testicular cancer is suspected, the NHS lists blood tests and an ultrasound scan of the testicles among the tests used, after which a specialist explains the results and the next steps.

    The reproductive in brief

    The reproductive system makes gametes and sex hormones and carries, delivers and feeds a pregnancy. On the female side the ovaries release an egg each cycle and make oestrogen and progesterone; the fallopian tubes carry the egg to the uterus, whose lining is shed each month as a period or supports a pregnancy; the cervix, vagina and vulva form the birth canal and external genitalia; and the breasts make milk. On the male side the testes produce sperm and testosterone, the epididymis and deferent ducts store and carry sperm, the seminal vesicles and prostate add most of the seminal fluid, and the erectile bodies of the penis deliver it. BodyParts3D, the dataset behind the 3D atlas, was built from one adult male, so this system holds the male organs. The female organs and the breasts are modelled from the NIH Human Reference Atlas and fitted into the same pelvis and chest as the Female reproductive layer, and the surgically constructed anatomy of gender-affirming surgery (neovagina, neophallus) is drawn schematically in the Vaginoplasty and Phalloplasty layers; all three are hidden by default and switch on from the systems list.
