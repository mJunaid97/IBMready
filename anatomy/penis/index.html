    Penis Anatomy

    The penis is the male organ of copulation and the final conduit for urine. It is built around three cylinders of erectile tissue: two corpora cavernosa, which form the bulk of the shaft, and the corpus spongiosum, which surrounds the spongy urethra and expands at the tip into the glans. Erection is a vascular event, driven by nitric oxide released from nerve endings that relaxes the smooth muscle of the penile arteries so that blood fills the erectile tissue faster than it can leave.

    Venous drainageDeep dorsal vein to the prostatic plexus; superficial dorsal vein to the external pudendal veins
    Nerve supplyDorsal nerve of the penis (pudendal nerve) for sensation; cavernous nerves from the prostatic plexus for erection
    FunctionErection, ejaculation of semen and passage of urine through the spongy urethra

    Location and parts

    The penis is divided into a root, a body and a glans. The root lies hidden in the superficial perineal space below the pelvic floor: the bulb of the penis is attached to the perineal membrane in the midline and is pierced by the urethra, while the two crura are anchored to the ischiopubic rami on either side. These three masses converge in front of the pubic symphysis to form the body, or shaft, which hangs free and is suspended from the pubis by the suspensory and fundiform ligaments. The body ends in the glans, whose expanded rim, the corona, overhangs a shallow groove, the neck. In the uncircumcised penis a double fold of skin, the prepuce or foreskin, forms a collar around the glans that protects and lubricates it. The skin of the shaft is thin, hairless and loosely attached, allowing it to move during erection.

    Erectile bodies and urethra

    Each of the two larger, lateral chambers is a corpus cavernosum, and together they make up the bulk of the penis. They lie side by side on the dorsal aspect of the shaft, enclosed in a dense fibrous tunica albuginea, and are connected through fenestrations in the septum between them so that blood moves freely from one to the other. Below them lies the corpus spongiosum, a smaller chamber that surrounds the spongy, or penile, urethra and can be felt as a raised ridge on the erect penis; proximally it expands into the bulb and distally into the glans. The tunica albuginea of the corpus spongiosum is thinner and more elastic than that of the corpora cavernosa, so the spongiosum stays less rigid during erection and the urethra within it can distend to allow semen to pass. The urethra opens at the external urethral meatus on the tip of the glans.

    Blood supply and venous drainage

    Branches of the internal pudendal arteries are the main suppliers of blood to the penis. The deep arteries of the penis run in the centre of each corpus cavernosum and give off helicine arteries that open into the cavernous spaces to supply the erectile tissue. The arteries of the bulb supply the bulbous part of the corpus spongiosum, the urethra and the bulbourethral gland. The dorsal arteries run along the dorsal vein between the corpora cavernosa to supply the fibrous tissue around the corpora, the corpus spongiosum, the spongy urethra and the penile skin. Venous blood from the erectile tissue collects in the deep dorsal vein, which passes beneath the pubic symphysis to the prostatic venous plexus, while the skin drains through the superficial dorsal vein to the external pudendal veins. Lymph drains to the superficial and deep inguinal and the internal iliac nodes.

    Innervation

    Sensation from the skin of the penis and the glans travels in the dorsal nerve of the penis, a terminal branch of the pudendal nerve. The glans has a high concentration of nerve endings, giving very sensitive skin that influences the likelihood of ejaculation. The erectile tissue is controlled by autonomic fibres. Parasympathetic fibres from the pelvic splanchnic nerves pass through the prostatic plexus and continue as the cavernous nerves, which run beside the prostate and under the pubic symphysis to the corpora cavernosa, prostate and urethra; these fibres release nitric oxide and are pro-erectile. Sympathetic fibres from the hypogastric nerves bring about ejaculation and return the penis to the flaccid state. The pudendal nerve also supplies the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles, which compress the root of the penis.

    Function

    During sexual arousal, nitric oxide is released from nerve endings near the blood vessels within the corpora cavernosa and spongiosum. It induces relaxation of the smooth muscle surrounding the penile arteries, producing the vasodilation necessary for erection: the helicine arteries open and blood floods the cavernous spaces, which expand against the tunica albuginea and compress the small veins that drain them. The result of increased blood flow into the penis and reduced blood return from it is erection; the stiff tunica of the corpora cavernosa converts the raised pressure into rigidity, while the more elastic spongiosum keeps the urethra open for ejaculation. At ejaculation, sympathetic activity contracts the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate, and the perineal muscles propel semen along the urethra, which when flaccid carries urine.

    Clinical relevance

    Because erection depends on intact arteries, nerves and hormones, diseases affecting any of these can cause erectile dysfunction; diabetes, certain medicines and psychological factors also contribute, and prostate or bladder cancer surgery can injure the nearby cavernous nerves and arteries. Assessment includes a genital examination looking for hypogonadism, phimosis, penile lesions or a palpable plaque. In Peyronie's disease a fibrous plaque in the tunica albuginea causes curvature or deformity of the erect penis, often with pain and some erectile dysfunction; its cause is unknown and may be autoimmune. Phimosis, a foreskin too tight to retract over the glans, and Peyronie's disease are anatomical findings that prompt referral to andrology. Priapism, a prolonged erection, is treated by aspirating blood from the corpora, possible because the two corpora communicate.

    The reproductive in brief

    The reproductive system makes gametes and sex hormones and carries, delivers and feeds a pregnancy. On the female side the ovaries release an egg each cycle and make oestrogen and progesterone; the fallopian tubes carry the egg to the uterus, whose lining is shed each month as a period or supports a pregnancy; the cervix, vagina and vulva form the birth canal and external genitalia; and the breasts make milk. On the male side the testes produce sperm and testosterone, the epididymis and deferent ducts store and carry sperm, the seminal vesicles and prostate add most of the seminal fluid, and the erectile bodies of the penis deliver it. BodyParts3D, the dataset behind the 3D atlas, was built from one adult male, so this system holds the male organs. The female organs and the breasts are modelled from the NIH Human Reference Atlas and fitted into the same pelvis and chest as the Female reproductive layer, and the surgically constructed anatomy of gender-affirming surgery (neovagina, neophallus) is drawn schematically in the Vaginoplasty and Phalloplasty layers; all three are hidden by default and switch on from the systems list.
