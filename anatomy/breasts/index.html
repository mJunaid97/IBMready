    Breast Anatomy

    Also known as: breast, mammary gland, mammary glands, nipple, chest

    The breasts are paired modified sweat glands on the front of the chest wall, lying over the pectoralis major muscles and made of milk-producing lobes embedded in fat and tethered by fibrous ligaments. They grow under oestrogen and progesterone, make milk under prolactin and release it under oxytocin, and they are the site of the commonest cancer in women. The atlas body is male, so this page highlights the chest wall muscles and vessels beneath and around the breast rather than the gland itself.

    Location and relations

    Each breast lies in the superficial fascia of the front of the chest wall, extending from about the second to the sixth rib and from the lateral edge of the sternum to the mid-axillary line. A tongue of glandular tissue, the axillary tail, runs from the upper outer quadrant along the lower border of pectoralis major towards the armpit, which is why breast tissue and breast lumps can be found in the axilla. About two-thirds of the base rests on the fascia over pectoralis major and the rest on serratus anterior. Between the gland and the pectoral fascia lies the retromammary space, a layer of loose connective tissue that lets the breast move freely over the muscle; a cancer that grows through it fixes the breast to the chest wall. The nipple usually sits at the fourth intercostal space, though its position varies with breast size and age.

    Structure: lobes, ducts and the nipple

    The gland consists of 15 to 20 lobes arranged like the spokes of a wheel around the nipple, each a tubulo-alveolar gland whose lobules of secretory alveoli drain into progressively larger ducts. Every lobe empties through a single lactiferous duct that widens into a lactiferous sinus beneath the areola and then narrows to open on the tip of the nipple. Smooth muscle in the nipple makes it erect with cold, touch or suckling, and the pigmented areola carries the small bumps of the areolar (Montgomery) glands, whose oily secretion protects the skin during breastfeeding. The lobes are embedded in fat, which gives the breast most of its size and shape, and the gland is tethered by the suspensory ligaments of Cooper, fibrous bands running from the skin to the deep fascia. A tumour that shortens these ligaments draws in the skin above it, the dimpling that warns of cancer.

    Blood supply and lymph

    Blood reaches the breast from several directions: perforating branches of the internal thoracic (internal mammary) artery pierce the upper intercostal spaces beside the sternum to supply the medial part, the lateral thoracic artery from the axillary artery supplies the upper and lateral parts, and perforating branches of the posterior intercostal arteries arrive from behind. Veins drain mainly to the axillary and internal thoracic veins. Lymph drainage matters most clinically, because breast cancer spreads along it. About three-quarters of the lymph, especially from the lateral quadrants, passes to the axillary nodes, which surgeons group into three levels around pectoralis minor. Most of the rest drains to the parasternal (internal mammary) nodes along the internal thoracic vessels. Injecting a tracer beside a tumour identifies the first, or sentinel, node it drains to, which can be examined instead of clearing the whole axilla.

    Innervation

    Sensation from the skin of the breast is carried by the anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of the fourth, fifth and sixth intercostal nerves, with the supraclavicular nerves supplying the skin of the upper part. The nipple and areola lie in the fourth thoracic dermatome and are supplied chiefly by the lateral cutaneous branch of the fourth intercostal nerve, so nipple sensation can be reduced or lost if that branch is divided during breast surgery. These nerves also carry sympathetic fibres to the blood vessels and to the smooth muscle of the nipple; the gland itself has no motor supply, because milk secretion is controlled by hormones. Suckling stimulates sensory endings in the nipple and the signal travels to the hypothalamus, releasing prolactin and oxytocin. Surgery in the axilla can injure the intercostobrachial nerve, leaving numbness on the inner side of the upper arm.

    Function and hormonal control

    The purpose of the breast is lactation. During pregnancy, oestrogen, progesterone and placental hormones make the ducts branch and the alveoli multiply, and prolactin from the anterior pituitary rises steadily, but the high sex hormone levels block its action so that little milk is made before birth. Once the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply and prolactin switches on full milk secretion within a few days; the first milk is colostrum, thick and rich in protein and antibodies. Suckling keeps the system going: input from the nipple sustains prolactin release and triggers oxytocin from the posterior pituitary, which contracts the myoepithelial cells around the alveoli and squeezes milk into the ducts, the let-down reflex. Supply follows demand, and the high prolactin of frequent breastfeeding often delays the return of ovulation and periods, though ovulation can return before the first period, so breastfeeding only prevents pregnancy reliably under the strict conditions of the lactational amenorrhoea method: fully breastfeeding, no periods yet returned and the baby under six months.

    Changes across life

    Before puberty, and in men throughout life, the breast is a small system of rudimentary ducts beneath the nipple that hormonal change or some medicines can enlarge (gynaecomastia). At puberty oestrogen grows the ducts and lays down fat, and progesterone develops the lobules once cycles become ovulatory; the lobules swell before each period, causing fullness and tenderness. Pregnancy enlarges the breast, and after the menopause glandular tissue gives way to fat. In trans women, breast growth usually begins about three to six months after starting oestrogen and continues over about two to three years, usually to a smaller size than in cisgender women. Trans women and non-binary people who have taken feminising hormones for several years are eligible for breast screening: those registered as female with a GP are invited in the same way as anyone else on the register, while those registered as male are not invited automatically and can ask their GP or local screening service to arrange it. Trans men who have not had chest surgery are eligible on the same basis and may also need to ask.

    Clinical relevance

    Breast cancer is the commonest cancer in women worldwide. It usually starts in the cells lining the ducts or lobules and can spread along the lymphatics to the axillary nodes, so a new lump, dimpled skin, a newly inverted nipple, blood-stained discharge or a lump in the armpit should be assessed promptly; a breast clinic combines examination, imaging and a needle biopsy. In England the NHS breast screening programme invites women, and anyone else registered female with a GP who has breast tissue, from the age of 50 up to their 71st birthday for mammography every three years. Most lumps are benign: fibroadenomas, cysts and fibrocystic change. A blocked duct during breastfeeding can lead to mastitis and, untreated, to an abscess. Breast cancer also occurs in men, though rarely. Chest masculinisation removes most but not all of the breast tissue, so a new lump still needs assessment, and augmentation implants sit over or under pectoralis major.

    The reproductive in brief

    The reproductive system makes gametes and sex hormones and carries, delivers and feeds a pregnancy. On the female side the ovaries release an egg each cycle and make oestrogen and progesterone; the fallopian tubes carry the egg to the uterus, whose lining is shed each month as a period or supports a pregnancy; the cervix, vagina and vulva form the birth canal and external genitalia; and the breasts make milk. On the male side the testes produce sperm and testosterone, the epididymis and deferent ducts store and carry sperm, the seminal vesicles and prostate add most of the seminal fluid, and the erectile bodies of the penis deliver it. BodyParts3D, the dataset behind the 3D atlas, was built from one adult male, so only the male organs are modelled in 3D; the female organs, the breasts and the surgically constructed anatomy of gender-affirming surgery (neovagina, neophallus) have full written anatomy pages that show the modelled pelvis and chest around them.
