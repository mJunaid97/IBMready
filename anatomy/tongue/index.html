    Tongue Anatomy

    The tongue is a muscular organ filling the floor of the mouth and extending back into the pharynx. Its muscles shape food into a bolus for swallowing and make it the most important articulator of speech, while its upper surface is studded with papillae that carry the taste buds and give it its rough texture.

    Location and relations

    The tongue occupies the oral cavity, resting against the hard and soft palate when the mouth is closed and surrounded by the lower teeth and gums. Its front two-thirds, the oral part, is free and mobile; the back third, the pharyngeal part or base, faces backwards into the oropharynx and lies in front of the epiglottis, with small pockets, the valleculae, between them where a swallowed pill or fish bone may lodge. On its under-surface a fold of mucous membrane, the lingual frenulum, tethers the tongue to the floor of the mouth; when this fold is unusually short or tight (tongue-tie) it can limit movement. The tongue is anchored below to the hyoid bone, in front to the mandible and above and behind to the styloid process of the skull and the soft palate, and these fixed points give its extrinsic muscles their leverage. The palatine tonsils lie at the sides of its base.

    Muscles

    The tongue is almost entirely muscle, arranged in two groups. The extrinsic muscles arise from bone outside the tongue and move it as a whole: they pull it from side to side, poke it out and draw it back, which lets it manipulate food for chewing and shape it into a bolus. The genioglossus, a fan-shaped muscle running back from the inside of the chin, forms most of the tongue's bulk and pulls it forward, which is why lifting the jaw forward in an unconscious person keeps the tongue off the back of the throat. The hyoglossus, from the hyoid bone, retracts and depresses the tongue, aiding swallowing. The intrinsic muscles lie entirely within the tongue and run lengthwise, across and vertically; by shortening different sets the tongue can curl, flatten, narrow or point. This combination of coarse movement and fine shaping produces the rapid sequence of positions needed for speech.

    Surface, papillae and taste buds

    The upper surface of the tongue is covered by four kinds of papillae. Filiform papillae are the most numerous; they carry touch receptors that help the tongue move food around, have no taste buds and give the tongue its rough surface. Fungiform papillae, scattered mainly over the front two-thirds, each hold a few taste buds and also sense pressure and temperature. Foliate papillae form folds along the sides towards the back, and the large circumvallate papillae, which contain the most taste buds, stand in a V-shaped row near the back margin. Taste buds occur in the fungiform, foliate and circumvallate papillae but never in the filiform ones, and their taste cells are replaced roughly every two weeks. Lingual glands beneath the surface secrete mucus and a watery fluid containing lingual lipase, an enzyme with a minor part in fat digestion that becomes active only in the stomach.

    Nerves and blood supply

    Taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue is carried by the chorda tympani, a branch of the facial nerve that passes through the middle ear on its way to the brainstem; it can be damaged during middle ear surgery, operations on the mandible and tongue resections, leaving taste on the front of the tongue lost or altered. Taste from the back third travels in the glossopharyngeal nerve, and injury to it impairs taste there. General sensation from the front of the tongue is carried by a branch of the trigeminal nerve and from the back by the glossopharyngeal nerve. Nearly all the tongue's muscles are driven by the hypoglossal nerve, the twelfth cranial nerve, from the medulla; if it is damaged on one side the protruded tongue deviates towards the weak side. The tongue is richly vascular, supplied on each side by the lingual artery, so injuries bleed freely and heal quickly.

    Development

    The tongue forms early, at about the fourth week of development, when paired lateral lingual swellings and a midline swelling, the tuberculum impar, appear on the first pharyngeal arch. These enlarge and merge to form the front two-thirds of the tongue, while the back third develops from tissue of the later pharyngeal arches, which is why the two parts have different nerve supplies: trigeminal and facial for the front, glossopharyngeal for the back. The muscles migrate in from elsewhere, bringing the hypoglossal nerve with them. The line of union between the two parts persists as a V-shaped groove, the sulcus terminalis, just behind the row of circumvallate papillae. Variations are common and usually harmless; the lingual frenulum may be short or attached far forward, producing tongue-tie, which is only treated if it interferes with feeding or speech.

    Clinical relevance

    In babies, tongue-tie can make breastfeeding or bottle-feeding difficult; if it causes no problems treatment is not usually needed. Glossitis is a swollen, inflamed tongue that may become smooth, pale or bright red and sore; it is often a sign of another condition, and swelling that affects breathing is an emergency. The tongue is also a common site of mouth cancer. The NHS lists the warning symptoms as a mouth ulcer lasting more than three weeks, a red or white patch inside the mouth and a lump inside the mouth or on the lip; white patches on the tongue may be an early sign, and numbness or difficulty moving the tongue, chewing or swallowing can also occur. Early cancer can look like an ordinary ulcer, so any persistent change is examined by a GP or dentist and referred for specialist assessment; found early, a complete cure is possible in most cases.

    The digestive in brief

    The digestive tract is a nine-metre tube from mouth to anus. Food is chewed, swallowed down the oesophagus, churned with acid in the stomach, digested and absorbed along the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), and its residue concentrated in the colon. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas pour bile and enzymes into the duodenum. The model includes the tongue, salivary glands and gums, the stomach and intestines in many segments, the liver as eight hepatovenous segments with its bile ducts, the gallbladder, the pancreas and the mesenteries that suspend the gut.
