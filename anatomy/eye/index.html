    Eye Anatomy

    Also known as: eyes, eyeball, vision

    The eye is a fluid-filled globe set deep in the bony orbit, with only about a sixth of its surface exposed to the air. Its clear front window, the cornea, and the adjustable lens behind it focus light onto the retina, a sheet of nervous tissue at the back of the eye that converts the image into electrical signals carried by the optic nerve to the brain.

    Location, orbit and eyelids

    The eyeball sits in the orbit, a cone-shaped bony socket that shields all but about one-sixth of its surface. The exposed front is covered by the eyelids, which protect the eye, spread tears across its surface with each blink and limit how much light enters. Each lid is stiffened by a tarsal plate, and the upper lid is lifted by the levator muscle helped by a small smooth muscle, the superior tarsal (Müller) muscle, which is routinely involved in surgery for a drooping lid (ptosis). The lacrimal gland in the upper outer corner of the orbit produces the watery part of the tear film. Tears flow to the inner corner, collect in the lacrimal lake and pass through tiny canaliculi into the lacrimal sac and down the nasolacrimal duct into the nose, which is why crying makes the nose run. Blinking, driven by muscles supplied by cranial nerves III and VII, is the main pump for this drainage.

    The three layers of the eyeball

    The wall of the eyeball has three coats. The outer fibrous tunic is the tough white sclera, continuous in front with the transparent cornea, which covers the anterior tip of the eye and lets light in; the cornea has five layers (epithelium, Bowman's membrane, stroma, Descemet's membrane and endothelium) and no blood vessels, which keeps it clear. The middle vascular tunic consists of the choroid, a pigmented, blood-rich layer lining the back of the eye; the ciliary body, a band just behind the corneoscleral junction (limbus) containing the ciliary muscle and ciliary processes; and the iris, the coloured smooth-muscle diaphragm whose central opening is the pupil. The innermost neural tunic is the retina, the nervous tissue that detects light. In front of the lens lies watery aqueous humour; the large cavity behind it holds the jelly-like vitreous body, which keeps the globe's shape.

    Focusing light: cornea, lens and iris

    Light passes first through the dome-shaped cornea, which bends it strongly; most of the eye's focusing power comes from this air-cornea interface. The pupil then regulates how much light continues inward: the iris constricts it in bright light and dilates it in dim light. Behind the pupil the lens works with the cornea to bring the image to a sharp focus on the retina. The lens is held in place by suspensory ligaments (zonule fibres) attached to the ciliary body, and when the ciliary muscle contracts or relaxes it changes the shape of the lens and so its focal distance, a process called accommodation that lets objects at different distances stay sharp. If the optical power of the cornea and lens does not match the length of the eye the image is blurred; in short-sightedness (myopia), for example, the image is focused in front of the retina rather than on it.

    The retina and the optic nerve

    When light reaches the retina, specialised photoreceptor cells turn it into electrical signals. Rods work in low light; cones work in daylight, provide colour and fine detail, and are concentrated at the fovea, the small central pit used for reading and recognising faces. The signals pass through layers of retinal nerve cells and leave the eye in the optic nerve, which carries them to the brain, where they become the images we see. The point where the nerve leaves is the optic disc, which has no photoreceptors and forms the blind spot. The inner retina is supplied by the central retinal artery, a branch of the ophthalmic artery; the outer retina and optic nerve head by the choroidal circulation (short posterior ciliary arteries); and the ciliary body by the long posterior and anterior ciliary arteries. These vessels can be seen through the pupil with an ophthalmoscope, making the eye the one place where small arteries and veins can be inspected directly.

    Muscles and nerves of the eye

    Three groups of muscles serve the eye. The six extraocular muscles turn the eyeball: the medial rectus pulls it inwards (adduction) and works in opposition to the lateral rectus, which pulls it outwards, while the superior and inferior recti and the two oblique muscles move it up, down and in rotation. The superior oblique is unusual in that its tendon passes through a fibrous pulley, the trochlea, before attaching to the eye, so it turns the eye down and out and rotates it inwards. The intraocular muscles, the iris sphincter and dilator and the ciliary muscle, control the size of the pupil and accommodation and are driven by the autonomic nervous system. The eyelid protractors and retractors open and close the lids. Sensation from the cornea is exquisitely sharp, which is why a speck of dust is so painful and why the blink reflex protects the eye so quickly.

    Clinical relevance and investigation

    Each layer has its own disorders: corneal scratches and infections, clouding of the lens (cataract), raised pressure damaging the optic nerve (glaucoma), and retinal disease from diabetes, high blood pressure or age-related degeneration. Visual acuity is measured by reading progressively smaller letters on a chart, and refraction uses a series of lenses to find the correction that makes them sharpest. A slit lamp, a microscope with a thin beam of light, shows the cornea, iris and lens. Dilating drops allow the fundus (retina, vessels and optic nerve head) to be viewed with an ophthalmoscope, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) images the retinal layers in cross-section. Tonometry measures the eye pressure. Visual field testing maps side vision, and electrical tests (ERG and VEP) record the responses of the photoreceptors and optic nerves.

    The sensory organs in brief

    The eye focuses light through the cornea and lens onto the retina, whose signals travel along the optic nerve to the brain. The model separates the cornea, sclera, choroid, iris, lens, vitreous body and retina, plus the lacrimal apparatus that produces and drains tears and the tarsal plates of the eyelids. The external ear collects sound towards the tympanic membrane; the middle and inner ear are not modelled in this release.
