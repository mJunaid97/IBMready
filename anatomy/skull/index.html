    Skull Anatomy

    Also known as: cranium, head bones, jaw

    The skull is formed by 22 bones in two groups, the brain case and the face. The brain case surrounds and protects the brain, while the facial bones give the face its structure and form the upper and lower jaws. Only the mandible, or lower jaw, is movable; the other bones are immobile and united into a single unit by fibrous joints called sutures. The 3D model shows each bone separately and also includes the hyoid bone, which is not part of the skull.

    Brain case and facial bones

    The skull has two regions: the neurocranium, or brain case, which protects the brain, and the viscerocranium, which forms the face. The brain case is made up of eight bones: the paired parietal and temporal bones and the single frontal, occipital, sphenoid and ethmoid bones. The face has fourteen bones: the paired maxilla, palatine, zygomatic, nasal and lacrimal bones and inferior nasal conchae, and the single vomer and mandible. The ethmoid is counted among the cranial bones but also contributes to the formation of facial structures. The hyoid bone is an independent bone that does not contact any other bone and so is not part of the skull.

    Sutures

    Sutures are fibrous joints in which connective tissue binds adjacent skull bones together. The coronal suture runs from side to side across the skull and joins the frontal bone to the right and left parietal bones. The sagittal suture extends backwards from the coronal suture, running along the midline at the top of the skull, and joins the two parietal bones. The lambdoid suture joins the occipital bone to the right and left parietal and temporal bones. The squamous suture unites the squamous part of the temporal bone with the parietal bone. Skull sutures are functionally classed as synarthroses, which are essentially immobile joints, although some allow slight movement.

    Mandible and jaw joint

    The mandible, or lower jaw, is the only movable bone of the skull. The mandible has a horizontal body and a vertical ramus on each side, and the outer margin where they join is the angle. The coronoid process on the ramus gives attachment to one of the biting muscles, while the condylar process is topped by the oval condyle. The condyle meets the mandibular fossa and articular tubercle of the temporal bone at the temporomandibular joint, where an articular disc lies between the bones. The joint allows the mandible to be lowered and raised, which opens and closes the mouth, to move forwards and backwards, and to move from side to side. The masseter, the main muscle used for chewing, arises from the zygomatic arch and raises the mandible to close the mouth.

    Cranial floor and openings

    The floor of the cranial cavity forms three fossae that become deeper from front to back. The anterior fossa is the shallowest and extends from the frontal bone to the lesser wings of the sphenoid. The middle fossa lies behind it, and its floor holds the optic canal, superior orbital fissure, foramen rotundum, foramen ovale and foramen spinosum. The posterior fossa is the deepest, extending to the occipital bone, and contains the foramen magnum, internal acoustic meatus, jugular foramina and hypoglossal canals. The skull base has many openings through which nerves and blood vessels pass. The foramen magnum is the largest of the cranial foramina, and its contents include the brainstem and the vertebral arteries. The optic canal transmits the optic nerve and the ophthalmic artery.

    Orbits, nose and sinuses

    Seven bones contribute to the walls of each orbit, or eye socket, which houses the eyeball and the muscles that move it: the frontal, zygomatic, maxillary, palatine, ethmoid, lacrimal and sphenoid bones. The nasal septum divides the nasal cavity into halves and is formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, which makes up its upper portion, together with the vomer and septal cartilage. The paranasal sinuses sit within the frontal, maxillary, sphenoid and ethmoid bones: the frontal sinus is the most anterior, the maxillary sinuses, below the orbits, are the largest, and the sphenoid sinus is the most posterior. The ethmoid bone contains the ethmoid air cells, small air spaces between the orbit and the upper nasal cavity.

    Growth and changes with age

    At birth, large areas of dense connective tissue called fontanelles lie between the bones of the brain case. The fontanelles provide flexibility to the skull during birth, allowing it to change shape as it passes through the birth canal. After birth the fontanelles allow for continued growth of the skull, and they decrease in size and eventually disappear. The flat bones of the brain case form by intramembranous ossification, in which mesenchyme cells differentiate directly into bone-producing cells, whereas the floor of the brain case forms by endochondral ossification, in which a cartilage model is gradually converted into bone. Late in life the sagittal, coronal and lambdoid sutures begin to ossify and fuse, a change called synostosis, or bony fusion.

    Clinical relevance

    At the pterion, where the frontal, parietal, temporal and sphenoid bones meet just above the ear, a blow to the side of the skull can tear the middle meningeal artery, a branch of the maxillary artery, and cause an epidural haematoma. Arterial bleeding into the epidural space accumulates quickly and does not cross suture lines. Temporomandibular joint disorder can arise from arthritis, wearing of the articular cartilage, muscle fatigue from overuse or grinding of the teeth, damage to the articular disc or a jaw injury. The NHS lists pain around the jaw, ear and temple, and clicking, popping or grinding sounds when the jaw moves, among the symptoms of temporomandibular disorder.

    Head injuries and skull fractures

    Most head injuries are not serious, but the NHS says it is important to get medical help for any symptoms. The NHS lists a head wound with something inside it, a dent to the head and clear fluid coming from the ears or nose among the signs for which it advises calling 999. For a severe head injury, a CT scan produces a detailed image of the inside of the head that shows whether there is any bleeding, bruising or swelling in the brain. Skull X-rays are not carried out any more because they do not provide information on brain damage. Most skull fractures heal by themselves, particularly simple linear fractures, although healing can take many months.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
