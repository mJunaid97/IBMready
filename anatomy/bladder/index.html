    Bladder Anatomy

    Also known as: urinary bladder, urethra

    The urinary bladder is a hollow, muscular organ in the pelvis that collects urine from the ureters. To empty it, the brain signals the sphincters to relax and then signals the muscular bladder wall to tighten, squeezing urine through the urethra. While the bladder fills, the sphincter muscles stay contracted to keep urine in. The urethra arises from the base of the bladder and carries urine out of the body.

    Location and relations

    In adults the bladder lies in the lesser pelvis when it is empty, just behind the pubic symphysis, and extends into the abdominal cavity when it is full. It is described as having four parts: the apex or dome, the body, the fundus, and the neck. Peritoneum covers the superior surface and part of the posterior surface of the bladder. Where a prostate is present, the rectum lies behind the bladder and the prostate lies immediately below it. Where a vagina is present, its anterior wall lies behind the bladder. The muscles of the pelvic diaphragm support the bladder from below.

    Wall, lining and trigone

    The bladder wall is made up of an inner urothelial lining, the lamina propria beneath it, the detrusor muscle (the muscularis propria) and an outer serosa or adventitia. The urothelium has more layers when the bladder is relaxed and reorganises into fewer layers as the bladder fills, without structural damage. The detrusor is made of crisscrossing bands of smooth muscle, and the whole organ is highly stretchable. On the inner surface the trigone is a triangular area of the bladder floor whose corners are the two ureteric openings and the opening of the urethra. It is the most sensitive region of the bladder for stretch receptors.

    Urethra and sphincters

    The urethra begins at the trigone area at the base of the bladder. The male urethra passes through the prostate gland just below the bladder and also carries semen. There are two urethral sphincters, an internal one and an external one, which differ in tissue type and control. The internal sphincter is smooth muscle at the bladder neck, continuous with the detrusor, and works involuntarily; in the male urethra it also helps stop semen flowing back into the bladder during ejaculation. The external sphincter is skeletal muscle under voluntary control and is supplied by the pudendal nerve. Urinary incontinence is commonly linked to problems with the urethral sphincters.

    Blood supply, nerves and lymph

    The superior and inferior vesical arteries provide the blood supply to the bladder, with the superior vesical artery, a branch of the internal iliac artery, supplying the upper part. Where a vagina is present, the vaginal artery replaces the inferior vesical artery. Venous blood drains through the vesical venous plexus into the internal iliac vein, and lymph drains mostly to the external iliac nodes. The bladder receives parasympathetic, sympathetic and somatic nerve fibres, and the parasympathetic fibres arise from the sacral spinal nerves and form the pelvic splanchnic nerves. Sympathetic activity, through the hypogastric nerves, suppresses contraction of the detrusor, whereas parasympathetic activity contracts the detrusor and relaxes the internal sphincter. Sensory information from the upper bladder wall follows the sympathetic pathway, whereas that from the lower part follows the parasympathetic nerves.

    Storage and voiding

    The bladder acts as a reservoir for urine. Urination alternates between a storage phase, when urine collects, and a voiding phase, when the bladder empties. During storage the detrusor, the muscle of the bladder wall, stays relaxed as the bladder fills, while the sphincter muscles stay contracted to keep urine in. Sympathetic tone to the bladder, carried by the hypogastric nerves, keeps the detrusor relaxed, and somatic stimulation through the pudendal nerve keeps the external sphincter closed. Voiding begins with increased sensory input from the detrusor, and higher brain centres start voluntary voiding. In the brainstem, the pontine micturition centre, also known as Barrington's nucleus, is the hub of the voiding reflex. The detrusor then contracts, the sphincters relax and urine flows out through the urethra.

    Infection and bladder pain

    Cystitis is a bladder infection. It often gets better by itself, but may sometimes be treated with antibiotics. Symptoms include pain or burning when passing urine, needing to go more often and urgently, dark, cloudy or strong-smelling urine, and pain low in the tummy. The shorter female urethra contributes to the higher incidence of bladder infections in females. The NHS says pain in the back, a high temperature or feeling sick can point to a kidney infection, which needs prompt treatment. The NHS advises seeing a GP for cystitis symptoms in children and in people with a prostate. Interstitial cystitis, also called bladder pain syndrome, is a chronic, or long-lasting, condition in which there is pain in the bladder. The pain tends to intensify as urine accumulates and to ease once the bladder is emptied, and severity varies from mild pelvic pressure or tenderness to intense bladder pain.

    Incontinence and bladder cancer

    The NHS describes several types of urinary incontinence. In stress incontinence urine leaks out at times when the bladder is under pressure, for example when coughing or laughing. Urge incontinence is a sudden and very intense need to pass urine that cannot be delayed, and it is usually linked to overactivity of the detrusor muscle, known as an overactive bladder. Almost all bladder cancers are urothelial carcinomas, which start in the urothelial cells that line the bladder and other urinary organs. Blood in the urine is the most common symptom of bladder cancer; it may look pink, red or dark brown, may come and go, or may be found only on a laboratory test. It can also have other causes, such as urinary tract infections or kidney or bladder stones, but the NHS advises having blood in the urine checked by a GP even when there are no other symptoms.

    How the bladder is investigated

    Cystoscopy lets a clinician look inside the bladder. A thin tube with a camera is gently passed into the urethra and moved through it into the bladder. In a flexible cystoscopy, local anaesthetic gel numbs the urethra and saline is run into the bladder to inflate it, which makes it easier to see inside. At cystoscopy the examiner inspects the bladder floor and trigone and records the number and position of the ureteric openings. For suspected bladder cancer the NCI says a doctor may order urine tests first and that cystoscopy is the most useful diagnostic test.

    The urinary in brief

    The two kidneys filter about 180 litres of plasma a day, returning almost all of it to the blood and excreting the rest as urine. Urine drains through the ureters into the bladder, a muscular reservoir that empties through the urethra. The kidneys also regulate blood pressure, red cell production and vitamin D activation.
