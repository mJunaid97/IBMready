    Adrenal Glands Anatomy

    Also known as: adrenal, adrenal gland, suprarenal

    The adrenal glands are a pair of small, triangular endocrine glands, one lying on the upper surface of each kidney. Each has an outer cortex that makes steroid hormones such as cortisol and aldosterone, and an inner medulla that makes adrenaline and noradrenaline for the fight-or-flight response. Together they help the body respond to stress and regulate salt and water balance.

    Location and relations

    Each adrenal gland is a small, triangular organ that sits on the upper surface of a kidney, within the renal (Gerota's) fascia. The glands are also known as the suprarenal glands. The two glands differ in shape: the right gland is pyramidal, whereas the left is crescentic (crescent-shaped). The right gland lies below the liver, behind the inferior vena cava and in front of the diaphragm.

    The two layers of each gland have different embryonic origins: the cortex is derived from mesoderm, whereas the medulla is derived from neural crest cells.

    Cortex and medulla

    Each gland has an outer cortex of glandular tissue and an inner medulla of nervous tissue, all surrounded by a connective tissue capsule. The cortex is organised into three zones, and each zone makes a different class of steroid hormone from cholesterol. The outermost zona glomerulosa makes mineralocorticoids, mainly aldosterone, which affect body minerals, especially sodium and potassium. The middle zona fasciculata makes glucocorticoids, chiefly cortisol, which raise blood glucose levels. The innermost zona reticularis makes androgens, a class of steroid sex hormones. The medulla at the core of the gland makes the amine hormones adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline (norepinephrine), and adrenaline is made in greater quantities.

    Blood supply and nerves

    The adrenal glands are among the most well-perfused organs in the body. Arterial blood reaches each gland from several sources: the superior suprarenal arteries (branches of the inferior phrenic arteries), the middle suprarenal arteries (from the abdominal aorta) and the inferior suprarenal arteries (from the renal arteries). Each gland is often drained by a single adrenal vein, although variations are common, particularly on the left. The left adrenal vein drains into the left renal vein, whereas the right adrenal vein drains directly into the inferior vena cava. The medulla receives preganglionic sympathetic nerve fibres, which release acetylcholine to stimulate the release of adrenaline and noradrenaline.

    Hormones and their control

    The medulla drives the short-term fight-or-flight response: adrenaline, together with noradrenaline, prepares the body for action and is released in response to sympathetic nervous system signals. The cortex drives the long-term response, through a pathway that runs from the hypothalamus, through ACTH released from the anterior pituitary, to the release of cortisol from the cortex.

    Cortisol regulates a wide range of processes, including metabolism and the immune response, and has an important role in helping the body respond to stress. Cortisol has a negative feedback loop that acts on the hypothalamus, anterior pituitary and adrenal gland to inhibit the release of corticotrophin-releasing hormone (CRH), ACTH and cortisol. Aldosterone's main role is to regulate salt and water in the body, which affects blood pressure. The renin-angiotensin system also drives aldosterone release, because angiotensin II stimulates the zona glomerulosa to make aldosterone.

    Clinical relevance: underactive adrenal glands

    Adrenal problems can arise when the glands are overactive or underactive. In Addison's disease, also called primary adrenal insufficiency, the glands are underactive and the cortex fails to supply cortisol, aldosterone and sex steroids. In developed countries, autoimmune disease is the usual cause: the immune system targets the outer layer of the adrenal gland, which disrupts production of cortisol and aldosterone. Early symptoms can resemble depression or flu. Common symptoms include fatigue, muscle weakness, loss of appetite and weight loss, and people with Addison's disease may also have darkening of the skin. An adrenal crisis is the most serious complication of adrenal insufficiency and is a medical emergency.

    Clinical relevance: excess hormones and tumours

    Cushing's syndrome is caused by too much cortisol in the body. Long-term use of high-dose steroid medicine is the most common cause, and tumours of the pituitary or adrenal glands, usually non-cancerous, can also cause it; when the pituitary is responsible it is called Cushing's disease. Features include more body fat on the neck, upper back, chest and tummy, a red, round face, easy bruising, stretch marks and muscle weakness.

    In primary hyperaldosteronism, the adrenal glands make too much aldosterone, and blood pressure can become very high. A non-cancerous tumour in one adrenal gland, called an aldosterone-producing adenoma, is a common cause. Cancer can also arise in the gland: for adrenocortical carcinoma, surgery to remove the adrenal gland (adrenalectomy) is often used. Phaeochromocytoma is a rare tumour of adrenal gland tissue that releases too much adrenaline and noradrenaline, and most people with it have attacks of symptoms such as headaches, palpitations, sweating and high blood pressure.

    How the adrenal glands are investigated

    Because the symptoms of Addison's disease come on slowly, they may be overlooked or confused with other illnesses. In suspected Addison's disease, blood tests measure the levels of sodium, potassium and cortisol. The ACTH stimulation test is the test used most often to diagnose adrenal insufficiency, and blood tests can also detect antibodies in autoimmune Addison's disease. For suspected Cushing's syndrome, cortisol can be checked in blood, urine or saliva, and a high result may lead to referral to an endocrinologist (a hormone specialist), who may arrange further tests on the pituitary and adrenal glands, such as blood tests and scans. For Cushing's syndrome, diagnosis is based on the medical history, a physical examination and laboratory tests.

    The endocrine in brief

    Endocrine glands release hormones into the blood to regulate metabolism, growth, stress responses and reproduction. The pituitary, hanging from the base of the brain, controls most other glands. The adrenal glands cap the kidneys, producing cortisol, aldosterone and adrenaline. The pineal body, which sets the sleep-wake rhythm, is modelled with the brain; the thyroid gland is not included in this release.
