    Oesophagus Anatomy

    Also known as: esophagus, gullet

    The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries swallowed food and drink from the pharynx in the neck, down through the chest behind the heart and great vessels, and through the diaphragm to the stomach. Rhythmic waves of muscle contraction push each mouthful along, and a sphincter at each end stops food and acid travelling the wrong way.

    Course and relations

    The oesophagus is divided into cervical, thoracic and abdominal parts. It begins in the neck at the lower border of the cricoid cartilage, as a continuation of the pharynx, and lies behind the trachea and in front of the vertebral column. The thoracic part extends from the suprasternal notch to the diaphragm: in the superior mediastinum it still occupies the space between the vertebral column and the trachea, then it passes behind the arch of the aorta and the left main bronchus into the posterior mediastinum, where it lies behind the left atrium of the heart, separated only by the pericardium, before swinging forwards and to the left to reach the oesophageal hiatus. The short abdominal part runs from the hiatus to the cardia, where it joins the stomach.

    Structure and sphincters

    The oesophagus has a mucosa, submucosa, muscular coat and outer connective tissue layer, with no serous covering in the chest. The mucosa is a tough, pale pink lining of flat squamous cells suited to the friction of swallowed food, and its glands secrete lubricating mucus. The muscular coat has inner circular and outer longitudinal layers; the upper third is mainly skeletal muscle, the middle third a mixture of skeletal and smooth muscle, and the lower third smooth muscle only. At the top, the upper oesophageal sphincter, a ring of skeletal muscle continuous with the lowest pharyngeal constrictor, regulates the movement of food from the pharynx into the oesophagus. At the bottom, the lower oesophageal sphincter, a thickening of smooth muscle reinforced by the right crus of the diaphragm, lets food pass into the stomach in one direction only.

    Blood supply, nerves and lymph

    Each part of the oesophagus is supplied by nearby vessels: branches of the inferior thyroid arteries in the neck, oesophageal branches from the thoracic aorta and bronchial arteries in the chest, and oesophageal branches of the left gastric vessels, which accompany it through the diaphragmatic hiatus, in the abdomen. Venous blood drains in the same segmental fashion, and at the lower end the veins draining to the left gastric vein connect with those draining upwards to the azygos system; when pressure in the liver circulation rises, as in cirrhosis, this connection distends into oesophageal varices that can bleed severely. The anterior and posterior vagal trunks (formed mainly from the left and right vagus nerves) lie on the lower oesophagus and pass with it through the hiatus; the vagus coordinates peristalsis and relaxes the lower sphincter, while sympathetic fibres run with the arteries.

    Function: swallowing and peristalsis

    Swallowing, or deglutition, is a three-stage process. In the voluntary phase the tongue pushes the chewed bolus back from the mouth into the oropharynx. In the pharyngeal phase, a reflex, the soft palate rises to close off the nose, the larynx is pulled upwards, the epiglottis folds over the glottis to protect the airway, and the constrictor muscles of the pharynx contract in sequence, forcing the bolus through the relaxed upper sphincter into the oesophagus. This starts the oesophageal phase: rhythmic waves of peristalsis, beginning in the upper oesophagus, propel the bolus towards the stomach, so that food moves on even when a person is lying down. As the wave approaches, the lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes to admit the bolus and then closes again. The oesophagus digests nothing itself; its job is transport.

    Clinical relevance

    Weakness of the lower sphincter or a hiatus hernia lets stomach acid reflux into the oesophagus, producing heartburn. Long-standing reflux can change the lining of the lower oesophagus from flat, pale pink squamous cells to taller red columnar cells like those of the stomach, a condition called Barrett's oesophagus; it is more common in men and older people and carries a small yearly risk of turning into cancer, so people with Barrett's are offered surveillance gastroscopies with biopsies. Oesophageal cancer typically presents with food feeling stuck when eating, bringing food back up, or new indigestion or heartburn that persists for weeks; because these symptoms are easily confused with less serious conditions it is often found late, and NHS advice is that a persistent swallowing problem should never be ignored.

    How the oesophagus is investigated

    The main test is gastroscopy (upper gastrointestinal endoscopy): an endoscopist passes a thin flexible tube with a camera in its tip through the mouth and down the oesophagus into the stomach, inspects the lining and, if necessary, takes small tissue samples (biopsies) for examination under a microscope. It also allows treatment such as stretching a narrowing. A less invasive screening test for Barrett's, the Cytosponge, uses a capsule on a string that is swallowed and then withdrawn, collecting cells from the lining as it passes; a positive result is usually followed by gastroscopy to confirm the diagnosis. A barium swallow is an X-ray examination taken while the patient swallows a contrast liquid, outlining the oesophagus and showing a narrowing, pouch or hernia. Manometry and acid monitoring assess the muscle, and CT and PET-CT stage cancer.

    The digestive in brief

    The digestive tract is a nine-metre tube from mouth to anus. Food is chewed, swallowed down the oesophagus, churned with acid in the stomach, digested and absorbed along the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), and its residue concentrated in the colon. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas pour bile and enzymes into the duodenum. The model includes the tongue, salivary glands and gums, the stomach and intestines in many segments, the liver as eight hepatovenous segments with its bile ducts, the gallbladder, the pancreas and the mesenteries that suspend the gut.
