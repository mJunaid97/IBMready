    Vulva & Clitoris Anatomy

    Also known as: clitoris, labia, external genitalia, vestibule, mons pubis

    The vulva is the external genitalia: the mons pubis, the labia majora and minora, the clitoris, the vestibule into which the urethra and vagina open, and the glands around them, resting on the front of the perineum between the thighs. Its centrepiece, the clitoris, is a densely innervated erectile organ homologous with the penis, most of which lies beneath the surface.

    Blood supplyInternal pudendal artery, with external pudendal branches from the femoral artery to the mons and front of the labia
    Nerve supplyPudendal nerve (S2-S4), with ilioinguinal and genitofemoral branches to the mons and anterior labia
    LymphSuperficial inguinal nodes in the groin
    DevelopmentFrom the same genital tubercle, folds and swellings that form the penis and scrotum

    Location and relations

    The vulva occupies the front of the perineum, the diamond-shaped region between the thighs bounded by the pubic symphysis in front, the ischial tuberosities at the sides and the coccyx behind; the urogenital triangle in front holds the vulva, the anal triangle behind holds the anal canal and its external sphincter. The mons pubis is the fat pad over the symphysis, and the labia extend back from it to the perineal body, the fibromuscular knot between the vaginal opening and the anus into which the pelvic floor and perineal muscles insert. Beneath the skin lie the crura of the clitoris on the ischiopubic rami and the vestibular bulbs beside the vaginal opening, covered by the ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles. The short female urethra, about 4 cm long, opens into the vestibule just in front of the vagina, one reason urinary tract infections are common in women.

    Structure

    The labia majora are two hair-bearing folds of skin and fat, meeting in front at the mons and behind near the perineal body. Within them lie the labia minora, thin hairless folds rich in sebaceous glands, vessels and nerve endings; in front each splits to form the prepuce (hood) over the glans of the clitoris and the frenulum beneath it, and behind they join at the fourchette. The clitoris has a glans, a short body, two crura along the ischiopubic rami and two vestibular bulbs flanking the vaginal opening, all erectile tissue that swells with blood during arousal; only the glans is visible, and most of the organ lies beneath the surface. The vestibule between the labia minora holds the urethral meatus, the vaginal opening, the ducts of Skene's (paraurethral) glands beside the urethra and the openings of Bartholin's (greater vestibular) glands on either side of the lower part of the vaginal opening.

    Blood supply and lymph

    Most of the vulva is supplied by the internal pudendal artery, a branch of the internal iliac that reaches the perineum by passing round the ischial spine and gives the perineal and posterior labial arteries, the artery of the bulb and the deep and dorsal arteries of the clitoris. The mons and the front of the labia receive external pudendal branches from the femoral artery. Venous blood returns through the internal pudendal veins and, from the clitoris, through the deep dorsal vein into the venous plexus around the bladder. The vestibular bulbs are so vascular that a straddle injury or a difficult birth can produce a large vulval haematoma. Lymph drains to the superficial inguinal nodes in the groin and then to the deep inguinal and external iliac nodes, which is why vulval cancer is staged by assessing the groin nodes and a vulval infection causes tender groin glands.

    Innervation

    The pudendal nerve (S2-S4) supplies most of the vulva. It gives perineal branches carrying the posterior labial nerves to the labia and vestibule, and the dorsal nerve of the clitoris, which makes the glans densely innervated and the most sensitive part of the genitalia. The mons and the front of the labia majora are supplied by the ilioinguinal nerve and the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (L1-L2), the back of the labia by the perineal branch of the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve. Autonomic fibres from the uterovaginal plexus reach the erectile tissue through the cavernous nerves and produce engorgement during arousal. A pudendal nerve block at the ischial spine anaesthetises the vulva and lower vagina for an instrumental birth or the repair of a tear, and in phalloplasty the dorsal nerve of the clitoris is joined to the nerve of the flap to give the neophallus sensation.

    Function and development

    The vulva protects the openings of the urethra and vagina and carries most of the sensation of the female genitalia. In arousal the clitoris and bulbs fill with blood, the labia swell and darken and the Bartholin's glands secrete mucus; the clitoris has no part in urination or reproduction, and its role is sensation and arousal. In the embryo the external genitalia of both sexes begin as the same genital tubercle, urogenital folds and labioscrotal swellings: without androgens the tubercle becomes the clitoris, the folds the labia minora and the swellings the labia majora, whereas testosterone and dihydrotestosterone turn the same tissues into the penis, penile urethra and scrotum, and the Bartholin's and Skene's glands correspond to the bulbourethral glands and prostate. These homologies underlie gender-affirming surgery: vaginoplasty fashions the glans of the penis into a neoclitoris, and metoidioplasty releases the enlarged clitoris to form a small phallus.

    Changes across life

    In childhood the labia majora are thin and hairless and the labia minora small. At puberty adrenal androgens bring pubic hair, and oestrogen deposits fat in the mons and labia majora, enlarges and darkens the labia minora and activates the glands. Pregnancy increases the blood supply, and vaginal birth stretches the vulva and perineum, where tears and episiotomies heal. After the menopause the loss of oestrogen thins the skin of the labia and vestibule, reduces labial fat and pubic hair, and causes dryness, itching and soreness: the genitourinary syndrome of menopause, which vaginal oestrogen relieves. Testosterone therapy in trans men and non-binary people enlarges the clitoris over months, typically to a few centimetres, a change that metoidioplasty makes use of. In trans women and some non-binary people, vaginoplasty constructs the labia from scrotal and penile skin and a neoclitoris from the glans of the penis, keeping its nerves and vessels.

    Clinical relevance

    A blocked Bartholin's gland forms a cyst beside the lower part of the vaginal opening; a small one often causes no symptoms, while a larger one feels uncomfortable, and an infected gland forms an abscess that is very painful and may need draining. Lichen sclerosus, a chronic inflammatory skin condition, causes itch, white thinned skin and scarring; it is treated with steroid ointment and is kept under review, because a small number of people with it go on to develop vulval cancer, which is rare and presents as a persistent lump, ulcer, itch or bleeding needing examination. Vulvodynia is persistent vulval pain with no visible cause; thrush, herpes and genital warts affect the vulval skin and are managed in sexual health clinics. Female genital mutilation, the cutting or removal of vulval tissue for non-medical reasons, is illegal in the UK and causes pain, scarring, urinary and menstrual problems, difficult childbirth and lasting psychological harm; specialist clinics offer support and deinfibulation where the opening has been narrowed. Wide variation in the size and shape of the labia is normal.

    The reproductive in brief

    The reproductive system makes gametes and sex hormones and carries, delivers and feeds a pregnancy. On the female side the ovaries release an egg each cycle and make oestrogen and progesterone; the fallopian tubes carry the egg to the uterus, whose lining is shed each month as a period or supports a pregnancy; the cervix, vagina and vulva form the birth canal and external genitalia; and the breasts make milk. On the male side the testes produce sperm and testosterone, the epididymis and deferent ducts store and carry sperm, the seminal vesicles and prostate add most of the seminal fluid, and the erectile bodies of the penis deliver it. BodyParts3D, the dataset behind the 3D atlas, was built from one adult male, so this system holds the male organs. The female organs and the breasts are modelled from the NIH Human Reference Atlas and fitted into the same pelvis and chest as the Female reproductive layer, and the surgically constructed anatomy of gender-affirming surgery (neovagina, neophallus) is drawn schematically in the Gender-affirming surgery layer; both are hidden by default and switch on from the systems list.
