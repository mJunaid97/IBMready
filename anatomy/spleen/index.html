    Spleen Anatomy

    Also known as: splenic

    The spleen is a soft, highly vascular organ in the left upper abdomen, tucked beneath the diaphragm behind the stomach and protected by the lower ribs. It is the largest lymphoid organ in the body: its red pulp filters the blood and removes old or damaged red cells, while its white pulp houses the lymphocytes that mount adaptive immune responses to blood-borne antigens.

    Location and relations

    The spleen lies in the left hypochondrium, high under the dome of the left diaphragm, with its long axis following the line of the tenth rib. Its smooth diaphragmatic surface is convex and faces upwards and backwards towards the diaphragm, which separates it from the pleura, the lung and the ninth, tenth and eleventh ribs. The visceral surface is moulded by its neighbours: the fundus of the stomach in front, the left kidney behind, the splenic flexure of the colon below, and the tail of the pancreas, which reaches the hilum. Because it is entirely hidden by the ribs, a normal spleen cannot be felt; it only becomes palpable below the left costal margin once it has enlarged considerably, and it then moves downwards and medially with breathing.

    Ligaments and hilum

    The spleen is covered by peritoneum except at the hilum, a fissure on the visceral surface that transmits the splenic vessels and nerves and provides attachment for two peritoneal folds. The gastrosplenic ligament runs forward to the greater curvature of the stomach and carries the short gastric and left gastro-epiploic vessels, while the splenorenal ligament runs backwards to the left kidney and contains the splenic artery and vein together with the tail of the pancreas. These ligaments suspend the spleen within the greater sac and form the left boundary of the lesser sac behind the stomach. A wandering or displaced spleen can result from laxity of these supporting ligaments, allowing the organ to drift on a long vascular pedicle that may twist and cut off its blood supply.

    Blood supply and innervation

    The splenic artery, the largest branch of the coeliac trunk, takes a tortuous course laterally across the superior aspect of the pancreas, enters the splenorenal ligament and divides into several branches near the hilum, each supplying a distinct region of the organ; these segments have little overlap, which is why partial removal of the spleen is possible. The splenic vein emerges from the hilum, passes behind the pancreas and joins the superior mesenteric vein to form the portal vein, so splenic blood reaches the liver before the general circulation. Lymph drains along the splenic vessels to nodes at the hilum and along the pancreas, then to the coeliac nodes. The nerve supply is sympathetic, from the coeliac plexus, travelling with the artery and controlling the tone of the capsule and vessels.

    Structure and function

    Beneath a thin capsule the spleen is divided by trabeculae into splenic nodules, each containing red pulp and white pulp. Red pulp consists mostly of red blood cells held in a network of reticular fibres with fixed and free macrophages and all the other cell types found in blood, including some lymphocytes. White pulp resembles the lymphoid follicles of a lymph node: it surrounds a central arteriole and consists of germinal centres of dividing B cells surrounded by T cells and accessory cells. The red pulp mainly filters the blood using relatively non-specific immune cells, removing injured and old red blood cells and recycling their iron, while the white pulp is where adaptive T-cell and B-cell responses to blood-borne antigens take place. The spleen also acts as a reservoir of platelets and monocytes.

    Variation

    Accessory spleens, small nodules of splenic tissue separate from the main organ, are a common finding and most often lie at the splenic hilum where the splenic vessels enter and leave; they may also be found in the gastrosplenic ligament, the tail of the pancreas or the greater omentum. They matter clinically because an accessory spleen left behind at splenectomy can continue to destroy blood cells in conditions where the operation was done to stop that, and because on imaging a nodule near the pancreatic tail can be mistaken for a tumour. The notches on the superior border of the spleen are remnants of the lobulated form it has in the embryo, when it develops from mesenchyme in the dorsal mesogastrium. The number of hilar arterial branches also varies between individuals.

    Clinical relevance

    An enlarged spleen is usually a sign of another condition, such as cirrhosis, leukaemia or rheumatoid arthritis, and is managed by treating the cause rather than by removal; people with an enlarged spleen are advised to avoid contact sports because the organ is more likely to rupture. Rupture can follow blunt trauma to the left lower ribs, and because the organ is so vascular it bleeds heavily into the peritoneal cavity; a painful spleen may signal damage, rupture or enlargement. Where possible the spleen is now preserved and the bleeding controlled, because removal carries a lifelong risk that a serious infection may develop quickly. People without a spleen are offered vaccinations, ideally before the operation, and some take long-term antibiotics. The spleen is assessed by palpation, ultrasound and CT; blood films showing Howell-Jolly bodies indicate loss of splenic function.

    The lymphatic & immune in brief

    Lymphatic vessels return tissue fluid to the blood and carry it through lymph nodes where immune cells inspect it. The spleen filters blood, recycles old red cells and mounts immune responses; the thymus, large in childhood, trains T lymphocytes. This release models the spleen and the two lobes of the thymus; lymph nodes and lymphatic vessels are not included.
