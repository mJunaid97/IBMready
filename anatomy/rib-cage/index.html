    Rib Cage Anatomy

    Also known as: ribs, rib cage, chest, sternum, breastbone

    The rib cage, or thoracic cage, is the bony and cartilaginous frame of the chest: the sternum in front, twelve pairs of ribs curving round from the thoracic vertebrae behind, and the costal cartilages that join most of the ribs to the sternum. It shields the heart, lungs and great vessels, gives attachment to the muscles of breathing and of the upper limb, and, because its joints allow the ribs to swing up and out, it changes the volume of the chest with every breath.

    JointsCostovertebral and costotransverse joints behind; sternocostal and interchondral joints in front; manubriosternal and xiphisternal joints

    Location and relations

    The thoracic cage encloses the thoracic cavity and extends from the thoracic inlet, bounded by the first ribs and the top of the manubrium, down to the diaphragm, which attaches around its lower margin. In front it is the sternum, flat and palpable along its whole length; behind, the ribs articulate with the twelve thoracic vertebrae; and on each side the ribs sweep downwards and forwards, so that the front of a rib lies lower than its back. The spaces between the ribs are filled by the three layers of intercostal muscles. The cage is narrow above, where it is overlapped by the clavicles and scapulae of the shoulder girdle, and widest around the eighth and ninth ribs. Its lower border, the costal margin, is formed by the fused seventh to tenth costal cartilages and marks the edge of the abdominal wall.

    Sternum and ribs

    OpenStax describes the sternum as three parts: the manubrium at the top, which receives the clavicles and first costal cartilages; the long body; and the small xiphoid process below, cartilaginous in youth and ossifying with age. The manubrium and body meet at the sternal angle, where the second costal cartilage attaches, and ribs three to seven join the body. Each rib ends in front in a hyaline costal cartilage. Ribs one to seven are true ribs because their cartilages reach the sternum directly; ribs eight to twelve are false ribs, with ribs eight to ten joining the cartilage of the rib above and ribs eleven and twelve, the short floating ribs, ending in the muscles of the abdominal wall. A typical rib has a head with two facets, a neck, a tubercle and a curved shaft grooved along its lower inner edge by the costal groove.

    Joints, ligaments and movements

    Behind, the head of each typical rib (StatPearls specifies ribs two to nine) articulates with the bodies of two adjacent vertebrae and the disc between them at the costovertebral joint, while the tubercle meets the transverse process at the costotransverse joint; the first and lowest ribs meet a single vertebra. In front, the first costal cartilage is fused to the manubrium, the second to seventh form small synovial sternocostal joints, and the cartilages of ribs six to ten meet at interchondral joints. The manubriosternal joint is cartilaginous and allows slight hinging at the sternal angle. The rib rotates about the axis through its two posterior joints: the upper ribs swing the sternum forwards like a pump handle, deepening the chest from front to back, while the lower ribs flare outwards like a bucket handle, widening it. Elastic costal cartilages recoil in expiration.

    Blood supply and nerves

    Each intercostal space is supplied from both ends: posterior intercostal arteries from the aorta meet anterior intercostal branches of the internal thoracic artery, forming a ring round the chest. StatPearls describes the intercostal vein, artery and nerve, in that order from above downwards, lying in the costal groove along the lower edge of the rib between the internal and innermost intercostal muscles, with a smaller collateral bundle on the upper edge of the rib below. The nerve lies lowest, which is why it is readily reached in an intercostal nerve block, and StatPearls cautions that the bundle is not always tucked safely under the rib, particularly in older people and upper spaces, which matters when placing a chest drain. Each intercostal nerve, the anterior ramus of a thoracic spinal nerve, supplies the intercostal muscles and a strip of skin.

    Function

    The cage protects the heart, lungs, great vessels, oesophagus and, under its lower ribs, the liver, spleen and kidneys. It is the engine of breathing: contraction of the diaphragm lowers the floor of the thorax, and the external intercostal muscles lift the ribs through the pump-handle and bucket-handle movements described above, enlarging the chest in every direction so that air is drawn in; quiet expiration is largely elastic recoil, with the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles recruited for forced breathing and coughing. The sternum and ribs anchor the pectoral muscles, serratus anterior and the muscles of the back that move the shoulder girdle, and the ribs contain red marrow that remains active throughout life. The cartilaginous front of the cage, rather than bone, is what allows the chest to be compressed during cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

    Clinical relevance

    Ribs break or bruise after falls, blows and road collisions. The NHS describes severe chest pain that is worse on breathing in or coughing, swelling or tenderness over the affected ribs, sometimes bruising, and a crack felt or heard at the time of injury. An X-ray is often unnecessary, because fractures may not show; when imaging is done it is to look for complications such as a pneumothorax, in which air collects around the lung and causes breathlessness and pain. Worsening breathlessness, increasing chest pain, pain in the abdomen or shoulder, or coughing up blood need emergency assessment, because lower rib fractures can injure the liver or spleen and several adjacent fractures can impair breathing. CT is used in major trauma. Pain at the costochondral junctions may arise from the joints themselves, and the sternum is a site for bone marrow sampling.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
