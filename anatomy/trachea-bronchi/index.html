    Trachea and Bronchial Tree Anatomy

    Also known as: windpipe, airway, airways, bronchi

    The trachea, or windpipe, is a rigid yet flexible tube that carries air from the larynx down through the neck and upper chest to the carina, where it divides into the right and left main bronchi. From there the airways branch again and again, like an upside-down tree, so that each lobe and each bronchopulmonary segment of the lungs receives its own bronchus.

    Location and relations

    The trachea begins where the cricoid cartilage of the larynx ends, in the lower neck, and descends in the midline in front of the oesophagus, which lies against its flat membranous back wall. In the neck it is covered by the isthmus of the thyroid gland and the strap muscles, and it is here that a tracheostomy opening is made when the upper airway is blocked. Passing behind the top of the breastbone it enters the superior mediastinum, where the arch of the aorta curves over the left main bronchus and the great veins and the thymus lie in front of it. It ends at the carina, the ridge at the point of division into the two main bronchi, which in an adult lies roughly at the level of the sternal angle. Each main bronchus runs outwards and downwards to the hilum of its lung, accompanied by the pulmonary artery and veins, nerves and lymph nodes.

    Structure of the trachea and carina

    The tracheal wall is held open by a stack of C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage, their open ends facing backwards; sources count between about fifteen and twenty of them. The gap at the back is bridged by a membrane containing smooth muscle (the trachealis) that can draw the ends of each ring together and narrow the airway, for example during coughing. Between the rings the wall is fibrous and elastic, allowing the trachea to lengthen as the larynx rises in swallowing and to bend with the neck. The inner lining is a ciliated mucous membrane that traps inhaled particles and sweeps them upwards. The carina is a cartilaginous projection from the lowest tracheal ring protruding into the lumen at the junction with the bronchi; through a bronchoscope it appears as a sharp keel-like ridge, and because it is very sensitive to touch, stimulating it evokes a vigorous cough.

    Main, lobar and segmental bronchi

    The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and steeper than the left, which is why an inhaled object most often ends up in the right lung. The right main bronchus divides into three lobar branches, for the upper, middle and lower lobes; the left gives two (the upper lobe bronchus also supplies the lingula). Each lobar bronchus then divides into segmental bronchi, each supplying one bronchopulmonary segment, the largest subdivision of a lobe. There are ten segments in the right lung and eight to ten in the left; the right upper lobe, for example, has apical, posterior and anterior segments and the middle lobe lateral and medial ones. Unlike the tracheal rings, the bronchial wall is supported by irregular plates of cartilage, which make it more deformable; down the tree the cartilage decreases and smooth muscle increases, until the bronchioles have none.

    Blood supply and nerves

    The airways themselves are nourished not by the pulmonary circulation but by small bronchial arteries: the left arise directly from the thoracic aorta and the single right bronchial artery usually from the third or fourth posterior intercostal artery; they run along the bronchi into the lungs and supply their walls down to the bronchioles. Venous blood returns partly through bronchial veins to the azygos system and partly into the pulmonary veins. The trachea and bronchi are richly innervated by branches of the vagus nerve and sympathetic trunk through the pulmonary plexuses: vagal fibres constrict the smooth muscle and increase mucus secretion, sympathetic fibres relax it, and sensory vagal endings in the lining, densest at the carina and the branch points, trigger the cough reflex when touched or irritated. This is the nervous pathway that inhaled drugs for asthma exploit and that makes a bronchoscope so provocative without local anaesthetic.

    Function

    The trachea and bronchi belong to the conducting zone of the respiratory system: they move air to and from the gas-exchanging tissue of the lungs but do not exchange gases themselves. The cartilage keeps the airway open against the negative pressure of inspiration, while the smooth muscle and the branching pattern let the system adjust resistance and distribute air between segments. The ciliated lining, mucus and cough reflex together form the lungs' main defence against inhaled dust, microbes and food, carrying debris up to the larynx to be swallowed or coughed out. The membranous back wall of the trachea allows the oesophagus behind it to expand as a bolus of food passes.

    Clinical relevance and investigation

    A blockage in the trachea threatens life immediately, whereas one in a bronchus collapses only the lung beyond it. Inhaled foreign bodies are a particular hazard in young children. NHS guidance is that a child with a significant choking or inhalation history should be referred urgently to the ear, nose and throat team even if they seem well, because the examination may be entirely normal early on and this does not exclude a foreign body; the child is usually admitted for observation and probable bronchoscopy, which both confirms the diagnosis and removes the object. A total blockage of the airway requires emergency help. The airways are examined with chest X-ray, CT, which displays the whole tree, and bronchoscopy, in which a flexible or rigid telescope inspects the carina and bronchi, takes samples and retrieves objects. Post-intubation narrowing, tumours and asthma are other disorders.

    The respiratory in brief

    Air enters through the nose, where cartilages support the nostrils and conchae warm and moisten it, passes the larynx, which guards the airway and produces the voice, and descends the trachea. The trachea divides into the main bronchi, which branch again and again into the segmental bronchial trees modelled here, one for each bronchopulmonary segment. Gas exchange takes place in the alveoli at the end of this tree; the lung tissue itself is not a separate piece in this dataset, so the bronchial trees stand in for the lungs.
