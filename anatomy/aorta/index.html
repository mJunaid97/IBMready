    Aorta Anatomy

    Also known as: aortic

    The aorta is the largest artery in the body, a thick elastic tube about 2.5-3 cm wide that carries every drop of oxygenated blood from the left ventricle. It rises from the heart, arches over it, and descends through the chest and abdomen, giving branches to every organ before dividing into the two common iliac arteries.

    PressureAbout 120/80 mmHg at rest

    Ascending aorta

    The aorta begins at the aortic valve inside the pericardium. Its first centimetres bulge into three aortic sinuses, from two of which the right and left coronary arteries arise, so the heart muscle is the first tissue the aorta supplies. The ascending aorta runs up and to the right for about 5 cm before curving into the arch.

    Arch

    The arch passes backwards and to the left over the left main bronchus and the pulmonary trunk, at the level of the sternal angle, giving off three great branches from its upper surface: the brachiocephalic trunk (which divides into the right common carotid and right subclavian arteries), the left common carotid artery and the left subclavian artery. Baroreceptors in the arch report blood pressure to the brainstem; the left recurrent laryngeal nerve hooks under it, so an enlarged arch can cause hoarseness.

    Descending thoracic aorta

    From the arch the aorta descends on the left side of the spine, then behind the oesophagus, to pass through the diaphragm at T12. It gives posterior intercostal arteries to the chest wall, bronchial arteries to the lungs, oesophageal branches and small vessels to the pericardium and diaphragm.

    Abdominal aorta

    In the abdomen the aorta lies to the left of the inferior vena cava, in front of the lumbar spine. Three unpaired branches supply the gut: the coeliac trunk (stomach, liver, spleen, pancreas), the superior mesenteric artery (small intestine and right colon) and the inferior mesenteric artery (left colon and rectum). Paired branches go to the adrenals, kidneys and gonads, and lumbar arteries to the back wall. At L4, level with the top of the hip bones, it divides into the common iliac arteries, which supply the pelvis and legs.

    Wall structure and function

    The aortic wall has an inner intima, a thick media of elastic lamellae and smooth muscle, and an outer adventitia carrying its own tiny vessels. During systole the elastic wall stretches to accept the ejected blood; during diastole it recoils and keeps blood flowing forward, smoothing the pulse. With age the elastin fragments, the aorta stiffens and systolic pressure rises.

    Clinical relevance

    Atherosclerosis roughens the aorta and its branches; cholesterol and blood pressure drive it. An aneurysm, most often of the abdominal aorta below the kidneys, can rupture with catastrophic bleeding, which is why men over 65 are offered a screening ultrasound. Aortic dissection, a tear in the intima that lets blood split the wall, causes tearing chest pain radiating to the back and is a surgical emergency. Coarctation is a congenital narrowing near the arch. Aortic valve stenosis obstructs the outlet from the heart.

    The arteries in brief

    Arteries carry blood away from the heart under pressure. The aorta leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart giving off the vessels to the head and arms, then descends through the thorax and abdomen before dividing into the iliac arteries for the pelvis and legs. The pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs. With 642 modelled pieces, this is the largest layer in the atlas, down to the segmental arteries of the lungs, liver and kidneys and the small branches on the surface of the brain.
