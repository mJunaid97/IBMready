    Shoulder Girdle Anatomy

    Also known as: shoulder, collarbone, shoulder blade

    The shoulder (pectoral) girdle is the pair of bones, clavicle and scapula, that connects each upper limb to the trunk. Unlike the pelvic girdle it is built for mobility rather than weight-bearing: its only bony link to the axial skeleton is the small sternoclavicular joint, and the scapula otherwise floats on the back of the rib cage, held and moved by muscles. This arrangement lets the shoulder socket be positioned over a wide range so that the hand can reach almost anywhere.

    Blood supplyClavicle: periosteal supply from the suprascapular, thoracoacromial and internal thoracic arteries, with no central nutrient artery; scapula: suprascapular, dorsal scapular and circumflex scapular arteries

    Location and relations

    The clavicle lies horizontally across the root of the neck at the top of the chest, easily felt and seen along its whole length. Its medial end rests on the manubrium of the sternum beside the first costal cartilage; its flattened lateral end meets the acromion at the point of the shoulder. Beneath its middle third pass the subclavian vessels and the brachial plexus as they cross the first rib into the axilla, so the bone acts as a strut that holds the limb away from the chest and keeps these structures clear. The scapula is a flat triangular bone lying on the posterolateral chest wall over ribs two to seven, separated from them by muscle. Its medial border runs parallel to the vertebral column, its lateral angle carries the glenoid cavity that faces forwards and outwards, and its spine crosses the back surface to end in the acromion, the bony tip of the shoulder.

    Bones and their features

    The clavicle is an S-shaped long bone; it is the first bone to begin ossifying in the embryo and one of the last to finish, and StatPearls notes that it has a periosteal blood supply with no central nutrient artery. The scapula has two surfaces and three borders. The concave front surface, the subscapular fossa, faces the ribs; the back surface is divided by the spine into the smaller supraspinous and larger infraspinous fossae, housing the muscles of the same names, and the spine continues laterally into the broad acromion. From the upper border the hook-like coracoid process juts forwards beneath the clavicle, giving attachment to ligaments and muscles. The glenoid cavity is a shallow, pear-shaped socket deepened slightly by a fibrocartilage labrum, with small tubercles above and below for the long heads of biceps and triceps.

    Joints, ligaments and movements

    OpenStax describes the sternoclavicular joint as the only bony articulation between the pectoral girdle and the axial skeleton. It is a saddle-shaped synovial joint with an intra-articular disc, capsular ligaments and, most importantly, the costoclavicular ligament tying the clavicle to the first rib; it rarely dislocates, and violent force breaks the clavicle instead. The acromioclavicular joint is a plane synovial joint whose own capsular ligaments are relatively weak, so its stability comes from the coracoclavicular ligament (conoid and trapezoid parts) anchoring the coracoid process to the clavicle. Together these joints let the scapula rise and fall, protract and retract, and rotate so that the glenoid tilts upwards; raising the arm overhead draws about a third of its range from this scapular rotation, a muscular gliding on the chest wall rather than a true joint.

    Muscles, blood supply and nerves

    The scapula is slung from the trunk by trapezius, levator scapulae and the rhomboids behind, and by serratus anterior and pectoralis minor in front; serratus anterior holds the medial border against the ribs, and when its long thoracic nerve is damaged the scapula wings away from the chest. According to StatPearls, the clavicle is supplied by branches of the suprascapular, thoracoacromial and internal thoracic arteries, and the acromioclavicular joint by the suprascapular artery (from the thyrocervical trunk of the subclavian) and the thoracoacromial artery (from the axillary), draining through the suprascapular vein towards the external jugular. The scapula receives the suprascapular, dorsal scapular and circumflex scapular arteries, which anastomose around the bone. Sensory nerves to the joints come from the supraclavicular, suprascapular, lateral pectoral and axillary nerves.

    Function

    The girdle is a mobile base for the arm. Because the glenoid is shallow and the clavicle is the only bony anchor, the shoulder sacrifices stability for range: the scapula can glide round the curved chest wall to point the socket forwards, upwards or sideways, and the clavicle, pivoting at the sternoclavicular joint, keeps the shoulder a fixed distance from the midline so that the arm does not collapse onto the chest. Forces transmitted up the arm, as when pushing or falling onto an outstretched hand, pass through the glenoid and along the clavicle to the sternum, which is why that bone is the one to break. The girdle also provides the attachment and lever arms for the muscles that lift, carry and throw, and the coracoid, acromion and coracoacromial ligament form an arch over the humeral head that stops it being driven upwards.

    Clinical relevance

    The clavicle is one of the most often fractured bones, usually after a fall onto the shoulder or outstretched hand. NHS emergency department leaflets describe pain, swelling and bruising over the bone, a palpable step and a drooping shoulder; numbness or tingling in the arm is uncommon but needs prompt attention. The diagnosis is confirmed on X-ray, and most fractures heal in a sling with simple painkillers, often leaving a lump; surgery is reserved for severe injuries such as bone breaking the skin or badly displaced fragments. Bone through the skin, a changed shoulder shape, heavy bleeding or difficulty breathing need emergency assessment. Acromioclavicular dislocations follow falls onto the point of the shoulder and are graded on X-ray by how far the clavicle rides up as the coracoclavicular ligament tears. Scapular fractures are rare, imply major trauma and usually prompt chest CT.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
