    Shoulder Anatomy

    Also known as: shoulder girdle, shoulder joint, glenohumeral joint, rotator cuff, collarbone, shoulder blade

    The shoulder is built from the clavicle and scapula, which together form the shoulder (pectoral) girdle, and the top of the humerus, which meets the scapula at the shoulder joint. Unlike the pelvic girdle it is built for mobility rather than weight-bearing: its only bony link to the axial skeleton is the small sternoclavicular joint, and the scapula otherwise floats on the back of the rib cage, held and moved by muscles. This arrangement lets the shoulder socket be positioned over a wide range so that the hand can reach almost anywhere.

    Location and relations

    The clavicle lies horizontally across the root of the neck at the top of the chest, easily felt and seen along its whole length. Its medial end rests on the manubrium of the sternum beside the first costal cartilage; its flattened lateral end meets the acromion at the point of the shoulder. Beneath its middle third pass the subclavian vessels and the brachial plexus as they cross the first rib into the axilla, so the bone acts as a strut that holds the limb away from the chest and keeps these structures clear. The scapula is a flat triangular bone lying on the posterolateral chest wall over ribs two to seven, separated from them by muscle. Its medial border runs parallel to the vertebral column, its lateral angle carries the glenoid cavity that faces forwards and outwards, and its spine crosses the back surface to end in the acromion, the bony tip of the shoulder. The head of the humerus, the top of the upper arm bone, meets the glenoid cavity of the scapula to form the shoulder joint.

    Bones and their features

    The clavicle is an S-shaped long bone; it is the first bone to begin ossifying in the embryo and one of the last to finish, and StatPearls notes that it has a periosteal blood supply with no central nutrient artery. The scapula has two surfaces and three borders. The concave front surface, the subscapular fossa, faces the ribs; the back surface is divided by the spine into the smaller supraspinous and larger infraspinous fossae, housing the muscles of the same names, and the spine continues laterally into the broad acromion. From the upper border the hook-like coracoid process juts forwards beneath the clavicle, giving attachment to ligaments and muscles. The glenoid cavity is a shallow, pear-shaped socket deepened slightly by a fibrocartilage labrum, with small tubercles above and below for the long heads of biceps and triceps. At the top of the humerus the smooth rounded head forms the ball of the shoulder joint. The anatomical neck lies just below the head and separates it from the greater and lesser tubercles. The greater tubercle is on the outer side and the lesser tubercle at the front, and both give attachment to rotator cuff tendons. The intertubercular (bicipital) groove runs between the tubercles and holds the tendon of the long head of biceps. Below the tubercles the bone narrows at the surgical neck, where the expanded upper end meets the shaft, and fractures are common there.

    The shoulder (glenohumeral) joint

    The shoulder, or glenohumeral, joint is a ball-and-socket synovial joint in which the rounded head of the humerus fits into the glenoid cavity of the scapula. Ball-and-socket joints have the greatest range of motion of all synovial joints. This mobility comes at a cost in stability, because the glenoid is a shallow socket and the joint capsule is loose. A rim of fibrocartilage called the glenoid labrum runs around the edge of the socket, deepening it and adding stability. Thickened bands within the capsule form the glenohumeral ligaments, which are among the static stabilisers of the joint, along with the labrum, the capsule and the shape of the bones. Muscles add dynamic stability, mainly the rotator cuff; the long head of biceps arises from the top of the glenoid and labrum, and its stabilising role is debated. The subacromial bursa lies beneath the acromion and deltoid and above the supraspinatus, which it protects from rubbing against the acromion. The rotator cuff tendons and this bursa pass through the space between the head of the humerus and the arch formed by the acromion, coracoid process and coracoacromial ligament.

    The rotator cuff and deltoid

    The rotator cuff is made up of four muscles, the supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis, often remembered by the letters SITS. Their tendons blend with the joint capsule to form a cuff around the joint. Supraspinatus helps to stabilise the joint and to start abduction, lifting the arm out to the side. Infraspinatus and teres minor rotate the arm outwards, while subscapularis rotates it inwards. The suprascapular nerve supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus, the axillary nerve supplies teres minor, and the upper and lower subscapular nerves supply subscapularis. The deltoid arises from the lateral part of the clavicle, the acromion and the spine of the scapula, and attaches to the deltoid tuberosity of the humerus. It has clavicular, acromial and spinal parts, is supplied by the axillary nerve, and its middle fibres abduct the arm.

    Sternoclavicular and acromioclavicular joints, and movement

    OpenStax describes the sternoclavicular joint as the only bony articulation between the pectoral girdle and the axial skeleton. It is a saddle-shaped synovial joint with an intra-articular disc, capsular ligaments and, most importantly, the costoclavicular ligament tying the clavicle to the first rib; it rarely dislocates, and violent force breaks the clavicle instead. The acromioclavicular joint is a plane synovial joint whose own capsular ligaments are relatively weak, so its stability comes from the coracoclavicular ligament (conoid and trapezoid parts) anchoring the coracoid process to the clavicle. Together these joints let the scapula rise and fall, protract and retract, and rotate so that the glenoid tilts upwards; raising the arm overhead draws about a third of its range from this scapular rotation, a muscular gliding on the chest wall rather than a true joint. The deltoid abducts the arm; its front fibres help flex it and its back fibres help extend it.

    Muscles, blood supply and nerves

    The scapula is slung from the trunk by trapezius, levator scapulae and the rhomboids behind, and by serratus anterior and pectoralis minor in front; serratus anterior holds the medial border against the ribs, and when its long thoracic nerve is damaged the scapula wings away from the chest. According to StatPearls, the clavicle is supplied by branches of the suprascapular, thoracoacromial and internal thoracic arteries, and the acromioclavicular joint by the suprascapular artery (from the thyrocervical trunk of the subclavian) and the thoracoacromial artery (from the axillary), draining through the suprascapular vein towards the external jugular. The scapula receives the suprascapular, dorsal scapular and circumflex scapular arteries, which anastomose around the bone. Sensory nerves to the joints come from the supraclavicular, suprascapular, lateral pectoral and axillary nerves. The axillary nerve comes from the posterior cord of the brachial plexus and passes through the quadrangular space beside the surgical neck of the humerus, together with the posterior circumflex humeral artery. The suprascapular nerve comes from the upper trunk of the brachial plexus and passes through the suprascapular notch. The anterior and posterior circumflex humeral arteries arise from the axillary artery and together supply the shoulder joint, while the suprascapular artery supplies supraspinatus and infraspinatus.

    Function

    The girdle is a mobile base for the arm. Because the glenoid is shallow and the clavicle is the only bony anchor, the shoulder sacrifices stability for range: the scapula can glide round the curved chest wall to point the socket forwards, upwards or sideways, and the clavicle, pivoting at the sternoclavicular joint, keeps the shoulder a fixed distance from the midline so that the arm does not collapse onto the chest. Forces transmitted up the arm, as when pushing or falling onto an outstretched hand, pass through the glenoid and along the clavicle to the sternum, which is why that bone is the one to break. The girdle also provides the attachment and lever arms for the muscles that lift, carry and throw, and the coracoid, acromion and coracoacromial ligament form an arch over the humeral head that stops it being driven upwards. The rotator cuff muscles stabilise the joint while the deltoid lifts the arm.

    Dislocation and fractures near the shoulder

    A dislocated shoulder means the upper arm bone has come out of its socket. The NHS advises going to A&E if the arm cannot be moved, the shoulder looks out of place or has changed shape, or it is painful, bruised or swollen. Treatment is usually a reduction in hospital, in which a clinician moves the bone back into place, followed by a sling. The axillary nerve is the nerve most often injured in shoulder dislocation, causing weak lifting of the arm to the side and loss of feeling over the outer upper arm. Most such nerve injuries recover without surgery, but numbness, pins and needles, or arm movement that is not improving should be checked: ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111, or go to A&E if it follows a new injury. A fracture of the proximal humerus, the top of the upper arm bone, causes pain, swelling and difficulty moving the arm, and bruising can spread to the chest, elbow and forearm. Many of these fractures do not need surgery, and a sling is the usual first step. A suspected fracture, especially with severe pain, needs assessment in A&E; if the arm or hand is also numb or tingling, has pins and needles, or looks pale, blue or grey or feels cold, go to A&E or call 999 straight away.

    Rotator cuff problems, impingement and frozen shoulder

    Rotator cuff problems range from irritation of the tendons, called tendinopathy or tendinitis, to partial or full-thickness tears. Tears can develop gradually over time or suddenly, for example after a fall. Rotator cuff tears can cause pain and weakness when lifting the arm, and night pain is common. Most people start with non-surgical care such as physiotherapy, and surgery is considered for some tears. Sudden arm weakness after an injury needs prompt medical assessment: go to A&E if the arm cannot be moved or the shoulder looks out of shape, or ask for an urgent GP appointment or get help from NHS 111; call 999 if there is also a drooping face or slurred speech, which can be signs of a stroke. Shoulder impingement is pain that arises when the rotator cuff tendons and subacromial bursa are compressed beneath the arch above the humeral head, and it typically hurts when the arm is lifted forwards or sideways. In frozen shoulder the joint capsule tightens and becomes inflamed, causing pain and stiffness, often with night pain. The cause is often unclear, but it is linked to diabetes, thyroid disorders and previous shoulder injury or surgery. NHS 111 Wales advises seeing a GP if shoulder pain does not improve.

    Collarbone and acromioclavicular injuries

    The clavicle is one of the most often fractured bones, usually after a fall onto the shoulder or outstretched hand. NHS emergency department leaflets describe pain, swelling and bruising over the bone, a palpable step and a drooping shoulder; numbness or tingling in the arm is uncommon but needs prompt attention. The diagnosis is confirmed on X-ray, and most fractures heal in a sling with simple painkillers, often leaving a lump; surgery is reserved for severe injuries such as bone breaking the skin or badly displaced fragments. Bone through the skin, a changed shoulder shape, heavy bleeding or difficulty breathing need emergency assessment. Acromioclavicular dislocations follow falls onto the point of the shoulder and are graded on X-ray by how far the clavicle rides up as the coracoclavicular ligament tears. Scapular fractures are rare, imply major trauma and usually prompt chest CT.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
