    Uterine Ligaments Anatomy

    Also known as: broad ligament, round ligament, cardinal ligament, uterosacral ligament, suspensory ligament of ovary, pelvic ligaments

    The uterus, uterine tubes and ovaries are held in the pelvis by a set of peritoneal folds and fibromuscular bands rather than by any bony attachment. The broad ligament drapes over the uterus like a sheet, the round ligaments pull the uterus forwards, and the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments anchor the cervix to the pelvic side walls and sacrum. Together with the pelvic floor muscles they keep the pelvic organs in position; when they weaken, the organs descend and prolapse into the vagina.

    Location and relations

    The uterus lies in the true pelvis between the bladder in front and the rectum behind, and in most women it is tilted forwards (anteverted) over the bladder and bent forwards on itself (anteflexed). Peritoneum covers its upper surface and runs off it on either side as the broad ligament, which reaches the lateral pelvic wall. In front, the peritoneum dips between the bladder and uterus to form the shallow vesico-uterine pouch; behind, it drops much lower between the uterus and rectum to form the recto-uterine pouch. Beneath the peritoneum, the condensed pelvic fascia around the cervix (the parametrium) thickens into the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments, which are the true load-bearing supports of the cervix and upper vagina.

    The broad ligament and its parts

    The broad ligament is a double sheet of peritoneum draped over the uterus and uterine tubes, connecting them to the pelvic side walls and floor. Its largest part, the mesometrium, hangs from the body of the uterus; the mesosalpinx is the narrow upper fold enclosing the uterine tube; and the mesovarium is the short fold that suspends the ovary from its posterior surface. Between the two peritoneal layers run the uterine tube, the round and ovarian ligaments, the uterine and ovarian vessels, lymphatics, nerves and, near the cervix, the ureter. StatPearls describes the broad ligament as giving only secondary support: it keeps the organs in their relative positions but does not itself hold the uterus up.

    Round, ovarian and suspensory ligaments

    The round ligament is a cord of fibromuscular tissue that leaves the uterus at the cornu, just in front of the uterine tube, runs forwards in the broad ligament, passes through the inguinal canal and fades into the labium majus. It is a remnant of the fetal gubernaculum and helps to keep the uterus anteverted and anteflexed; it carries small vessels, lymphatics and nerves. The ovarian ligament is the other half of the same embryological cord, running from the ovary to the lateral angle of the uterus behind the tube. The suspensory ligament of the ovary is not a ligament in the fibrous sense but a fold of peritoneum that conveys the ovarian artery, vein, lymphatics and nerves from the lateral abdominal wall to the ovary, continuous with the mesovarium.

    Cardinal and uterosacral ligaments

    The paired cardinal (transverse cervical or Mackenrodt's) ligaments form the lower edge of the broad ligament. Each is a thickening of parametrium and pelvic fascia extending from the cervix and vaginal fornix medially to the lateral pelvic wall, and it provides lateral support for the cervix. The uterine artery travels within the cardinal ligament on its way from the internal iliac artery to the uterus, crossing above the ureter close to the cervix, which is why the ureter is at risk during hysterectomy. The uterosacral ligaments run from the back of the cervix, beneath the peritoneum, to the front of the sacrum on each side of the rectum, resisting forward and downward displacement. StatPearls describes the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments together as the apical support of the uterus and vaginal vault.

    Function

    The ligaments share the work of support with the levator ani muscles of the pelvic floor, on which the pelvic organs normally rest. The cardinal-uterosacral complex suspends the cervix and upper vagina over the levator plate; the round ligaments steer the uterus forwards so that intra-abdominal pressure presses it onto the bladder rather than down the vagina; and the broad ligament keeps the tubes and ovaries in a position from which a released egg can be caught by the fimbriae. The fibromuscular ligaments are not rigid: they stretch during pregnancy as the uterus rises out of the pelvis, and the round ligaments in particular can be a source of sharp lower abdominal pain in later pregnancy as they are pulled taut.

    Clinical relevance

    Weakening of the pelvic floor muscles and supporting ligaments allows the uterus, bladder or bowel to bulge into the vagina: pelvic organ prolapse. The NHS lists pregnancy and childbirth (particularly a large baby, an assisted delivery or a long labour), being overweight, chronic constipation or coughing, previous pelvic surgery such as hysterectomy, the menopause and a family history as factors that make it more likely. Women describe heaviness or something coming down, often eased by lying flat, and may have bladder or bowel symptoms. Diagnosis is clinical: the NHS describes an examination that feels the pelvis for lumps and uses a speculum to look at the vaginal walls, which also grades the prolapse. Surgery for apical prolapse often uses the uterosacral or sacrospinous ligaments to re-suspend the vaginal vault, and the round ligament is a common source of pain in pregnancy.

    The female reproductive in brief

    This layer brings the female body into an atlas whose reference model is male. Its pieces come from the united female model of the Human Reference Atlas (HRA), the reference built by the HuBMAP Consortium and the NIH from the Visible Human Female, scaled and placed so that the pubic symphysis, sacral promontory and hip bones of the female pelvis coincide with those of the BodyParts3D pelvis; the breasts sit on the reference chest wall at the level of the fourth to fifth rib. The uterus lies behind and above the bladder and in front of the rectum, tilted forward; the cervix opens into the top of the vagina, which runs down and back to the perineum; the uterine tubes (fallopian tubes) arch from the upper corners of the uterus out over the ovaries, which lie against the side walls of the pelvis; and the broad, round, cardinal and uterosacral ligaments and the suspensory and ovarian ligaments hold everything in place. The female bladder from the same model, with its trigone and neck, replaces the male bladder while the layer is shown, and a short schematic urethra runs from its neck down the front of the vagina to the vestibule (the HRA model has no urethra mesh). Each breast is shown as its body (fat and gland), the lobes of the mammary gland, the lactiferous ducts and sinuses, the suspensory ligaments, the areola with its tubercles and the nipple. Two things to know when reading it. The Visible Human Female was a woman of 59, and the whole layer is scaled to the male pelvis, so the uterus and ovaries are those of a woman past the menopause and smaller than the textbook figures on the organ pages. And because the reference skin is male, the breasts stand outside the skin overlay when both are shown. Switching this layer on hides the male reproductive organs, bladder and urethra so the two bodies are not drawn through each other; switching it off brings them back. The Female body preset shows the whole body with the female organs. The vulva and clitoris are not part of the HRA model and remain a written page.
