    Brainstem Anatomy

    Also known as: brain stem

    The brainstem is the tapering stalk that joins the cerebrum and cerebellum to the spinal cord, made up from above downwards of the midbrain, pons and medulla oblongata. Every ascending sensory pathway and descending motor pathway passes through it, most of the cranial nerves attach to it, and its nuclei keep the body alive by driving breathing, heartbeat and blood pressure and by maintaining consciousness.

    Location and relations

    The brainstem lies in the posterior cranial fossa, resting on the sloping bone (the clivus) in front of the cerebellum, to which it is bound by the three pairs of cerebellar peduncles. At its upper end the midbrain passes through the opening in the tentorium cerebelli to join the diencephalon and cerebral hemispheres; at its lower end the medulla narrows and becomes the spinal cord at the level of the foramen magnum, the large hole in the base of the skull. The fourth ventricle lies between the back of the pons and upper medulla and the cerebellum, and the cerebral aqueduct runs through the midbrain, carrying cerebrospinal fluid down from the third ventricle. The brainstem is the only route between the brain above and the body below, so even a small lesion here can interrupt movement, sensation and the regulation of vital functions all at once.

    Midbrain, pons and medulla

    The midbrain is the smallest and most cranial segment. Its roof (tectum) carries the colliculi, which coordinate visual, auditory and somatosensory information; its base carries the cerebral peduncles. The pons is the broad bulge below, named for the bridge of transverse fibres that sweep into the middle cerebellar peduncles; it is the main connection between cerebrum and cerebellum and, with the medulla, houses centres regulating the cardiovascular and respiratory systems. The medulla oblongata is the lowest part, continuous with the spinal cord; it contains the cardiovascular and respiratory centres, the swallowing, coughing and vomiting reflexes, and the nuclei of cranial nerves IX to XII. At every level there are three layers: a dorsal tectum, a central tegmentum and a ventral basis carrying the descending tracts.

    Cranial nerves and pathways

    The cranial nerves connect through the brainstem and supply the brain with the sensory input and motor output of the head and neck, including most of the special senses: eye movement, facial sensation and expression, hearing and balance, taste, swallowing, voice and the movement of the tongue. Their nuclei are arranged in orderly columns in the tegmentum, and clinicians use the pattern of cranial nerve signs to work out exactly where in the brainstem a lesion lies. Alongside these nuclei run the major ascending and descending pathways between the spinal cord and the cerebrum. Because many of these tracts cross the midline within the brainstem, a lesion on one side typically combines cranial nerve weakness on that side of the face with weakness or numbness of the opposite arm and leg, a 'crossed' pattern that is a hallmark of brainstem disease.

    Function

    The brainstem keeps the body alive. Centres in the pons and medulla set the rate and depth of breathing and adjust heart rate and blood pressure from moment to moment in response to signals arriving from the lungs, heart and blood vessels. It relays signals between the brain and the body in both directions, and the reticular formation running through its core maintains wakefulness, so the brainstem is essential to consciousness and awareness as well as to movement. The midbrain coordinates sensory maps of the visual, auditory and somatosensory worlds and can send corrective output to motor commands, while the pons passes information between the cerebral cortex and the cerebellum so that movements are coordinated. Reflexes such as coughing, gagging and the constriction of the pupils to light are organised here, which is why they are examined when brainstem function is in doubt.

    Development

    In the embryo the neural tube enlarges at its head end into a series of vesicles. The midbrain develops from the mesencephalon, which keeps its name and changes relatively little, while the hindbrain vesicle divides into the metencephalon, which forms the pons and the cerebellum, and the myelencephalon, which forms the medulla oblongata. This shared origin explains why the pons and cerebellum are so intimately connected and why the medulla has so much in common structurally with the spinal cord below it. The cavity of the neural tube persists as the cerebral aqueduct in the midbrain and widens into the fourth ventricle behind the pons and medulla.

    Clinical relevance

    Posterior-circulation strokes, which include brainstem infarcts, make up roughly a fifth of ischaemic strokes; brainstem infarcts cause combinations of double vision, vertigo, facial weakness, difficulty swallowing and limb weakness; MRI shows them best. Brainstem death is the permanent loss of all brainstem function: the person has permanently lost the potential for consciousness and the capacity to breathe, and in the UK it is one of the two ways doctors can certify death. Two senior doctors perform two sets of tests confirming that the patient is unconscious and unresponsive, depends entirely on a ventilator, and has no brainstem reflexes such as coughing, gagging or pupils responding to light; the ventilator is briefly disconnected to confirm that rising carbon dioxide does not trigger a breath. A vegetative state is different: the brainstem still works and recovery remains possible.

    The nervous system in brief

    The nervous system gathers information, decides and commands. The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system; the cranial and spinal nerves with their ganglia form the peripheral nervous system. This model is richest in the head: the cerebral gyri and lobes, the basal ganglia, thalamus and hypothalamus, the brainstem and cerebellum, the ventricles, the optic pathway and the nerves of the orbit. Peripheral nerves of the limbs are not part of this BodyParts3D release.
