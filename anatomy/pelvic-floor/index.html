    Pelvic Floor Anatomy

    Also known as: levator ani, pelvic diaphragm, pelvic floor muscles, Kegel muscles

    The pelvic floor is a funnel-shaped sheet of skeletal muscle, mainly the levator ani and coccygeus on each side, that closes the lower opening of the bony pelvis and holds up the bladder, the rectum and, in women, the uterus and vagina. Everyone has one: it contracts to keep urine and faeces in and relaxes to let them out, stretches enormously in childbirth, and is weakened by pregnancy, ageing, straining and some operations. The muscles themselves are modelled in the atlas, so the structures shown on this page are the pelvic floor rather than its surroundings.

    Location and relations

    The pelvic floor spans the pelvic outlet, the diamond-shaped opening bounded by the pubic symphysis in front, the ischial tuberosities at the sides and the coccyx behind. It is a bowl of muscle that slopes down and forwards from the pelvic walls to the midline, so the pelvic cavity above it holds the bladder, rectum and internal genital organs while the perineum lies below. On each side the sheet arises from the back of the pubic bone, from the tendinous arch of levator ani that runs across obturator internus, and from the ischial spine, and it inserts on the coccyx and the anococcygeal ligament. The bladder base rests on its anterior part, the prostate in men or the vagina and uterus in women lie in the middle, and the rectum bends forwards over the puborectalis sling. Below it lies the fat-filled ischioanal fossa, along whose lateral wall the pudendal nerve and vessels run.

    Structure: levator ani, coccygeus and the perineum

    The pelvic diaphragm is formed by levator ani and, behind it, coccygeus. Pubococcygeus arises from the pubic bone and sweeps back to the coccyx; its most medial fibres, puborectalis, loop around the back of the anorectal junction as a U-shaped sling, and the fibres beside the urethra, vagina or prostate are named pubovaginalis or levator prostatae. Iliococcygeus arises further back from the tendinous arch and the ischial spine and forms the thinner lateral part. Coccygeus runs from the ischial spine to the lower sacrum and coccyx. The gap between the medial edges of the two levator ani muscles, the urogenital hiatus, transmits the urethra and, in women, the vagina, with the anal canal passing behind it. Beneath the front of the diaphragm the perineal membrane carries the external urethral sphincter, and the perineal body, between the anal canal and the vagina or bulb of the penis, anchors the perineal muscles and the external anal sphincter.

    Blood supply and lymph

    The pelvic floor receives its blood from branches of the internal iliac artery. The internal pudendal artery leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, passes round the ischial spine and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen to run in the pudendal canal along the lateral wall of the ischioanal fossa; its inferior rectal branches supply the anal sphincter and the lower surface of levator ani, and its perineal branches the perineal muscles and the external genitalia. The inferior gluteal artery contributes to the upper, pelvic surface. Veins accompany the arteries to the internal iliac veins, and the venous plexuses around the rectum, prostate and vagina lie directly on the muscle sheet. Lymph from the deep pelvic structures drains to the internal iliac nodes, while lymph from the perineal skin and the lower anal canal drains to the superficial inguinal nodes, which is why a perineal infection can produce tender groin glands.

    Innervation

    Levator ani is supplied on its pelvic surface by the nerve to levator ani, a direct branch of the sacral plexus from S3 and S4, and coccygeus by branches of S4 and S5. The perineal surface of puborectalis and the external anal sphincter receive fibres from the inferior rectal branch of the pudendal nerve (S2 to S4), which also supplies the external urethral sphincter and the perineal muscles and carries sensation from the perineum and the lower anal canal. Stretching of the pudendal nerve during a long or instrumental vaginal birth can leave it working poorly for months and contributes to incontinence. Damage to the sacral cord or the cauda equina removes both voluntary and reflex control and causes retention or incontinence. New difficulty passing urine or new incontinence with numbness around the genitals and inner thighs or new weakness in the legs can mean compression of the nerves at the base of the spine and needs emergency assessment straight away, at A&E or by calling 999.

    Function

    Its resting tone holds the pelvic organs up against gravity and against the rises in intra-abdominal pressure produced by coughing, sneezing, lifting and straining, and it tightens reflexly just before such efforts. The puborectalis sling pulls the anorectal junction forwards, creating an angle that helps keep faeces in the rectum; the sling relaxes and the angle opens during defecation. Contraction of the anterior fibres compresses the urethra and supports the bladder neck, so that a cough does not force urine out. The muscles contract rhythmically during orgasm, and their tone contributes to sexual sensation. In labour the levator hiatus must stretch to several times its resting size to let the head of the baby pass, and the muscles usually recover over the following weeks to months, though the stretch can leave lasting weakness. It also forms the base of the abdominal cylinder with the diaphragm and abdominal wall.

    Changes across life

    Pregnancy loads the floor and vaginal birth stretches it further: levator ani can tear from the pubic bone (levator avulsion), the anal sphincter can be torn and the pudendal nerve stretched, so leakage of urine after birth is common and usually improves with training. Chronic straining, a persistent cough, heavy lifting and obesity weaken it over years, and menopause thins the supporting tissues. Prostate removal takes away part of the urethral closure mechanism, and stress incontinence is common afterwards and usually improves over the following months with pelvic floor muscle training, although it can persist and needs assessment if it does. Training helps at any age: squeeze as if stopping the flow of urine and holding back wind, hold, relax and repeat, NICE advising at least eight contractions three times a day for at least three months. After vaginoplasty the neovagina lies within the levator ani, and pelvic floor physiotherapy to relax the muscles makes dilation easier.

    Clinical relevance

    Weakness of the pelvic floor underlies two very common problems. Stress urinary incontinence is leakage on coughing, laughing or exercise, and pelvic organ prolapse is descent of the bladder, uterus or rectum into the vagina, felt as a dragging heaviness or a bulge; both are common after childbirth and the menopause, and both are treated first with supervised pelvic floor muscle training (at least three months for incontinence and at least sixteen weeks for prolapse in NICE guidance), with pessaries, vaginal oestrogen or surgery for those who need more. Urgency incontinence, a sudden desperate need to pass urine caused by an overactive bladder muscle rather than a weak floor, is managed differently: NICE advises bladder training for at least six weeks before medicines are considered. Faecal incontinence follows sphincter injury at birth or nerve damage; obstetric anal sphincter injury is the term for third and fourth degree tears. An overactive, tight pelvic floor causes pelvic pain, painful sex and difficulty emptying the bladder or bowel, and is treated with relaxation and physiotherapy rather than strengthening. Incontinence, though common, is not a normal part of ageing and should be assessed.

    The muscles in brief

    Skeletal muscle makes up roughly 40% of body mass. Each muscle spans one or more joints, attaching to bone through tendons or flat aponeuroses; when its fibres shorten it pulls the bones together. Muscles work in groups: agonists produce a movement, antagonists control it, and stabilisers hold neighbouring joints still. The model includes the muscles of the limbs, trunk, neck, face, larynx and pharynx, and the diaphragm.
