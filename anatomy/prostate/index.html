    Prostate Anatomy

    The prostate is a walnut-shaped gland that sits at the base of the bladder and surrounds the first part of the urethra. It receives sperm and seminal vesicle fluid through the ejaculatory ducts and adds its own thin, milky, alkaline secretion to form semen. Its position against the front wall of the rectum allows it to be examined by digital rectal examination, and its growth in later life can obstruct the urethra that runs through it.

    Location and relations

    The prostate lies in the lesser pelvis immediately below the neck of the bladder, with its base fused to the bladder and its apex resting on the external urethral sphincter and the pelvic floor. The prostatic urethra passes through it from base to apex, so the gland is often described as doughnut-shaped. In front, a fat-filled retropubic space separates it from the pubic symphysis; behind, only the thin rectovesical fascia separates its posterior surface from the anterior wall of the rectum, which is why the gland can be palpated through the rectum and sampled by transrectal biopsy. On each side the levator ani muscles cradle it, and the seminal vesicles and ampullae of the vasa deferentia lie against its upper posterior surface before their ejaculatory ducts enter the gland.

    Lobes and zones

    Anatomically the prostate has traditionally been split into five lobes, but in clinical practice it is described as having two lateral lobes, right and left, separated by a shallow posterior groove, and a median lobe lying between the urethra and the ejaculatory ducts. Histologically a three-zone model is more useful. The central zone forms the base of the gland and encircles the ejaculatory ducts as they run down to open on either side of the seminal colliculus in the prostatic urethra. The peripheral zone makes up most of the glandular tissue, lies posteriorly and laterally, and is the part felt on rectal examination. The transition zone surrounds the urethra above the ejaculatory ducts and is the zone that enlarges with age. In front of the urethra the gland is largely non-glandular anterior fibromuscular stroma, continuous with the smooth muscle of the bladder neck.

    Blood supply, lymphatics and innervation

    The inferior vesical artery, a branch of the internal iliac artery, is the main supplier of the prostate, with the middle rectal and internal pudendal arteries contributing as well. Venous blood drains through the prostatic venous plexus, between the gland and its fibrous sheath, into the internal iliac veins; the plexus also communicates with the vertebral venous plexuses, a route by which prostate cancer can spread to the spine. Lymphatics drain to the internal iliac and sacral nodes. Autonomic fibres reach the gland from the inferior hypogastric plexus: sympathetic input via the hypogastric nerve drives contraction of the glandular smooth muscle at ejaculation, while parasympathetic supply comes from the pelvic splanchnic nerves through the prostatic plexus, whose cavernous branches continue forwards to the penis. Sensory fibres travel in the hypogastric and pelvic plexuses.

    Function

    The prostate is an accessory gland of the reproductive system. During ejaculation it receives sperm and seminal vesicle fluid from the ejaculatory ducts and adds its own alkaline secretion to the mixture as it passes through the prostatic urethra; the seminal vesicles and prostate together contribute most of the volume of semen, with sperm making up only a small share. The glandular tissue makes a thin, milky fluid that contains citrate, which acts as a nutrient for sperm, enzymes, and prostate-specific antigen. The prostatic enzymes help the semen first to clot and then to liquefy after ejaculation, which allows the sperm to move onwards in the female reproductive tract. The smooth muscle of the gland and bladder neck contracts at the same time, expelling the secretion and closing the bladder neck so that semen is directed forwards rather than into the bladder.

    Development and age change

    The prostate develops in fetal life as buds of epithelium growing out from the urethra into the surrounding mesenchyme, a process driven by androgens made by the fetal testes; the central zone is thought to have a different embryological origin, related to the Wolffian ducts, from the rest of the gland, which may explain why it rarely gives rise to disease. The gland remains small until puberty, when testosterone causes it to grow to its adult size. From middle age onwards the transition zone around the urethra tends to enlarge again as benign nodules form, slowly compressing the prostatic urethra, whereas the peripheral zone is the usual site of cancer. Because these two processes affect different zones, a gland can be enlarged without harbouring cancer and can harbour cancer without being enlarged.

    Clinical relevance

    Benign enlargement compresses the urethra and causes weak urine flow, hesitancy and incomplete emptying; advanced prostate cancer can produce the same symptoms, while early prostate cancer may cause no symptoms at all. Assessment begins with a digital rectal examination of the posterior surface of the gland and a blood test for prostate-specific antigen. A raised PSA has benign causes, including enlargement and prostatitis, and rectal examination alone is poor at detecting cancer, so multiparametric MRI is increasingly used before biopsy to target suspicious areas and avoid unnecessary sampling; only a biopsy, usually guided by transrectal ultrasound or MRI fusion, confirms the diagnosis. Obstruction is relieved by transurethral resection, and the closeness of the cavernous nerves to the capsule explains the risk of erectile dysfunction after radical prostatectomy.

    About

    A walnut-sized gland surrounding the first part of the urethra below the bladder.

    Function. Secretes fluid that makes up about a third of semen.

    Clinical note. Benign enlargement slows the urinary stream in older men; prostate cancer is among the commonest male cancers. The prostate is kept in vaginoplasty, so prostate care still applies to trans women.

    The reproductive in brief

    The reproductive system makes gametes and sex hormones and carries, delivers and feeds a pregnancy. On the female side the ovaries release an egg each cycle and make oestrogen and progesterone; the fallopian tubes carry the egg to the uterus, whose lining is shed each month as a period or supports a pregnancy; the cervix, vagina and vulva form the birth canal and external genitalia; and the breasts make milk. On the male side the testes produce sperm and testosterone, the epididymis and deferent ducts store and carry sperm, the seminal vesicles and prostate add most of the seminal fluid, and the erectile bodies of the penis deliver it. BodyParts3D, the dataset behind the 3D atlas, was built from one adult male, so this system holds the male organs. The female organs and the breasts are modelled from the NIH Human Reference Atlas and fitted into the same pelvis and chest as the Female reproductive layer, and the surgically constructed anatomy of gender-affirming surgery (neovagina, neophallus) is drawn schematically in the Vaginoplasty and Phalloplasty layers; all three are hidden by default and switch on from the systems list.
