    Pituitary Gland Anatomy

    Also known as: hypophysis

    The pituitary is a pea-sized gland that hangs from the base of the brain on a thin stalk and sits in a bony cup, the sella turcica, in the sphenoid bone. It has two lobes with different origins and different ways of working: the anterior lobe makes and releases hormones under orders carried from the hypothalamus in a private portal blood system, while the posterior lobe stores and releases hormones made in the hypothalamus itself. Its output controls growth, the thyroid and adrenal glands, the gonads, lactation and water balance.

    Location and relations

    The pituitary occupies the sella turcica, a saddle-shaped depression in the body of the sphenoid bone at the centre of the skull base. A fold of dura, the diaphragma sellae, roofs the fossa and is pierced by the pituitary stalk, which rises to the hypothalamus. Immediately above the diaphragm lies the optic chiasm, so a gland that enlarges upwards presses first on the crossing fibres from the nasal halves of both retinas and classically narrows the outer halves of both visual fields. On each side of the fossa is a cavernous sinus containing the internal carotid artery and the nerves to the eye muscles. Below, only a thin plate of bone separates the gland from the sphenoid air sinus, which is the route used in transsphenoidal pituitary surgery, approached through the nose.

    Lobes and origin

    The anterior lobe (adenohypophysis) is glandular tissue that develops from an upgrowth of the roof of the embryonic mouth (Rathke's pouch), whereas the posterior lobe (neurohypophysis) is nervous tissue that grows down from the floor of the developing brain and keeps its connection as the infundibulum or stalk. Between them lies a narrow pars intermedia, whose cells produce melanocyte-stimulating hormone among other products. The anterior lobe is the larger and contains several populations of hormone-secreting cells; the posterior lobe contains no secretory cell bodies at all, only the axon endings of hypothalamic neurons and their supporting cells. Remnants of Rathke's pouch may persist as small cysts in the adult gland.

    Blood supply and the portal system

    The gland is supplied by hypophyseal branches of the internal carotid arteries. The paired superior hypophyseal arteries form a primary capillary plexus in the median eminence at the top of the stalk, where hypothalamic neurons release their regulating hormones. This blood is collected into hypophyseal portal veins that run down the stalk and open into a second plexus of fenestrated capillaries among the cells of the anterior lobe, so hypothalamic signals reach the anterior pituitary undiluted and in high concentration. Small superior hypophyseal branches also reach the stalk, optic nerves and chiasm, the intermediate lobe is fed by anastomoses between the anterior and posterior capillary beds, and the posterior lobe receives the inferior hypophyseal arteries directly. Short veins drain the gland into the neighbouring dural venous sinuses and so into the systemic circulation.

    Hormones and control

    The anterior pituitary produces six main hormones in response to releasing or inhibitory signals from the hypothalamus and to feedback from the level of circulating hormones: adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), which acts on the adrenal cortex to drive cortisol release; growth hormone, stimulated by growth hormone-releasing hormone and inhibited by somatostatin; thyroid-stimulating hormone; the two gonadotrophins, luteinising hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone; and prolactin. The posterior pituitary does not make hormones. Anti-diuretic hormone (vasopressin), which controls water balance and blood pressure, and oxytocin, which drives uterine contractions in labour and milk release during breastfeeding, are synthesised in the hypothalamus, carried down the axons of the stalk and stored in the posterior lobe until nerve impulses release them into the blood.

    Function

    The pituitary is the link between the nervous system and the endocrine system. Hypothalamic neurons sense the body's state, including blood osmolality, stress, temperature, light and the levels of thyroid, adrenal and sex hormones, and translate it into pulses of releasing hormones that set the pituitary's output. Each pituitary hormone then controls a distant target: growth hormone acts on bone, muscle and the liver; TSH on the thyroid; ACTH on the adrenal cortex; LH and FSH on the ovaries and testes; prolactin on the breast; ADH on the kidney's collecting ducts; and oxytocin on the uterus and breast. Negative feedback from the target glands' hormones on both the hypothalamus and pituitary keeps each axis stable, which is why measuring a pituitary hormone together with its target hormone tells a clinician where in the axis a fault lies.

    Clinical relevance

    Most pituitary disease is caused by benign adenomas. The National Cancer Institute's PDQ summary notes that symptoms depend on whether a tumour over-produces a hormone, under-produces hormones by compressing the normal gland, or presses on neighbouring structures, causing headache, blurred or double vision, loss of the outer visual fields and drooping eyelids. Hypopituitarism, described by the Society for Endocrinology, is under-production of one or more pituitary hormones after tumours, surgery, radiotherapy, injury or inflammation. Investigation pairs each pituitary hormone with its target hormone, tests the visual fields and images the gland: MRI is the modality of choice for its soft-tissue contrast, with CT less sensitive. Sudden headache with visual loss and collapse suggests pituitary apoplexy, bleeding into or infarction of a tumour, an emergency.

    The endocrine in brief

    Endocrine glands release hormones into the blood to regulate metabolism, growth, stress responses and reproduction. The pituitary, hanging from the base of the brain, controls most other glands. The adrenal glands cap the kidneys, producing cortisol, aldosterone and adrenaline. The pineal body, which sets the sleep-wake rhythm, is modelled with the brain; the thyroid gland is not included in this release.
