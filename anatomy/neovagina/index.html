    Neovagina Anatomy

    Also known as: neovaginal canal, neoclitoris, vaginoplasty anatomy

    A neovagina is a vaginal canal and vulva constructed by vaginoplasty, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people, and occasionally where the vagina is absent from birth or lost to cancer surgery. In the commonest technique the skin of the penis and scrotum lines a canal made between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind, and part of the glans becomes the clitoris. Its skin lining needs lifelong care that differs from that of a vagina developed before birth. The 3D model is schematic: the canal, neoclitoris and labia drawn to typical dimensions in the reference pelvis, since no open scan of post-operative anatomy exists.

    PositionBetween the urethra, prostate and bladder in front and the rectum behind, passing through the levator ani
    ClitorisA reduced glans penis kept alive on its dorsal neurovascular bundle
    DepthVaries with the donor tissue available; commonly around 10 to 15 cm
    Retained and removedThe prostate and a shortened urethra remain; the testes and most of the erectile tissue are removed
    LiningKeratinised skin that does not lubricate and lacks the lactobacillus flora of a vagina
    MaintenanceRegular dilation for life to keep depth and width

    Location and relations

    The canal occupies a space that in the pelvis of a person assigned male at birth is only potential: the plane between the prostate and membranous urethra in front and the rectum behind, which the surgeon opens from the perineum and widens by parting the pubococcygeus and puborectalis of each side. Its axis runs upward and backward towards the sacrum, as a vagina developed before birth does. The bladder base lies above and in front of the upper canal, the prostate directly behind the anterior wall, and the rectum and external anal sphincter behind the posterior wall, separated from it by only a thin layer of tissue. The perineal body between the introitus and the anus is rebuilt to support the opening, and the tone of the surrounding pelvic floor affects how comfortable dilation is.

    Structure and lining

    In penile-inversion vaginoplasty the penile skin is separated from the erectile bodies as a tube, turned inside out and drawn into the canal as its lining; a graft of scrotal skin, cleared of hair beforehand, extends the depth when needed. The corpora cavernosa are largely removed, the corpus spongiosum is trimmed around the shortened urethra, and the new meatus opens in the vestibule below the clitoris. Both testes are removed (orchidectomy), which ends sperm production and the testes' output of testosterone, so fertility preservation is discussed beforehand. The glans is reduced to form the neoclitoris; scrotal skin forms the labia majora and urethral or penile skin the labia minora and vestibule. The lining is keratinised skin, not the glycogen-rich epithelium of a vagina, so it does not lubricate with arousal as a vagina does. Peritoneal techniques line the upper canal with peritoneum, intestinal vaginoplasty uses a segment of sigmoid colon that secretes mucus, and a zero-depth vulvoplasty creates the external anatomy without a canal.

    Blood supply and lymph

    The tissues keep the vessels they had as the penis and scrotum. The neoclitoris survives on the dorsal neurovascular bundle, the paired dorsal arteries (branches of the internal pudendal artery), the deep dorsal vein and the dorsal nerves, preserved together on a strip of tissue during the dissection; injury to this pedicle is the main threat to the clitoris. The inverted penile skin is nourished through its base and, once healed, by the surrounding tissue, whereas a scrotal graft has no vessels of its own and must gain capillaries from the canal wall, so packing holds it firmly in place for the first days. Lymph from the canal and vulva drains, as from the penis and scrotum, to the superficial inguinal nodes; the prostate and bladder drain to the internal iliac nodes. An intestinal segment keeps its own mesenteric vessels.

    Innervation

    Sensation comes from the nerves that supplied the penis and perineum. The dorsal nerve of the penis, a branch of the pudendal nerve (S2 to S4), travels with the dorsal vessels to the neoclitoris, so the clitoris keeps tactile and erotic sensation and the ability to orgasm is usually retained. The inverted skin of the canal shares this supply but is less sensitive, as shaft skin was. The prostate and the urethra behind the anterior wall receive autonomic fibres from the inferior hypogastric plexus, and pressure on this region during penetration adds to sexual sensation. The pudendal nerve also supplies the external anal sphincter and perineal skin, and the ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerves the front of the labia majora. Because nerve regrowth is slow, numbness, altered sensation or hypersensitivity is common in the first months and usually settles.

    Function and hormonal influence

    The neovagina serves receptive penetrative sex and, for many people, a sense of bodily congruence; it has no menstrual or reproductive function, and there is no cervix or uterus above it. Because the lining is skin it does not lubricate with arousal, and lubricant is needed for dilation and sex (services usually advise a water-based product with dilators). A skin-lined canal is not reliably colonised by the lactobacilli that keep a vagina acidic; its flora resemble those of skin and the perineum, so odour or discharge can come from retained debris, granulation tissue or overgrowth of skin and bowel organisms as well as sexually transmitted infections, and persistent discharge, bleeding or pain needs assessment. An intestinal neovagina produces mucus continuously. Oestrogen taken as gender-affirming therapy is usually continued after recovery, although some teams pause it around surgery because of the risk of venous thrombosis. The canal has no monthly cycle.

    Changes after surgery and over life

    Vaginoplasty is one part of gender-affirming care that some, not all, trans women and non-binary people choose; gender incongruence is not a mental illness, and the World Health Organization classifies it under conditions related to sexual health. The canal is most fragile in the first weeks, when the graft is taking and the lining contracting; left unopposed, a skin-lined canal narrows and shortens, so dilation begins once the packing is removed. Schedules vary between services, frequent at first and then reducing to a maintenance routine that continues for life unless regular penetrative sex maintains depth. Rinsing removes debris, and pelvic floor physiotherapy helps those who find dilation painful because the levator ani tightens. Over the years, hair can grow in the canal if the donor skin was not cleared. After orchidectomy the body makes little sex hormone of its own, so oestrogen is usually continued long term to protect bone.

    Clinical relevance

    Complications include bleeding, wound breakdown along the labia or introitus, granulation tissue, loss of depth or stenosis from insufficient dilation and, rarely, a fistula between the canal and the rectum or urethra, which presents as stool or urine in the canal. Stool or urine passing into the canal, heavy bleeding or fever needs urgent assessment by the surgical team. Urinary tract infection is a recognised complication after surgery, and the repositioned meatus can give a stream that sprays or deflects. Because the prostate is retained, benign enlargement and prostate cancer remain possible; the gland can be felt through the anterior wall, and prostate-specific antigen is interpreted knowing that oestrogen and anti-androgens lower it. There is no cervix, so cervical screening is not needed, although sexually transmitted infections can affect the canal. Breast screening follows the guidance for trans women on oestrogen. Examination needs a small speculum and lubricant, remembering that depth, axis and lining differ from those of a vagina.

    The gender-affirming surgery in brief

    No open 3D scan of anatomy after gender-affirming surgery exists, so this layer is schematic: simple solids drawn to the typical dimensions and positions described in the surgical literature, placed in the reference pelvis, and labelled so that every piece opens the same clinical pages as the rest of the atlas. It shows the results of the two main genital operations together. After penile-inversion vaginoplasty a canal about 12 to 15 cm deep lined with penile and scrotal skin runs up and back between the urethra and prostate in front and the rectum behind, a clitoris fashioned from part of the glans sits under the pubic arch, and the scrotal and shaft skin form the labia; the prostate is kept. After phalloplasty a phallus about 13 to 15 cm long built from a forearm or thigh flap hangs from the pubic region, often with a lengthened urethra running to its tip, and the labia majora are joined into a scrotum that later receives testicular implants; an erectile implant may be added later. Metoidioplasty, which releases the clitoris enlarged by testosterone instead of using a flap, is described on the neophallus page. Turning this layer on hides the male reproductive organs; show it with the skeleton, urinary and digestive systems to see the relations that matter in surgery and aftercare.
