    Larynx Anatomy

    Also known as: voice box, vocal cords

    The larynx, or voice box, is a cartilaginous segment of the airway in the front of the neck, suspended from the hyoid bone above and continuous with the trachea below. Its first job is to guard the lower airway, closing off the trachea so that food and drink cannot be inhaled; its second is phonation, the production of sound by the vocal folds.

    Location and relations

    The larynx lies in the anterior part of the neck, in the midline, at the level of the third to sixth cervical vertebrae, and is held in position by muscles and ligaments. Above, the epiglottis is attached to the hyoid bone and the larynx opens into the lower part of the pharynx, so that the airway and the food passage share the same space for a few centimetres; below, the cricoid cartilage joins the first ring of the trachea. The thyroid gland wraps around its lower part and the upper trachea, and the great vessels of the neck run on either side. The whole organ rises and falls visibly during swallowing, and the laryngeal prominence of the thyroid cartilage (the 'Adam's apple') is usually more noticeable in men. The larynx is also smaller in women, which is one reason for the higher pitch of the female voice.

    Cartilage skeleton

    The skeleton of the larynx consists of three unpaired midline cartilages and three pairs of smaller cartilages (arytenoid, corniculate and cuneiform). From above downwards the main pieces are the epiglottis, thyroid cartilage, paired arytenoids with the small corniculates on their tips, and the cricoid. The thyroid cartilage is the largest, forming the front and sides of the voice box. The cricoid below it is the only complete cartilage ring in the larynx, shaped like a signet ring, wide at the back and narrow in front. The epiglottis is a flexible flap of elastic cartilage attached to the inside of the thyroid cartilage; during swallowing it folds back over the entrance to the airway. The arytenoids, perched on the back of the cricoid plate, are the most mobile part of the framework and carry the back ends of the vocal folds. The cricothyroid joints let the thyroid cartilage tilt on the cricoid, tightening the folds.

    The glottis and vocal folds

    Inside, the larynx is divided into three regions: the supraglottis above the vocal folds, the glottis at their level, and the subglottis below, down to the lower edge of the cricoid. The glottis consists of the true vocal folds and the space between them (the rima glottidis); the vestibular folds belong to the supraglottis. The vestibular folds, or false vocal cords, are a pair of soft folds of mucous membrane lying above the true folds and help to close the airway during swallowing and straining. The true vocal folds are white, membranous folds attached by muscle to the thyroid cartilage in front and the arytenoid cartilages behind; each contains a vocal ligament, the free upper edge of the conus elasticus, with the vocalis muscle alongside it. Their pearly white colour, in contrast to the pink mucosa around them, makes them easy to identify when the larynx is viewed with an endoscope.

    Muscles and nerves

    The intrinsic muscles of the larynx move the cartilages relative to one another and so control the position, length and tension of the vocal folds. The main ones are the cricothyroid, the thyroarytenoid, the lateral and posterior cricoarytenoid muscles and the interarytenoid (transverse and oblique arytenoid) muscles. The posterior cricoarytenoid is the only muscle that opens (abducts) the folds for breathing; the others close them or adjust their tension for voice. Extrinsic muscles attached to the hyoid and sternum raise and lower the whole larynx during swallowing. Every intrinsic muscle except cricothyroid is supplied by the recurrent laryngeal nerve (a branch of the vagus), and cricothyroid by the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, which loops down into the chest before climbing back up beside the trachea to reach the larynx; this long course is why disease in the chest, including some lung cancers, can present as a hoarse voice.

    Function

    The primary function of the larynx is to protect the lower respiratory tract from the aspiration of food into the trachea. During swallowing the larynx is pulled upwards and forwards, the epiglottis folds over the entrance, and the vestibular and vocal folds close tightly, so that the bolus passes around the closed airway into the oesophagus. A cough, which clears the airway if anything does get through, depends on the same closure: the folds shut, pressure builds in the chest, and they open suddenly to release a blast of air. For phonation the vocal folds are brought together and air driven up from the lungs sets them vibrating, producing sound that is shaped into speech by the pharynx, tongue, palate and lips; during quiet breathing the folds are held apart. Pitch is adjusted by lengthening and tensing the folds, and loudness by the force of the airflow.

    Clinical relevance and investigation

    Laryngitis, in which the voice box or vocal folds become irritated or swollen, usually settles on its own within a week or two; symptoms that persist or keep recurring are checked by a GP. A hoarse voice that does not go away is the most common presenting feature of laryngeal cancer, and NHS guidance is that persistent unexplained hoarseness, especially with a lump in the neck, pain or difficulty swallowing, breathlessness, a persistent earache or unexplained weight loss, should prompt specialist referral. Reflux, vocal overuse, trauma and infection are other causes. The larynx is examined first by nasendoscopy, a thin flexible telescope passed through the nose, and, if the view is poor or an abnormality is seen, by laryngoscopy, in which a longer endoscope passed through the mouth gives a more detailed view and allows a biopsy. CT, MRI or PET-CT scans then show the extent of any cancer.

    The respiratory in brief

    Air enters through the nose, where cartilages support the nostrils and conchae warm and moisten it, passes the larynx, which guards the airway and produces the voice, and descends the trachea. The trachea divides into the main bronchi, which branch again and again into the segmental bronchial trees modelled here, one for each bronchopulmonary segment. Gas exchange takes place in the alveoli at the end of this tree; the lung tissue itself is not a separate piece in this dataset, so the bronchial trees stand in for the lungs.
