    Foot Anatomy

    Also known as: ankle, toes, tarsal bones

    The skeleton of the foot is built from twenty-six bones: seven tarsals forming the heel and midfoot, five metatarsals forming the forefoot, and fourteen phalanges in the toes. Arranged as a set of arches and bound by strong plantar ligaments, these bones carry the whole weight of the body on a small base, absorb the shock of each step and act as a rigid lever when the calf muscles push the body forwards.

    Key facts

    Bones26 per foot: 7 tarsals, 5 metatarsals and 14 phalanges (two in the big toe, three in each of the others)
    TarsalsTalus, calcaneus, navicular, cuboid and the medial, intermediate and lateral cuneiforms
    Largest boneCalcaneus, which forms the heel and receives the Achilles tendon
    Ankle jointTalus articulating with the distal tibia, the medial malleolus of the tibia and the lateral malleolus of the fibula
    ArchesMedial and lateral longitudinal arches (tarsals behind, metatarsals in front) and a transverse arch; flattening is pes planus (flat foot)
    Blood supplyDorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries, whose branches anastomose around the joints
    NervesMedial and lateral plantar nerves to the sole, deep fibular nerve to the dorsum of the tarsal joints, plantar digital nerves to the toe joints

    Location and regions

    The foot lies at right angles to the leg below the ankle, and its skeleton is described in three regions. The hindfoot is the talus and calcaneus; the midfoot is the navicular, cuboid and three cuneiforms; and the forefoot is the five metatarsals and the phalanges of the toes. The talus is the keystone: it sits in the mortise formed by the tibia and fibula at the ankle, rests on the calcaneus below and projects its rounded head forwards into the navicular, so that all the weight from the leg passes through it before being shared between the heel behind and the ball of the foot in front. No muscle attaches to the talus, and most of its surface is covered by articular cartilage. The weight-bearing points on the ground are the heel, the heads of the metatarsals and, at the side, the lateral border of the foot; the medial border is normally raised off the ground by the arch.

    Bones

    OpenStax describes the seven tarsals forming the posterior half of the foot, with the talus most superior and the calcaneus, the largest bone of the foot, beneath it forming the heel. In front of the talus the boat-shaped navicular lies medially; in front of the calcaneus the cuboid lies laterally; and the medial, intermediate and lateral cuneiforms sit in a row between the navicular and the first three metatarsals. The five metatarsals, numbered from the medial side, each have a base, shaft and head: the first is short and thick because it bears the most load, the second is the longest and is locked between the cuneiforms, and the fifth has a tuberosity on its base that is felt on the outer edge of the foot. The big toe has two phalanges and each other toe three, and two sesamoid bones under the head of the first metatarsal take the weight at push-off.

    Joints, ligaments and arches

    The talocrural (ankle) joint is a hinge for dorsiflexion and plantarflexion. Beneath it the subtalar and transverse tarsal joints allow inversion and eversion, tilting the sole to adapt to uneven ground; the tarsometatarsal and intertarsal joints allow only slight gliding, and the metatarsophalangeal and interphalangeal joints flex and extend the toes. StatPearls lists the calcaneofibular, talocalcaneal, calcaneocuboid and calcaneonavicular ligaments stabilising the calcaneus, with the plantar aponeurosis and long plantar ligament supporting the arch. OpenStax describes the longitudinal arches as tarsals behind and metatarsals in front, united by strong ligaments that resist collapse under weight; the spring ligament under the head of the talus supports the medial arch, and the plantar fascia, running from the calcaneus in five bands to the toes, acts as a tie-beam.

    Blood supply and nerves

    According to StatPearls, the foot is supplied mainly by the dorsalis pedis artery on the dorsum and the posterior tibial artery, which passes behind the medial malleolus and divides into medial and lateral plantar arteries; their branches anastomose around the joints, each joint fed by whichever reaches it. The dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial pulses are the standard checks of circulation to the foot. The plantar side of the tarsal joints is innervated by the medial and lateral plantar nerves, branches of the tibial nerve, and the dorsal side by the deep fibular nerve; the metatarsophalangeal joints depend mainly on plantar digital nerves, and the interphalangeal joints receive the medial dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial fibular nerve and the plantar interdigital nerves. The plantar fascia is supplied by the plantar nerves and small branches of the posterior tibial artery.

    Function

    The foot has two conflicting jobs: to be supple enough to mould itself to the ground and absorb impact, and stiff enough to act as a lever. It achieves both through its arches. At heel strike the arches flatten slightly and the ligaments and plantar fascia stretch, storing energy and spreading the load across the heel and metatarsal heads; as the body moves forwards and the toes extend, the plantar fascia tightens like a windlass, the arch rises and the tarsal joints lock, converting the foot into a rigid beam through which the calf muscles, pulling on the calcaneus, lift the body onto the ball of the foot. The subtalar and transverse tarsal joints let the heel and forefoot twist relative to each other so that the sole stays flat on sloping surfaces, and the short muscles of the sole fine-tune the arches with every step. The big toe provides the final push and helps with balance.

    Clinical relevance

    Fractures of the toes and metatarsals follow stubbing, crushing, twisting falls and repetitive loading (stress fractures). NHS leaflets describe pain, swelling and tenderness over the fracture, bruising, difficulty walking or taking weight and, in the toes, bleeding or discolouration under the nail. Only an X-ray can confirm a fracture, but a lesser toe in normal alignment is usually treated without one, whereas the big toe, a deformed toe or tenderness at the base of the fifth metatarsal is X-rayed to confirm and classify the injury. Reduced mobility afterwards carries a risk of a blood clot, so calf pain or breathlessness needs urgent assessment. Other disorders include pes planus, plantar fasciitis at the calcaneal attachment of the fascia, hallux valgus, calcaneal fractures and Lisfranc injuries of the tarsometatarsal joints, often missed on plain films and needing CT.

    The skeleton in brief

    The adult skeleton has 206 bones plus the cartilages that cap joints, join the ribs to the sternum and shape the nose and airway. It is organised into an axial skeleton (skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum) that protects the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, and an appendicular skeleton (the limbs with their girdles) built for movement. Bone is living tissue: it remodels throughout life in response to load, stores calcium and phosphate, and houses the marrow that makes blood cells.
