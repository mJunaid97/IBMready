    Diaphragm Anatomy

    The diaphragm is a broad, dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon that separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity and forms the floor of the chest. It is the main muscle of breathing: each time it contracts the domes flatten and air is drawn into the lungs. Its three main openings let the inferior vena cava, the oesophagus and the aorta pass between chest and abdomen.

    Location, attachments and shape

    The diaphragm closes the lower opening of the thoracic cage. Its muscle fibres arise all around the rim: from the back of the xiphoid process of the sternum in front, from the inner surfaces of the lower six ribs and their cartilages at the sides, and from the upper lumbar vertebrae behind by way of the two crura and the arcuate ligaments that arch over the muscles of the posterior abdominal wall. From this ring the fibres sweep upwards and inwards to insert into a strong central tendon on which the heart rests in its pericardium. The muscle forms two domes, the right higher than the left because the liver lies beneath it, and their level changes with breathing and posture. Above lie the lungs, pleura and heart; below lie the liver, stomach, spleen, kidneys and adrenal glands. Because the domes rise high into the chest, a wound to the lower chest can injure abdominal organs.

    The crura and the openings

    The right and left crura are the tendinous and muscular pillars arising from the front of the upper lumbar vertebrae; the right is longer and stronger. The vena caval foramen lies in the central tendon, towards the right, roughly at the level of the eighth thoracic vertebra; it transmits the inferior vena cava and some branches of the right phrenic nerve; being in tendon it stays open when the muscle contracts. The oesophageal hiatus lies in the muscular part, left of the midline within a sling of fibres from the right crus, at about the tenth thoracic vertebra; it transmits the oesophagus, both vagal trunks, oesophageal branches of the left gastric vessels and lymphatics. The aortic hiatus lies behind, between the crura in front of the twelfth thoracic vertebra, and transmits the aorta, thoracic duct and azygos vein.

    Nerve and blood supply

    The diaphragm is innervated by the two phrenic nerves, which arise from the cervical spinal cord, mainly from the third to fifth cervical roots with a contribution from the sixth. The right nerve descends lateral to the inferior vena cava to the caval opening; the left passes lateral to the pericardium. The phrenic nerves carry the motor supply and sensation from the central part and the membranes above and below it, so irritation of the diaphragm by blood, pus or an inflamed gallbladder is felt as pain at the tip of the shoulder, whose skin shares the same cervical segments; the peripheral rim is supplied by the lower intercostal nerves. Arterial blood arrives from above with the phrenic nerves and from below through the inferior phrenic arteries.

    Function

    The diaphragm is the principal muscle of quiet breathing. When it contracts, the domes descend and flatten, the vertical dimension of the chest increases and the lower ribs are lifted outwards, so the pressure in the chest falls and air is drawn in; at the same time the abdominal organs are pushed down and the abdominal wall moves out, which is why relaxed breathing is 'belly breathing'. Expiration at rest is passive: the muscle relaxes and the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall returns the domes to their resting height. Contracting with the abdominal muscles against a closed glottis it raises the pressure within the abdomen for coughing, sneezing, vomiting, defecation, lifting and childbirth. And its crural part around the oesophagus behaves as a separate muscle, tightening to prevent reflux and relaxing during swallowing and vomiting to let material pass.

    Clinical relevance and investigation

    The commonest disorder of the diaphragm is a hiatus hernia, in which part of the stomach squeezes up into the chest through the oesophageal hiatus. The hernia itself rarely causes symptoms; when symptoms occur they come from acid reflux, with heartburn, an unpleasant taste in the mouth, chest pain, difficulty swallowing, nausea or a cough and wheeze that is worse at night. Most are managed with lifestyle measures and acid-suppressing medicines; a large or troublesome hernia can be repaired by keyhole surgery, usually combined with an anti-reflux wrap. A rare congenital form in newborn babies results from a defect of the diaphragm that lets abdominal organs enter the chest. Damage to a phrenic nerve paralyses that half of the diaphragm; it is seen as a raised dome on a chest X-ray and confirmed by watching the diaphragm with ultrasound or fluoroscopy while the patient sniffs.

    About

    The dome-shaped sheet of muscle separating the chest from the abdomen.

    Function. The main muscle of breathing: contracting flattens the dome, enlarging the chest and drawing air in. Openings carry the aorta, oesophagus and inferior vena cava.

    Clinical note. Hiatus hernia lets the stomach slide through the oesophageal opening; the phrenic nerves that drive it arise from C3 to C5.

    The muscles in brief

    Skeletal muscle makes up roughly 40% of body mass. Each muscle spans one or more joints, attaching to bone through tendons or flat aponeuroses; when its fibres shorten it pulls the bones together. Muscles work in groups: agonists produce a movement, antagonists control it, and stabilisers hold neighbouring joints still. The model includes the muscles of the limbs, trunk, neck, face, larynx and pharynx, and the diaphragm.
