    Gallbladder & Bile Ducts Anatomy

    Also known as: gall bladder, bile duct, biliary

    The gallbladder is a pear-shaped sac tucked under the right lobe of the liver that stores and concentrates bile between meals. Bile made by liver cells drains through the hepatic ducts into the common hepatic duct, which joins the cystic duct from the gallbladder to form the common bile duct; this carries bile to the duodenum, where it meets the pancreatic duct at the hepatopancreatic ampulla.

    Location and relations

    The gallbladder lies in a shallow fossa on the under-surface of the right lobe of the liver, with its rounded fundus projecting just beyond the liver's inferior margin so that it touches the anterior abdominal wall near the tip of the ninth right costal cartilage. Its body is in contact with the first part of the duodenum and the transverse colon, and its neck points towards the porta hepatis, where the hepatic ducts, hepatic artery and portal vein enter and leave the liver. The extrahepatic bile ducts run within the free edge of the lesser omentum, with the common bile duct lying to the right of the hepatic artery and in front of the portal vein, before the duct passes behind the first part of the duodenum and the head of the pancreas to reach the second part of the duodenum.

    Parts and duct system

    The gallbladder has a fundus, a body and a neck. The body narrows through an asymmetric infundibulum, Hartmann's pouch, into the neck, which in most people connects to the common bile duct through a single cystic duct. Bile produced by hepatocytes passes along ductules into bile ducts that merge into the right and left hepatic ducts, then into the common hepatic duct. After leaving the gallbladder the cystic duct joins the common hepatic duct to form the common bile duct, which carries bile towards the small intestine. Near the duodenum the common bile duct meets the pancreatic duct at a shared opening, the hepatopancreatic ampulla, guarded by a sphincter of smooth muscle. The cystic duct usually joins the extrahepatic bile duct about halfway between the porta hepatis and the ampulla, most often on its medial side, although the level and side of the junction vary.

    Blood supply, innervation and Calot's triangle

    The cystic artery, usually a branch of the right hepatic artery, supplies the gallbladder and cystic duct, and it lies within Calot's triangle, the space bounded by the cystic duct laterally, the common hepatic duct medially and the inferior margin of the liver above. The cystic lymph node (the node of Lund, or Calot's node) also lies within the triangle. Venous blood from the gallbladder drains partly through small veins directly into the liver and partly through a cystic vein to the portal system. Autonomic nerves reach the gallbladder with the vessels: parasympathetic fibres from the vagus and sympathetic fibres from the coeliac plexus, with sensory fibres that explain why gallbladder pain is felt in the epigastrium and right upper quadrant and, when the diaphragm is irritated, referred to the right shoulder.

    Function

    The gallbladder's two main jobs are to store bile and to concentrate it. Between meals the sphincter at the ampulla is closed, so bile backs up the common bile duct and cystic duct into the gallbladder, whose lining reabsorbs water and ions until the bile is up to about ten times more concentrated than when it left the liver. When fatty chyme enters the duodenum, the duodenal hormone cholecystokinin triggers contraction of the gallbladder wall and relaxation of the sphincter, releasing bile alongside pancreatic enzyme secretion. Bile salts and phospholipids then break large fat globules into small droplets, a step needed for lipid digestion and absorption. Most bile salts are reabsorbed in the ileum and return to the liver to be secreted again.

    Variation

    The biliary tree is one of the most variable regions of abdominal anatomy, which matters because it is operated on so often. The cystic duct may be long or short, may run parallel to the common hepatic duct for some distance before joining it, or may enter on the right, left or posterior aspect. The cystic artery may arise from the common or left hepatic artery, may be double, or may cross in front of the common hepatic duct. Accessory hepatic ducts and a right hepatic duct draining into the cystic duct are recognised traps. Identifying Calot's triangle and dissecting it until only two structures, the cystic duct and cystic artery, are seen entering the gallbladder is the basis of the 'critical view of safety' that surgeons use to avoid injuring the bile ducts or hepatic artery during cholecystectomy.

    Clinical relevance

    Gallstones form when concentrated bile precipitates cholesterol or pigment, and most cause no symptoms. A stone obstructing the cystic duct causes biliary colic, severe pain in the upper abdomen often on the right side, and if the obstruction persists, inflammation of the gallbladder. A stone that passes into the common bile duct blocks bile flow, causing jaundice and infection, and because the duct shares its opening with the pancreatic duct it can also provoke pancreatitis. Diagnosis rests on the symptoms, right upper abdominal tenderness, blood tests and ultrasound, with MR cholangiopancreatography when duct stones are suspected. The main treatment is cholecystectomy, removal of the gallbladder, while duct stones can be cleared by endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. During surgery the common bile duct can be mistaken for the cystic duct and is at risk of transection.

    The digestive in brief

    The digestive tract is a nine-metre tube from mouth to anus. Food is chewed, swallowed down the oesophagus, churned with acid in the stomach, digested and absorbed along the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), and its residue concentrated in the colon. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas pour bile and enzymes into the duodenum. The model includes the tongue, salivary glands and gums, the stomach and intestines in many segments, the liver as eight hepatovenous segments with its bile ducts, the gallbladder, the pancreas and the mesenteries that suspend the gut.
