    Ear Anatomy

    Also known as: ears, hearing

    The ear is the organ of hearing and balance, built in three parts. The external ear gathers sound and funnels it to the eardrum; the air-filled middle ear carries the vibration across three tiny bones to the inner ear; and the fluid-filled inner ear, buried in the temporal bone, turns vibration into nerve signals in the cochlea and senses the position and movement of the head in the vestibular system.

    The external ear

    The external ear is the only part of the ear modelled in the atlas and the only part that can be seen. It consists of the auricle (pinna), a flap of elastic cartilage covered by skin whose folds and hollows are shaped to capture sound waves and funnel them inwards; the external auditory canal, a curved tube running into the temporal bone that carries sound from the pinna towards the middle ear and whose skin produces wax; and, at its inner end, the tympanic membrane or eardrum, a thin oval membrane that separates the canal from the middle ear cavity and moves when sound waves strike it. The auricle, canal and eardrum together make up the outer ear. Because the eardrum is a translucent membrane stretched across the end of a tube, a clinician can inspect it directly with an otoscope and judge from its colour, position and surface whether the middle ear behind it is healthy.

    The middle ear

    The middle ear is a small air-filled space within the temporal bone. Three tiny bones, the auditory ossicles, cross it in a chain: the malleus (hammer) is attached to the inside of the eardrum and meets the incus (anvil), which in turn meets the stapes (stirrup), whose footplate sits in the oval window of the inner ear. The chain carries the vibrations of the eardrum to the oval window and, by concentrating the movement of the large eardrum onto the small footplate, transfers sound energy efficiently from air into the fluid of the inner ear. Two small muscles attached to the ossicles tense the chain in response to loud sound, the acoustic reflex. The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the pharynx and keeps the air pressure on both sides of the eardrum equal, which is why ears 'pop' when flying and why a blocked tube during a cold leaves the ear feeling full and dull.

    The inner ear

    The inner ear, or labyrinth, is a complex system of canals hollowed out of the dense petrous part of the temporal bone (the bony labyrinth) and lined by a membranous labyrinth filled with a fluid called endolymph. It has two main parts. The cochlea is the organ of hearing, a coiled tube containing the organ of Corti, where thousands of tiny hair cells arranged in rows (three rows of outer hair cells and one row of inner hair cells) turn sound vibrations travelling through the fluid into electrical signals. The vestibular system is the organ of balance: the vestibule, containing the utricle and saccule, senses the position of the head and linear acceleration, while the three semicircular canals, set at right angles to one another, detect rotation of the head. Together they keep track of head position and movement so that posture and the direction of gaze can be maintained.

    Nerves and how hearing works

    Sound reaches the ear as pressure waves in air. The outer ear collects them, the eardrum converts them into mechanical vibration, the ossicles pass the vibration to the oval window, and the resulting waves in the cochlear fluid bend the hair cells, which signal to the cochlear fibres of the vestibulocochlear (eighth cranial) nerve. Signals from the balance organs travel in the vestibular part of the same nerve. The nerve runs through the internal auditory canal of the temporal bone to the brainstem, from where auditory signals are relayed to the cortex and perceived as sound, while vestibular signals are used by the brainstem and cerebellum to steady the eyes and body. The eardrum has its own sensory nerve supply, which is why middle ear infections are so painful, and the chorda tympani, a taste branch of the facial nerve, crosses the middle ear and can be damaged in middle ear surgery.

    Clinical relevance and investigation

    Problems arise at each level: wax or infection in the canal, fluid or infection behind the eardrum, stiffening of the ossicles, and damage to the hair cells or nerve from noise, ageing, infection or drugs. In the UK hearing tests are free on the NHS; a GP can refer a person to an audiologist. The audiologist takes a history, examines the ears for wax or infection, and performs pure tone audiometry, in which the person wears headphones and presses a button each time a tone is heard, to find the quietest sound audible at a range of pitches in each ear. Tympanometry uses a small device in the ear to measure how well the eardrum moves and whether there is fluid behind it, and speech audiometry tests the understanding of words. Results are plotted on an audiogram. Sudden hearing loss is assessed urgently.

    The sensory organs in brief

    The eye focuses light through the cornea and lens onto the retina, whose signals travel along the optic nerve to the brain. The model separates the cornea, sclera, choroid, iris, lens, vitreous body and retina, plus the lacrimal apparatus that produces and drains tears and the tarsal plates of the eyelids. The external ear collects sound towards the tympanic membrane; the middle and inner ear are not modelled in this release.
