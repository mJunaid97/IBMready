    Small Intestine Anatomy

    Also known as: bowel, gut, duodenum, jejunum, ileum

    The small intestine is the part of the digestive tract that runs from the pylorus of the stomach to the ileocaecal valve, where it meets the large intestine. It has three regions, the duodenum, jejunum and ileum, and it is the site of most chemical digestion and almost all absorption. Intestinal brush border enzymes and pancreatic enzymes do most of the chemical digestion, and bile salts emulsify lipids, which increases the surface area available to lipases.

    Location and relations

    The three regions follow one another from the stomach, with no clear dividing line between the jejunum and the ileum. The duodenum lies below the stomach, and its C-shaped curve surrounds the head of the pancreas. Its first part is intraperitoneal, whereas its second, third and fourth parts are retroperitoneal, lying behind the peritoneum. The jejunum and ileum are also intraperitoneal, and the mesentery carries their blood vessels, nerves and lymphatics. The jejunum lies mainly in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen and the ileum mainly in the right lower quadrant. The ileum ends at the ileocaecal valve, where the small intestine meets the large intestine.

    Regions

    The duodenum is the shortest part of the small intestine, and it has four parts: the bulb, the descending part, the horizontal part and the ascending part. Bile and pancreatic juice enter the second (descending) part of the duodenum through the hepatopancreatic ampulla, also called the ampulla of Vater, which forms where the common bile duct meets the pancreatic duct. The ileum, the final region, is the longest part of the small intestine. In life the whole small intestine is about three metres long, and in a cadaver it is about twice as long. The regions also differ in what they absorb: the duodenum and upper jejunum are the main sites of iron absorption, while the terminal ileum absorbs vitamin B12, which needs intrinsic factor made in the stomach.

    Wall and lining

    The wall of the small intestine has four layers: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria and serosa. Circular folds (plicae circulares), villi and microvilli together greatly increase the surface area available for absorption. The microvilli give the mucosa a fuzzy appearance called the brush border, and enzymes that finish the digestion of carbohydrates and proteins are fixed to the membranes of the microvilli. The cells of the epithelium and intestinal glands include absorptive cells, goblet cells (which secrete mucus), Paneth cells (which secrete lysozyme) and enteroendocrine cells (which release hormones). Intestinal juice is a mixture of water and mucus. The epithelial cells are renewed rapidly.

    Blood supply, nerves and lymph

    The small intestine receives arterial blood from the coeliac trunk and the superior mesenteric artery. The proximal duodenum is supplied mainly from the coeliac trunk through the gastroduodenal artery, and the distal duodenum from the superior mesenteric artery; the superior and inferior pancreaticoduodenal arteries, which arise from these two sources, anastomose. The jejunum and ileum are supplied by jejunal and ileal branches of the superior mesenteric artery, which run through the mesentery. Veins follow the arterial supply and drain into the superior mesenteric vein, which joins the splenic vein to form the portal vein. Lymph begins in the mucosa and drains to nodes along the mesentery, the arterial arcades and the superior mesenteric artery, and from there to the coeliac nodes. The lesser splanchnic presynaptic sympathetic fibres synapse in the superior mesenteric ganglia, and sympathetic fibres decrease small intestinal secretions and motility and cause vasoconstriction.

    Function: digestion and absorption

    Bile salts emulsify lipids, and the pancreas supplies lipid-digesting enzymes such as lipase. Pancreatic amylase and brush-border disaccharidases such as maltase and sucrase digest carbohydrates, and the pancreas secretes protein-digesting enzymes such as trypsin and chymotrypsin. Brush-border enzymes then complete the digestion of carbohydrates and proteins.

    Blood capillaries in the villi take up sugars and amino acids, while the lacteals take up fat. Inside the intestinal cells, fats are reassembled into triglycerides and packaged with other lipids and proteins into chylomicrons, which are too big to pass through the basement membranes of blood capillaries and so enter the large pores of the lacteals. Water absorption is thought to occur through osmotic gradients and aquaporins in the intestinal membranes. Segmentation separates chyme and then pushes it back together, mixing it and providing time for digestion and absorption.

    Clinical relevance

    Coeliac disease is an autoimmune condition in which gluten causes damage to the lining of the small bowel: the finger-like villi flatten, which reduces the surface area available for absorption and can lead to nutrient deficiencies. Crohn's disease is a long-term condition in which abnormal immune reactions cause inflammation in the digestive tract, most commonly in the small intestine and the beginning of the large intestine. Ulcers can form in the stomach or in the duodenum, and the NHS says stomach ulcers are usually caused by H. pylori infection or by taking anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs).

    Short bowel syndrome develops when the small intestine is shortened or damaged and cannot absorb enough nutrients, most often after surgery to remove part of it. Small intestine cancers include adenocarcinoma, sarcoma, carcinoid (neuroendocrine) tumours, gastrointestinal stromal tumours and lymphoma; unexplained weight loss and abdominal pain can be signs, although other conditions can cause the same signs and symptoms. Persistent symptoms should be assessed by a clinician.

    How the small intestine is investigated

    A gastroscopy checks inside the food pipe, the stomach and the top of the intestine using a thin tube with a camera, and a small sample of cells (a biopsy) may be collected. The top of the intestine is the duodenum, the first region of the small intestine. The NHS says a gastroscopy can also be used for treatment, for example to stop bleeding inside the stomach or food pipe or to remove growths. For coeliac disease, the NHS describes two main methods of diagnosis: a blood test for coeliac antibodies and a biopsy of the small intestine taken with an endoscope passed through the mouth.

    The digestive in brief

    The digestive tract is a nine-metre tube from mouth to anus. Food is chewed, swallowed down the oesophagus, churned with acid in the stomach, digested and absorbed along the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum), and its residue concentrated in the colon. The liver, gallbladder and pancreas pour bile and enzymes into the duodenum. The model includes the tongue, salivary glands and gums, the stomach and intestines in many segments, the liver as eight hepatovenous segments with its bile ducts, the gallbladder, the pancreas and the mesenteries that suspend the gut.
