    Shortness of breath

    Also known as: breathlessness, dyspnoea, dyspnea

    The sensation of not getting enough air. It arises when breathing demands more effort than expected, when the blood carries too little oxygen or too much carbon dioxide, or when the heart cannot keep up. Speed of onset is the key clue: seconds, hours, days or months.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Breathlessness that comes on suddenly or is severe at rest
    • Blue lips, confusion, or inability to speak in full sentences
    • Breathlessness with chest pain, coughing blood or a swollen leg
    • Swelling of the face or tongue, or noisy breathing (stridor)
    • Oxygen saturation below 92% on a home oximeter

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    AsthmaWheeze, cough and tightness that come and go, worse at night or with triggers.
    Chest infections and pneumoniaCough, fever and breathlessness over days.
    COPDProgressive breathlessness on exertion in smokers, with chronic cough.
    Heart failureBreathless lying flat, waking at night gasping, swollen ankles.
    Anaemia and deconditioningGradual breathlessness on effort without chest signs.
    Anxiety and hyperventilationAir hunger with tingling fingers and light-headedness.

    Less common causes

    Pulmonary embolismSudden breathlessness with pleuritic pain, fast pulse, sometimes a swollen leg.
    PneumothoraxSudden one-sided breathlessness and chest pain.
    Heart attack or arrhythmiaBreathlessness can be the main symptom, especially in women, the elderly and people with diabetes.
    Anaphylaxis or airway obstructionSwelling of lips and tongue, stridor, hives.
    Interstitial lung diseaseSlowly progressive breathlessness with a dry cough and crackles.

    Often occurs with

    Chest painCoughSwelling (oedema)FatigueDizziness

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Methotrexate Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease.
    • Montelukast Montelukast is a prescription medicine that helps prevent asthma symptoms.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.

    Guides and news

    • Altitude sickness symptoms and prevention Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.
    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • Long flights and DVT: risk and how to lower it Long journeys can raise the chance of a leg blood clot (DVT); the NHS and TravelHealthPro describe moving, drinking water and sometimes stockings.
    • Echocardiogram: types, what happens and results An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to picture the heart.
    • Panic attacks: what they are and what helps A panic attack is a rush of intense fear with physical symptoms such as a racing heartbeat.
    • Reliever and preventer inhalers explained A reliever inhaler eases symptoms when they happen, while a preventer is taken every day to reduce airway inflammation.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
    • Spirometry: the lung function test explained Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast.
    • Swollen ankles and legs: causes and red flags Swollen ankles and legs often come from sitting or standing for long periods, but can signal a clot or a heart, kidney or liver problem.
