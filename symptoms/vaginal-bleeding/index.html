    Unusual vaginal bleeding

    Also known as: abnormal vaginal bleeding, bleeding between periods, intermenstrual bleeding, bleeding after sex, postmenopausal bleeding, spotting

    Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex, after the menopause (once a year has passed since the last period), or during pregnancy. Cervical screening is offered to people without symptoms and is not the test for unusual bleeding: bleeding needs the cervix to be looked at directly. The blood may come from the lining of the uterus, the cervix, the vaginal wall or the vulva, and is occasionally confused with bleeding from the urethra or rectum. Stage of life narrows the causes. In the reproductive years hormonal and infective causes are the commonest, and a pregnancy test is part of every assessment. After the menopause even a single episode of light bleeding is treated as a warning sign: most post-menopausal bleeding turns out to have a benign cause, most often thinning of the lining of the vagina and of the uterus, but it can be the first sign of cancer of the uterine lining, and NICE guidance treats it as a reason for urgent investigation. Trans men and non-binary people with a uterus usually stop having periods on testosterone; bleeding that starts again needs the same assessment.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Heavy bleeding with dizziness, fainting, a racing pulse or breathlessness
    • Bleeding in pregnancy or when pregnancy is possible, especially with one-sided pain, shoulder-tip pain or faintness
    • Any bleeding after the menopause, even a single episode of spotting, which needs an urgent GP appointment
    • Bleeding after sex on more than one occasion, or bleeding between periods that continues for more than a few cycles
    • Bleeding with pelvic pain, fever or an offensive discharge, or bleeding that returns after periods had stopped on testosterone

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Hormonal contraceptionBreakthrough bleeding or spotting is common in the first few months of the pill, patch, implant, injection and hormonal coil, and after missed pills; it usually settles, but bleeding that persists is checked for other causes.
    Irregular ovulationIn the first years after periods start, in the years before the menopause and in polycystic ovary syndrome, cycles are unpredictable and bleeding can be irregular, prolonged or arrive between expected periods.
    Cervical ectropion and polypsSoft glandular cells on the outer surface of the cervix (common in pregnancy and on the pill) and benign polyps of the cervix or uterine lining bleed easily, typically after sex or as spotting between periods.
    InfectionChlamydia, gonorrhoea and pelvic inflammatory disease cause bleeding after sex or between periods, often with discharge, pain or fever; swabs for sexually transmitted infections are part of the assessment.
    FibroidsBenign growths of the uterine muscle cause heavy, prolonged periods and sometimes bleeding between them.
    Early pregnancyLight spotting around the time of implantation is common, but bleeding in a known or possible pregnancy can signal a threatened or actual miscarriage and always needs assessment.

    Less common causes

    Ectopic pregnancyLight, often dark bleeding with one-sided pelvic pain, shoulder-tip pain or faintness about six to eight weeks after the last period; a pregnancy outside the uterus can rupture and is an emergency.
    Cervical cancerBleeding after sex, between periods or after the menopause, with or without an unusual discharge. Cervical screening is a test for people without symptoms, so bleeding needs the cervix to be examined directly.
    Cancer of the uterine liningEndometrial cancer most often presents as post-menopausal bleeding and is more likely with obesity, diabetes, polycystic ovary syndrome or oestrogen taken without a progestogen; it is investigated with a transvaginal ultrasound of the lining and an endometrial biopsy.
    Vaginal atrophy and injuryLow oestrogen after the menopause, and in trans men on testosterone, thins the vaginal lining so that it bleeds after sex; injury to the vulva or vagina is another cause. Atrophy is settled on as the explanation only once cancer has been excluded.
    Bleeding disorders and anticoagulant medicinesInherited clotting disorders and anticoagulant medicines make menstrual and other bleeding heavier and more prolonged.

    Conditions

    • Cervical cancer A malignant tumour of the cervix, the neck of the uterus, that develops almost always from persistent infection with high-risk types of human…
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Chlamydia A sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium Chlamydia trachomatis.
    • Miscarriage The loss of a pregnancy.

    Medical tests

    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • Cervical screening (smear test) A sample of cells brushed from the transformation zone of the cervix.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Biomarkers

    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.
    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Medications

    • Combined oral contraceptive pill The combined pill is a hormonal contraceptive that contains two hormones, oestrogen and a progestogen.

    Guides and news

    • Bleeding after menopause or between periods Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be checked by a GP.
    • Cervical screening: what happens and results Cervical screening checks a sample from the cervix for high-risk HPV.
    • Menopause: symptoms and the help available HRT is the main medicine for menopause symptoms, and non-hormone options exist.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
