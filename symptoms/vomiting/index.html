    Vomiting

    Also known as: being sick, emesis, throwing up

    Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway, tenses the abdominal wall and reverses the direction of gut contractions. A day or two of vomiting is usually harmless; persistent, bloody or painful vomiting is not.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Vomiting blood or black material like coffee grounds
    • Green or faecal-smelling vomit with a swollen painful abdomen
    • Vomiting after a head injury, or with severe headache, stiff neck or confusion
    • Signs of dehydration: very dark urine or none for 8 hours, dizziness on standing, confusion; in babies a sunken soft spot and few wet nappies
    • Vomiting for more than two days, or any vomiting in a baby under three months

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    GastroenteritisViral (norovirus, rotavirus) or bacterial; sudden onset, often with diarrhoea.
    Food poisoningHours after a meal, often affecting several people.
    Migraine
    PregnancyHyperemesis when severe.
    Alcohol excess and medication
    Reflux and gastric outlet problemsVomiting undigested food some time after eating.

    Less common causes

    Bowel obstructionGreen (bile-stained) or faeculent vomit with pain and distension.
    Appendicitis, pancreatitis, cholecystitisVomiting with localised pain.
    Bleeding from the stomach or oesophagusVomiting blood or material like coffee grounds.
    Head injury, meningitis, raised intracranial pressureVomiting with headache, drowsiness or after trauma.
    Diabetic ketoacidosisVomiting with thirst, deep breathing and drowsiness in a diabetic.
    Pyloric stenosis in infantsProjectile vomiting in the first weeks of life.

    Conditions

    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Brain tumours A brain tumour is a growth of abnormal cells in or around the brain, and it may be non-cancerous or cancerous.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Hepatitis C Hepatitis C is an infection with a virus that causes inflammation of the liver, which can damage the liver over time.

    Medical tests

    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.

    Biomarkers

    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.
    • Doxycycline Doxycycline is a tetracycline antibiotic, available on prescription.
    • Metoclopramide Metoclopramide is an anti-sickness medicine used to stop nausea (feeling sick) and vomiting (being sick).
    • Mirtazapine Mirtazapine is an antidepressant, prescribed mainly for depression and sometimes for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and anxiety.
    • Ondansetron Ondansetron is an anti-sickness medicine used to prevent nausea and vomiting caused by chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery.
    • Sumatriptan Sumatriptan is a medicine used to treat migraines and cluster headaches once an attack has begun, and it does not stop migraines from starting.
    • Tirzepatide (Mounjaro) Tirzepatide is a prescription medicine used to manage type 2 diabetes and to treat obesity.
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Drug classes

    • 5-HT3 receptor antagonists Anti-sickness medicines that block 5-HT3 receptors; ondansetron is the member covered on this site.
    • Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines are used to treat nausea and vomiting, and metoclopramide is the member covered on this site.

    Guides and news

    • Feeling sick and vomiting in adults NHS inform says vomiting in adults usually lasts only 1 or 2 days, but blood in vomit means calling 999 or going to A&E.
    • Head injury and concussion: when to get help Most head injuries are not serious, but the NHS says being knocked out and not waking, a fit or being unable to stay awake need a 999 call.
    • Preventing food poisoning at home: the 4 Cs NHS inform and the Food Standards Agency describe four steps for food safety at home: cleaning, cooking, chilling and avoiding cross-contamination.
    • Avoiding and managing travellers' diarrhoea Travellers can lower the risk of diarrhoea with careful food, drink and hand hygiene, and replacing lost fluid is the main way it is managed.
