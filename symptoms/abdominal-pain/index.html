    Abdominal pain

    Also known as: stomach ache, belly pain, tummy pain

    Pain between the ribs and the pelvis. Where it sits, how it started and what makes it better or worse point to the organ involved: the gut, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidneys, bladder and reproductive organs all lie here, and some pain is referred from the chest or spine.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Sudden severe pain, or pain with a rigid, very tender abdomen
    • Pain with repeated vomiting, vomiting blood or black tarry stools
    • Pain with fever and inability to keep fluids down
    • Pain in pregnancy or possible pregnancy, especially one-sided with bleeding
    • Pain with collapse, a racing pulse or cold clammy skin

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    GastroenteritisCramping pain with vomiting and diarrhoea, usually viral, lasting a few days.
    Indigestion, reflux and peptic ulcerBurning or gnawing upper abdominal pain related to meals.
    Constipation and irritable bowel syndromeBloating and cramping relieved by passing stool or wind.
    Trapped wind and food intoleranceBloating and cramps after particular foods.
    Urinary tract infectionLower abdominal ache with burning on passing urine and frequency.
    Period painCramping low abdominal pain in the days around menstruation.

    Less common causes

    AppendicitisPain starting around the navel and moving to the lower right, with loss of appetite, nausea and fever.
    Gallstones and cholecystitisSevere right upper pain after fatty meals, sometimes with fever or jaundice.
    PancreatitisConstant severe upper pain boring through to the back, with vomiting, often after alcohol or with gallstones.
    Kidney stonesAgonising waves of pain from loin to groin with blood in the urine.
    Bowel obstructionColicky pain, vomiting, swelling and no wind or stool.
    Ectopic pregnancyLow one-sided pain with a missed period; an emergency.
    Abdominal aortic aneurysmDeep pain with collapse in an older person.

    Conditions

    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Ovarian cancer A malignant tumour of the ovary or, as is now recognised for many cases of the commonest type, of the adjacent fallopian tube.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Hepatitis B tests A set of blood tests that shows whether someone has hepatitis B now, had it in the past, is immune from vaccination, or carries the virus long term.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.

    Biomarkers

    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.
    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…

    Medications

    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Lactulose Lactulose is a laxative used to treat constipation, and it is also used for hepatic encephalopathy, a condition caused by severe liver disease.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.
    • Loperamide Loperamide is a medicine for diarrhoea that slows the activity of the bowel so that stools firm up.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Tirzepatide (Mounjaro) Tirzepatide is a prescription medicine used to manage type 2 diabetes and to treat obesity.

    Guides and news

    • Colonoscopy: preparation and what happens A colonoscopy uses a thin camera to look inside the large bowel.
    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
    • Kidney stones: symptoms and treatment Kidney stones form when chemicals in urine build up; small ones may cause no symptoms, while larger ones can cause severe pain.
    • Stomach ache: causes by area and warning signs Where a stomach ache sits helps point to causes such as appendicitis, gallstones or IBS, and sudden severe pain needs 999 or A&E.
    • Avoiding and managing travellers' diarrhoea Travellers can lower the risk of diarrhoea with careful food, drink and hand hygiene, and replacing lost fluid is the main way it is managed.
    • UTI and cystitis: what they are A urinary tract infection (UTI) can affect the bladder, urethra or kidneys; cystitis is a UTI of the bladder, and antibiotics are not always needed.
