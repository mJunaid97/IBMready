    Back pain

    Also known as: low back pain, lumbago

    Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back. Nine in ten episodes are 'non-specific': a strain of the muscles, ligaments and small joints that settles within weeks. Persistent, progressive or nerve-related pain needs closer assessment.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Numbness around the buttocks or genitals, difficulty passing or controlling urine or stool, or weakness in both legs: possible cauda equina syndrome, an emergency
    • Back pain after significant trauma
    • Back pain with fever, unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer
    • Severe pain with a pulsating abdominal mass or collapse

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Non-specific mechanical back painMuscle and ligament strain from lifting, posture or sudden movement; worse with movement, better with gentle activity.
    Sciatica from a slipped (herniated) discPain shooting down the buttock and leg below the knee, sometimes with numbness or weakness.
    Degenerative disc and facet joint diseaseStiffness and ache that worsen with age and inactivity.
    Spinal stenosisLeg pain and heaviness on walking, relieved by sitting or leaning forward.

    Less common causes

    Vertebral fractureSudden pain after a fall, or in osteoporosis after minimal strain.
    Infection of the spine or discsConstant pain with fever and night sweats, often with diabetes or intravenous drug use.
    Cancer spread to the spineUnremitting night pain, weight loss, a known cancer.
    Inflammatory back pain (ankylosing spondylitis)Morning stiffness lasting over an hour in a young adult, better with exercise.
    Kidney stones, pyelonephritis or aortic aneurysmBack pain from organs rather than the spine.

    Often occurs with

    NumbnessTingling (pins and needles)WeaknessFeverPelvic pain

    Conditions

    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Abdominal aortic aneurysm An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a bulge in the aorta, the body's main blood vessel, where it runs through the tummy.
    • Bladder cancer Bladder cancer is cancer that forms in the tissues of the bladder, the organ that stores urine.
    • Cervical cancer A malignant tumour of the cervix, the neck of the uterus, that develops almost always from persistent infection with high-risk types of human…
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.

    Medications

    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Naproxen Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.
