    Dizziness

    Also known as: light-headedness, vertigo, faintness

    An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint, usually a blood-flow problem), imbalance, and vague woozy feelings. Sorting out which one the person means is the first step.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Dizziness with new weakness, numbness, slurred speech, double vision or difficulty walking
    • Dizziness with chest pain, palpitations or a blackout
    • Dizziness with a severe headache or after a head injury
    • Vertigo with sudden hearing loss

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Benign paroxysmal positional vertigoBrief spinning triggered by rolling over or looking up, from loose crystals in the inner ear.
    Vestibular neuritis and labyrinthitisDays of constant vertigo with nausea after a viral infection.
    Postural (orthostatic) hypotensionLight-headedness on standing, common with dehydration, blood pressure tablets and age.
    MigraineVertigo can occur with or without headache.
    Anxiety and hyperventilationFloating, unreal feelings with rapid breathing.
    Medication side effects and alcoholSedatives, blood pressure drugs and anticonvulsants are frequent culprits.

    Less common causes

    Stroke or TIA in the brainstem or cerebellumSudden vertigo with double vision, slurred speech, weakness or inability to walk.
    Heart rhythm problemsDizziness with palpitations or blackouts.
    Meniere's diseaseAttacks of vertigo with hearing loss, ringing and ear fullness.
    Anaemia, low blood sugar or heart valve diseaseFaintness on exertion.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Anxiety and generalised anxiety disorder Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is a long-term condition in which a person feels worried or anxious about many different things, much of the time.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Medications

    • Amitriptyline Amitriptyline is a prescription-only medicine from a group called tricyclic antidepressants, used for nerve pain and to help prevent migraine.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Duloxetine Duloxetine is an antidepressant medicine that is also used for anxiety and nerve pain.
    • Fluoxetine Fluoxetine is an SSRI antidepressant, available only on prescription, that the NHS says is used for depression.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Gabapentin A prescription medicine used to treat epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.

    Guides and news

    • Altitude sickness symptoms and prevention Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.
    • Anxiety: signs, treatment and getting help Anxiety becomes a health problem when worry is hard to control and affects daily life.
    • Dizziness and vertigo: causes and warning signs Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure.
    • Falls in older people and reducing the risk Strength and balance exercises and simple changes at home can lower the risk of falls in older people, according to the NHS.
    • Head injury and concussion: when to get help Most head injuries are not serious, but the NHS says being knocked out and not waking, a fit or being unable to stay awake need a 999 call.
    • Hearing loss: causes, tests and what helps Gradual hearing loss is common and a GP can arrange hearing tests.
    • Heart rhythm monitors: Holter and loop recorders A Holter monitor records the heart's electrical activity for 24 to 48 hours or longer, to catch rhythm problems that come and go.
    • Heat exhaustion and heatstroke: what to know Heat exhaustion usually eases with cooling and rest, while heatstroke is an emergency for which the NHS says to call 999.
    • Panic attacks: what they are and what helps A panic attack is a rush of intense fear with physical symptoms such as a racing heartbeat.
    • Protecting your hearing from loud noise The WHO and NHS sources say noise-related hearing loss is permanent but can be prevented, mainly by lowering volume.
