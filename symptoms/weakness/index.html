    Weakness

    Also known as: muscle weakness, loss of strength

    Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness. True weakness means a muscle cannot do what it could before, and its pattern (one side, one limb, both legs, face and eyes, or everywhere) points to the brain, spinal cord, nerve, junction or muscle.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Sudden weakness of the face, arm or leg, especially on one side: call emergency services (stroke)
    • Weakness of both legs, or weakness with loss of bladder or bowel control
    • Weakness that spreads over hours or days, or affects swallowing or breathing
    • Weakness after a head, neck or back injury

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Deconditioning, illness and ageGeneralised weakness after bed rest, infection or with muscle loss in older age.
    Nerve compressionFoot drop from a slipped disc, wrist weakness from radial nerve pressure, hand weakness from carpal tunnel or ulnar nerve entrapment.
    Bell's palsySudden weakness of one side of the face including the forehead.
    Low potassium, thyroid disease, steroidsGeneralised or proximal weakness (difficulty rising from a chair, lifting arms).
    Statin and other drug-induced myopathyAching weak thighs and shoulders.

    Less common causes

    Stroke and TIASudden weakness of the face, arm or leg on one side.
    Spinal cord compressionWeakness of both legs with numbness and bladder problems.
    Guillain-Barre syndromeAscending weakness over days, can affect breathing.
    Myasthenia gravisFatigable weakness: drooping eyelids, double vision, worse as the day goes on.
    Motor neurone disease and muscular dystrophiesProgressive weakness with wasting or twitching.
    Multiple sclerosisEpisodes of weakness, numbness or vision loss.

    Often occurs with

    NumbnessTingling (pins and needles)FatigueDizzinessBack pain

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Brain tumours A brain tumour is a growth of abnormal cells in or around the brain, and it may be non-cancerous or cancerous.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Medical tests

    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Simvastatin Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes.
    • Testosterone The main androgen, given as a gel, patch, injection or implant to replace what the testes cannot make in confirmed hypogonadism and as the…

    Guides and news

    • Numbness and tingling: causes and when to act Pins and needles are usually brief and harmless, but numbness or tingling that lasts, keeps returning or comes with weakness needs checking.
    • Stroke and TIA: spotting the signs A stroke and a TIA are both emergencies: the NHS and the Stroke Association say to call 999 even if the symptoms pass.
    • Tiredness and fatigue: causes and next steps Tiredness is common and often has an ordinary cause such as poor sleep, stress or illness.
