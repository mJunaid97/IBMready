    Fever

    Also known as: high temperature, pyrexia

    A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals, most often from infection. Fever is a response, not a disease, and it helps the immune system; the cause and the person's overall condition matter more than the number.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Fever with a rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed on it, a stiff neck, or severe headache
    • Fever with fast breathing, chest pain, confusion, drowsiness or mottled cold skin
    • Fever in a baby under three months (38 °C or more) or under six months (39 °C or more)
    • Fever with a seizure, or in someone on chemotherapy or with a weakened immune system
    • Fever after travel to a malaria area
    • A temperature above 40 °C, or fever lasting more than five days

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Viral infectionsColds, flu, COVID-19, glandular fever; usually with sore throat, cough or aches.
    Bacterial infectionsTonsillitis, ear infection, chest infection, urinary infection, cellulitis, dental abscess.
    Gastroenteritis
    Vaccination reactionsMild, brief fever within a day or two.
    Childhood viral illnessesRoseola, hand-foot-and-mouth and many others.

    Less common causes

    SepsisInfection overwhelming the body: fast breathing, fast pulse, confusion, mottled skin, low urine output.
    MeningitisFever with headache, stiff neck, light sensitivity, a non-blanching rash.
    Pneumonia, pyelonephritis, endocarditis, abscessesDeep-seated bacterial infections.
    Tropical infectionsMalaria, dengue and typhoid after travel.
    Non-infectious causesInflammatory diseases, some cancers (lymphoma), drug reactions, heat stroke.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.
    • Cellulitis Cellulitis is an infection of the deeper layers of the skin caused by bacteria.
    • COVID-19 COVID-19 is an illness caused by a virus called coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2), with symptoms that overlap with colds and flu.
    • Diverticulitis and diverticular disease Diverticular disease is a condition in which small pouches in the wall of the large intestine cause symptoms.
    • Ear infection (otitis media) An ear infection is an infection, usually of the middle ear behind the eardrum, that is common in children and often settles without treatment.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • HIV test A blood test that looks for the human immunodeficiency virus.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Lymphocytes The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells).
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.
    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…
    • Vaginoplasty Construction of a vagina and vulva, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans women and some non-binary people.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Guides and news

    • Blood in urine: causes and what happens next Blood in the urine is not usually serious, but the NHS advises seeing a GP every time, even if the amount is small.
    • Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth Chickenpox, measles and hand, foot and mouth all cause rashes in children, but their other signs and the advice on getting help differ.
    • Which NHS service to use: 999, 111, GP, A&E The NHS says 999 is for life-threatening emergencies and NHS 111 for urgent problems or when unsure, with pharmacies.
    • Fever in adults: causes and when to get help A high temperature in adults is usually a sign of infection and often settles at home.
    • Fever and rashes in children: when to get help Most fevers in children can be looked after at home.
    • Flu or a cold: how they differ Flu tends to start faster and feel worse than a cold, with fever, aches and exhaustion; a cold mostly stays in the nose and throat.
    • How travellers can reduce malaria risk UK guidance sums up malaria prevention as ABCD: awareness of risk, bite avoidance, antimalarial tablets where needed, and prompt diagnosis.
    • Sepsis: warning signs and when to act Sepsis is a life-threatening reaction to an infection; the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E if sepsis is suspected.
    • Sore throat: causes, treatment, when to act Most sore throats are caused by viruses and clear up on their own, but struggling to breathe or swallow needs 999 or A&E.
    • Teething in babies: signs and what helps The NHS lists sore red gums, a flushed cheek and extra dribbling as signs of teething, and suggests gum massage or a cool teething ring.
