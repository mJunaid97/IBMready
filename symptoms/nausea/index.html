    Nausea

    Also known as: feeling sick, queasiness

    The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit. It is coordinated by centres in the medulla that respond to signals from the gut, the inner ear, the blood (toxins, drugs, hormones) and the higher brain (smells, fear, pain).

    Seek urgent care if

    • Nausea with chest pain, sweating or breathlessness
    • Nausea with severe abdominal pain or a swollen, rigid abdomen
    • Nausea with a severe headache, stiff neck or confusion
    • Persistent nausea in pregnancy with inability to keep fluids down

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Gastroenteritis and food poisoningWith vomiting, diarrhoea and cramps.
    MedicationsAntibiotics, painkillers (opioids, NSAIDs), chemotherapy, iron, metformin.
    Early pregnancyMorning sickness in the first trimester.
    MigraineNausea accompanies most attacks.
    Motion sickness and inner ear disordersWith vertigo.
    Reflux, gastritis and overeatingUpper abdominal discomfort with nausea after meals.
    Alcohol and hangover

    Less common causes

    Bowel obstruction or appendicitisNausea with pain and a swollen abdomen.
    Heart attackNausea and sweating may be the only symptoms, especially in women and diabetics.
    Raised pressure in the skullMorning nausea and headache.
    Kidney failure, liver failure, diabetic ketoacidosisMetabolic causes with drowsiness.
    Gallstones and pancreatitisWith upper abdominal pain.

    Conditions

    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Brain tumours A brain tumour is a growth of abnormal cells in or around the brain, and it may be non-cancerous or cancerous.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Hepatitis B tests A set of blood tests that shows whether someone has hepatitis B now, had it in the past, is immune from vaccination, or carries the virus long term.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.

    Biomarkers

    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Diclofenac Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones.
    • Doxycycline Doxycycline is a tetracycline antibiotic, available on prescription.
    • Duloxetine Duloxetine is an antidepressant medicine that is also used for anxiety and nerve pain.
    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Fluconazole Fluconazole is an antifungal medicine used for thrush and other fungal infections.

    Drug classes

    • 5-HT3 receptor antagonists Anti-sickness medicines that block 5-HT3 receptors; ondansetron is the member covered on this site.
    • Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines Dopamine antagonist anti-sickness medicines are used to treat nausea and vomiting, and metoclopramide is the member covered on this site.

    Guides and news

    • Altitude sickness symptoms and prevention Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.
    • Dizziness and vertigo: causes and warning signs Dizziness is common and has many causes, from inner ear problems to low blood pressure.
    • Feeling sick and vomiting in adults NHS inform says vomiting in adults usually lasts only 1 or 2 days, but blood in vomit means calling 999 or going to A&E.
    • Gastroscopy: what happens and what to expect A gastroscopy uses a thin camera to look at the gullet and stomach.
    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
    • Heat exhaustion and heatstroke: what to know Heat exhaustion usually eases with cooling and rest, while heatstroke is an emergency for which the NHS says to call 999.
    • Kidney stones: symptoms and treatment Kidney stones form when chemicals in urine build up; small ones may cause no symptoms, while larger ones can cause severe pain.
    • How travellers can reduce malaria risk UK guidance sums up malaria prevention as ABCD: awareness of risk, bite avoidance, antimalarial tablets where needed, and prompt diagnosis.
    • Migraine and tension headache compared Tension headache is usually a dull, tight pressure on both sides of the head, while migraine often throbs and brings nausea or sensitivity to light.
    • Preventing food poisoning at home: the 4 Cs NHS inform and the Food Standards Agency describe four steps for food safety at home: cleaning, cooking, chilling and avoiding cross-contamination.
