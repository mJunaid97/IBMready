    Breast lump

    Also known as: lump in the breast, breast mass, breast nodule, lumpy breasts

    A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue, which extends from about the second to the sixth rib and up into the armpit as the axillary tail. Breast tissue is a mixture of glands, ducts and fat that changes with the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, breastfeeding and age, so lumpiness that comes and goes with periods is common. Most lumps are benign, but no lump can be reliably sorted by touch alone: a new lump is assessed at a breast clinic by triple assessment, meaning clinical examination, imaging (mammography, usually with ultrasound, or ultrasound alone in younger people) and, where needed, a needle biopsy. Breast screening is offered to people who have no symptoms; a new lump should not wait for the next screening appointment, and a normal recent screening mammogram does not exclude cancer in a lump found since. Men have a small amount of breast tissue behind the nipple, trans women on oestrogen develop breast tissue, and trans men who have had chest surgery retain some, so a lump needs the same assessment in everyone.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Any new lump or area of thickening in the breast or armpit, in anyone, which needs a GP appointment within days rather than weeks
    • Dimpling, puckering or redness of the skin, or a change in the size or shape of one breast
    • A nipple that has newly turned inwards, a rash or crusting around the nipple, or blood-stained discharge
    • A hot, red, swollen, painful breast with fever, which may be an abscess needing drainage
    • A lump that has been checked before but has since grown or changed

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    FibroadenomaA smooth, firm, rubbery lump that moves easily under the fingers, most common in teenagers and women in their twenties and thirties; benign, and often simply monitored once confirmed.
    Breast cystA fluid-filled sac that can appear quickly, feel smooth and tense or tender, and vary with the cycle; most common from the forties up to the menopause. Ultrasound confirms it and draining it with a needle relieves it.
    Fibrocystic changeGeneralised lumpiness or ropiness, often in the upper outer part of the breast, tender in the days before a period and settling afterwards; a normal variation rather than a disease.
    Mastitis and breast abscessInfection, most often during breastfeeding, causing a hot, red, painful, swollen area with fever; an abscess forms a tender, fluctuant lump that needs draining.
    Fat necrosis and lipomaA firm lump after a knock, injury or surgery to the breast (fat necrosis) can mimic cancer and needs imaging to confirm; a lipoma is a soft, benign lump of fatty tissue.

    Less common causes

    Breast cancerTypically a hard, irregular, painless lump that feels fixed to the surrounding tissue, sometimes with skin dimpling, a newly inverted nipple, nipple discharge or a lump in the armpit. Risk rises steeply with age; it is rare in men but does occur.
    Phyllodes tumourAn uncommon, often fast-growing lump that resembles a fibroadenoma; most are benign, but they can recur or occasionally be malignant, so they are removed.
    Gynaecomastia in menEnlargement of the glandular tissue behind the nipple, usually on both sides and sometimes tender, common at puberty and in older age; it may follow hormone changes, liver disease or medicines. A firm one-sided lump away from the nipple is assessed for cancer.
    Lumps after gender-affirming careTrans women on oestrogen develop breast tissue and can form the same benign and malignant lumps as cisgender women; trans men who have had chest masculinisation surgery keep a thin layer of breast tissue, so a lump on the chest wall still needs assessment. Breast screening invitations depend on how a person is registered with their GP: trans women who have been on gender-affirming hormones for several years and trans men who have not had chest surgery are eligible, and may need to ask to be invited. A new lump is assessed in the same way in everyone and should not wait for screening.

    Conditions

    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Imaging

    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.

    Medications

    • Finasteride Finasteride is a tablet that stops testosterone turning into the hormone DHT, used for an enlarged prostate and for male pattern hair loss.

    Guides and news

    • Found a breast lump: what happens next Most breast lumps are harmless, but the NHS advises seeing a GP about any new lump so it can be checked.
    • Breast screening: who is invited and what happens NHS breast screening uses mammograms to look for breast cancer in people without symptoms.
