    Cough

    Also known as: chesty cough, dry cough, tickly cough

    A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material. An acute cough (under three weeks) almost always follows a viral infection; a chronic cough (over eight weeks) needs a cause found.

    Seek urgent care if

    • Coughing up blood
    • Cough with severe breathlessness, blue lips or chest pain
    • Cough lasting more than three weeks, especially with weight loss, night sweats or hoarseness
    • A child with noisy breathing, drawing in of the ribs or drooling and difficulty swallowing

    In an emergency call 999 (112 elsewhere in Europe). For an urgent problem that is not an emergency, contact NHS 111 or your GP the same day.

    Common causes

    Viral upper respiratory infectionCold or flu; cough can linger for weeks after other symptoms settle.
    Acute bronchitisProductive cough after a cold, usually viral.
    AsthmaDry cough worse at night or with exercise and cold air; may be the only symptom.
    Post-nasal drip and rhinitisTickly cough with a blocked or runny nose and throat clearing.
    Acid refluxCough worse lying down or after meals.
    Smoking and COPDMorning cough with phlegm.
    ACE inhibitor tabletsA dry cough in up to 1 in 10 people taking them.

    Less common causes

    PneumoniaCough with fever, breathlessness and chest pain.
    Whooping coughBouts of coughing ending in a gasp or vomiting.
    TuberculosisCough over three weeks with night sweats, weight loss, sometimes blood.
    Lung cancerNew or changed cough in a smoker, with blood, weight loss or hoarseness.
    Heart failure and pulmonary embolismCough with breathlessness, frothy sputum or blood.
    Inhaled foreign bodySudden cough and wheeze, especially in toddlers.

    Often occurs with

    Shortness of breathFeverChest painFatigue

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Bronchitis Bronchitis is inflammation of the airways in the lungs, usually caused by an infection.
    • COVID-19 COVID-19 is an illness caused by a virus called coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2), with symptoms that overlap with colds and flu.
    • Flu (influenza) Flu is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Chlorphenamine A first-generation antihistamine, sold over the counter, that relieves hay fever.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Methotrexate Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease.

    Guides and news

    • ACE inhibitors, ARBs and amlodipine compared ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.
    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • Flu or a cold: how they differ Flu tends to start faster and feel worse than a cold, with fever, aches and exhaustion; a cold mostly stays in the nose and throat.
    • A cough that will not go: causes and tests The NHS advises a GP visit for a cough lasting over 3 weeks, and an urgent GP appointment or NHS 111 for coughing up blood.
    • Sore throat: causes, treatment, when to act Most sore throats are caused by viruses and clear up on their own, but struggling to breathe or swallow needs 999 or A&E.
    • Spirometry: the lung function test explained Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast.
    • Why antibiotics do not work on viruses Antibiotics act on bacteria, not viruses, so the NHS says they do not help colds, flu or most coughs and sore throats.
