    CT vs MRI

    Both produce cross-sectional images of the body, but by different physics: CT uses X-rays and is fast, MRI uses magnetic fields and radio waves and shows soft tissue in more detail without radiation. The choice depends on the question, the urgency and the patient.

    AspectCT scanMRI scan
    How it worksRotating X-ray beam and detectors reconstruct slices from tissue densityStrong magnetic field and radio pulses map hydrogen nuclei; sequences highlight different tissue properties
    RadiationYes: a moderate dose that adds up with repeated scansNone
    SpeedSeconds to a few minutes20-60 minutes, lying still in a narrow tunnel
    Best tissue contrastBone, lung, acute bleeding, calcification, contrast-enhanced vesselsBrain, spinal cord, muscle, ligaments, cartilage, pelvic organs, marrow
    Contrast agentsIodinated contrast (allergy and kidney precautions; metformin paused)Gadolinium (avoided in severe kidney impairment)
    LimitationsRadiation, less soft-tissue detail, iodine contrast risksMetal implants and devices, claustrophobia, cost and availability, motion

    Where CT scan fits

    Emergencies (trauma, stroke within minutes, bleeding, pulmonary embolism), bone and lung detail, people who cannot lie still or have MRI-incompatible implants

    Where MRI scan fits

    Brain and spinal cord, joints, ligaments and cartilage, the pelvis, liver lesions, and when radiation must be avoided
