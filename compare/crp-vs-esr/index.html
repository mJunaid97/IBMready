    CRP vs ESR

    Both are 'inflammatory markers', and they are often ordered together, but they measure different things at different speeds. CRP is a single protein that rises and falls within days; the ESR is a physical property of blood that changes over weeks and is pushed up by many things besides inflammation.

    AspectCRP testESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate)
    What is measuredConcentration of C-reactive protein, an acute-phase protein made by the liverHow far red cells settle in a tube in one hour, a proxy for fibrinogen and immunoglobulins
    Speed of changeRises within 6 hours, peaks at about 48 hours, falls quickly (half-life about 19 hours)Rises over days and falls over weeks
    Non-inflammatory influencesObesity, smoking and pregnancy raise it modestlyAnaemia, pregnancy, age, kidney disease and high immunoglobulins raise it; polycythaemia and sickle cells lower it
    Typical useInfection and sepsis, post-operative monitoring, inflammatory bowel disease, point-of-care antibiotic decisionsGiant cell arteritis, polymyalgia rheumatica, myeloma, rheumatoid arthritis monitoring
    Units and rangemg/L; laboratory upper limit, no diagnostic thresholdmm/h; reference limit rises with age and differs by sex
    When they disagreeHigh CRP with normal ESR: early or minor inflammationHigh ESR with normal CRP: anaemia, pregnancy, lupus, myeloma or age

    Where CRP test fits

    Tracking an acute infection or the response to antibiotics or surgery, where speed matters

    Where ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) fits

    Suspected giant cell arteritis or polymyalgia, myeloma, and long-term monitoring of some rheumatic diseases

    Educational content. A comparison explains what each test, marker or medicine is for and how they differ. It is educational and does not say which one a person should have; that decision belongs to the clinician who knows the person and the question.
