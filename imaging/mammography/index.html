    Mammography

    Also known as: mammogram, breast screening, breast X-ray, tomosynthesis

    A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt, used for screening programmes and for investigating breast symptoms.

    Open in the 3D explorerRib cage anatomy

    How it works

    A low-dose X-ray of each breast, compressed between two plates for a few seconds to spread the tissue thin and hold it still. Two views are taken of each side. Digital tomosynthesis takes a sweep of images to reconstruct thin slices, reducing overlap. Cancers show as masses, distortions or clusters of tiny calcifications, often years before they can be felt. At an NHS screening appointment a female mammographer positions one breast at a time on the machine; the person undresses from the waist up in private, the breast is pressed firmly between two clear plates for a few seconds, and the pressure both holds the breast still and lets the machine use the lowest radiation dose that still gives a clear image. The machine is then tilted for a side view and the other breast is imaged the same way. The images are read by radiologists or trained mammogram readers, who look for masses, deposits of calcium and the pattern of breast density; dense breast tissue makes a cancer harder to see. Diagnostic mammography, used when there is a symptom or a screening recall, takes images from more angles than a screening study and so delivers a somewhat higher dose. Digital breast tomosynthesis (3D mammography) combined with standard mammography finds more tumours than standard mammography alone, although whether it reduces breast cancer deaths is still being tested. In the United States, the Mammography Quality Standards Act requires every facility to be FDA-certified, surveyed annually by a medical physicist, inspected yearly, and to send each patient a plain-language result.

    What it shows

    • Breast cancer, including ductal carcinoma in situ (calcifications)
    • Benign lumps: cysts, fibroadenomas (with ultrasound to tell them apart)
    • Breast density, which affects both risk and how easily cancers are seen
    • Reporting: in the United States results are graded with BI-RADS categories from 0 (more imaging needed) to 6 (known cancer); in England a screening result is either normal or a recall for assessment

    Best for

    • Population screening: in England women are invited every 3 years from a first invitation between 50 and 53 until their 71st birthday, and can continue to book screening themselves afterwards; US guidance (USPSTF) recommends screening every 2 years from 40 to 74
    • Investigating a new lump, nipple change or skin change in women over 40; trans women on long-term oestrogen and trans men who have not had chest surgery are offered screening on the same basis as the tissue they have
    • Follow-up after breast cancer treatment
    • Earlier or more frequent screening in women at high risk, such as carriers of harmful BRCA1 or BRCA2 variants, usually alongside MRI

    Not the right tool for

    • Younger women with dense breasts, where ultrasound and MRI see more
    • Assessing implants (MRI); implants can hide breast tissue, so the facility is told in advance and special implant-displacement views are used
    • Ruling out cancer completely: the NHS is clear that mammograms do not find all cancers, so a new lump or change is always investigated even after a normal screen
    • Preventing cancer: screening only finds cancer that is already present

    What to expect

    Modality
    Mammography
    Preparation
    No deodorant, antiperspirant or talc on the day (they can affect the images); book after a period if breasts are tender. Staff confirm identity and ask about any previous breast problems; necklaces and nipple piercings are removed, and a binder is taken off before the images. Anyone with implants tells the facility when booking.
    Duration
    The NHS says the whole appointment should take no longer than about 30 minutes; the imaging itself takes only a few minutes, with each compression lasting a few seconds. Results are usually sent by letter within about 2 weeks and the GP is informed.
    Radiation
    Low. The NHS states that the amount of radiation used in a mammogram is very small and the health risk very low, and the compression that holds the breast still is what lets the machine use the lowest dose for a clear image. A diagnostic study, which takes more views, gives a somewhat higher dose than a two-view screening study.
    Contrast
    Contrast-enhanced mammography with iodine is an emerging alternative to breast MRI.
    Risks
    Discomfort from compression, which some people find briefly painful; it eases quickly and the mammographer can be asked to stop at any point. A very small radiation dose. False alarms: in England about 4 in every 100 women screened are called back for further tests (examination, more mammograms, ultrasound and sometimes a needle biopsy), and about 1 of those 4 is found to have cancer, so most recalls are not cancer. Overdiagnosis: some women are diagnosed and treated for a cancer that would never have been found otherwise and would not have become life-threatening. Missed cancers: a cancer may not be visible, or very occasionally is missed by the reader, which is why changes between screens are reported to a GP. Set against these, the NHS estimates that screening saves about 1,400 lives a year in England; it does not prevent breast cancer.

    Conditions

    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.

    Guides and news

    • Found a breast lump: what happens next Most breast lumps are harmless, but the NHS advises seeing a GP about any new lump so it can be checked.
    • Breast screening: who is invited and what happens NHS breast screening uses mammograms to look for breast cancer in people without symptoms.
    • NHS screening: who is invited and how often In England the NHS invites eligible people for bowel, breast, cervical, abdominal aortic aneurysm and diabetic eye screening.
