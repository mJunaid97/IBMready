    CT scan

    Also known as: computed tomography, CAT scan, CT angiography, CTPA, CT colonography

    A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs, blood vessels and bleeding in fine detail; the workhorse of emergency imaging.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    How it works

    An X-ray tube spins around the body while it moves through the ring, collecting thousands of projections that a computer reconstructs into thin cross-sectional slices. The slices are stacked to view any plane or a 3D model. Iodine contrast injected into a vein lights up blood vessels and shows how organs and tumours take up blood. In practice the person lies on a flat bed that moves through the ring-shaped scanner while the radiographer operates it from an adjoining room, able to hear and speak to them throughout; they are asked to keep still and sometimes to breathe in, breathe out or hold their breath for a few seconds, because movement blurs the slices. The FDA describes CT as a tool for diagnosing disease, trauma and abnormality, guiding procedures and monitoring treatment such as cancer therapy, and notes that the detail it provides can sometimes make exploratory surgery unnecessary. Most newer scanners can lower the radiation dose, and UK departments compare the doses they deliver against national diagnostic reference levels published by UKHSA.

    What it shows

    • Brain: bleeding, stroke (once established), tumours, injury
    • Chest: lung nodules and cancer, blood clots in the lung arteries (CTPA), fibrosis, infection
    • Abdomen and pelvis: appendicitis, diverticulitis, kidney stones, tumours, abscesses, bowel obstruction
    • Bones: complex fractures, the spine and pelvis
    • Blood vessels: aneurysms, dissection, coronary artery narrowing (CT coronary angiography)

    Best for

    • Emergencies where speed matters: trauma, stroke, suspected bleeding, severe abdominal pain
    • Lung disease and cancer staging
    • Kidney stones (no contrast needed)
    • Detailed bone anatomy for surgical planning
    • Guiding biopsies, drains and other procedures, and monitoring the response of cancer to treatment

    Not the right tool for

    • Early stroke (may look normal for hours), spinal cord, ligaments and cartilage (MRI)
    • Repeated scans in children and young adults where dose accumulates
    • People with severe kidney disease or contrast allergy (contrast may be withheld)
    • Reassurance scans without a clear question: the FDA's principles of justification and optimisation mean a CT is performed only when it is medically warranted and a test using less or no radiation would be less appropriate, and then at the lowest dose that answers the question

    What to expect

    Modality
    CT (computed tomography)
    Preparation
    Fasting for a few hours before contrast; kidney function checked; a cannula placed. Oral contrast is sometimes drunk an hour before abdominal scans. Before the scan, staff ask about the possibility of pregnancy, kidney problems, allergies and any previous reaction to contrast, and metal objects and clothing with metal fastenings are removed from the area being scanned. Children are generally scanned only for serious conditions because they are more sensitive to radiation, and a different exam that uses less or no radiation is chosen when it would answer the question as well.
    Duration
    The scan itself takes seconds to a few minutes; the NHS says the whole scan usually takes about 10 to 20 minutes, and a visit is typically 20-30 minutes. People given contrast may be asked to wait around 30 minutes afterwards so that staff can check for a reaction before they leave.
    Radiation
    Considerably more than a plain X-ray. UKHSA's patient-dose tables list an abdomen-pelvis CT at around 10 mSv: several years of natural background radiation. The dose depends on the body region, the protocol and the scanner, modern machines use far less than older ones, and departments benchmark their doses against UKHSA's national diagnostic reference levels. For a medically necessary scan the FDA judges the risk quite small when set against the benefit of an accurate diagnosis.
    Contrast
    Iodine-based, injected into a vein for vessels, organs and tumours; causes a warm flush and a metallic taste. Metformin may be paused around the scan. The NHS describes a warm feeling, a metallic taste and a sensation of needing to pass urine that settles within minutes. People are often advised to drink plenty of fluids for the rest of the day, and to report a rash or itching that appears in the days after the scan.
    Risks
    A small increase in lifetime cancer risk with each scan, weighed against the benefit; the risk is larger in children and young adults, both because cancer risk per unit dose is higher at younger ages and because there is a longer lifetime for an effect to appear, and it accumulates with repeated scans. Tissue effects such as skin reddening or cataracts are not expected at the doses used for diagnostic CT. Contrast reactions are rare but can occasionally be severe (weakness, sweating or trouble breathing are reported at once), a temporary fall in kidney function occurs very rarely and mostly in people with known kidney disease, and contrast sometimes leaks from the vein at the injection site causing local pain and redness; an itchy rash can appear up to a week later. Unexpected incidental findings may lead to further, sometimes invasive, tests. Avoided in pregnancy unless there is an urgent medical reason.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Knee replacement The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when…
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.
