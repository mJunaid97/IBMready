    CT scan

    Also known as: computed tomography, CAT scan, CT angiography, CTPA, CT colonography

    A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs, blood vessels and bleeding in fine detail; the workhorse of emergency imaging.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    How it works

    An X-ray tube spins around the body while it moves through the ring, collecting thousands of projections that a computer reconstructs into thin cross-sectional slices. The slices are stacked to view any plane or a 3D model. Iodine contrast injected into a vein lights up blood vessels and shows how organs and tumours take up blood.

    What it shows

    • Brain: bleeding, stroke (once established), tumours, injury
    • Chest: lung nodules and cancer, blood clots in the lung arteries (CTPA), fibrosis, infection
    • Abdomen and pelvis: appendicitis, diverticulitis, kidney stones, tumours, abscesses, bowel obstruction
    • Bones: complex fractures, the spine and pelvis
    • Blood vessels: aneurysms, dissection, coronary artery narrowing (CT coronary angiography)

    Best for

    • Emergencies where speed matters: trauma, stroke, suspected bleeding, severe abdominal pain
    • Lung disease and cancer staging
    • Kidney stones (no contrast needed)
    • Detailed bone anatomy for surgical planning

    Not the right tool for

    • Early stroke (may look normal for hours), spinal cord, ligaments and cartilage (MRI)
    • Repeated scans in children and young adults where dose accumulates
    • People with severe kidney disease or contrast allergy (contrast may be withheld)

    What to expect

    Modality
    CT (computed tomography)
    Preparation
    Fasting for a few hours before contrast; kidney function checked; a cannula placed. Oral contrast is sometimes drunk an hour before abdominal scans.
    Duration
    The scan itself takes seconds to a few minutes; the visit 20-30 minutes.
    Radiation
    A head CT is about 2 mSv, a chest CT 5-7 mSv and an abdomen-pelvis CT around 10 mSv: several years of background radiation, though modern scanners use far less than older ones.
    Contrast
    Iodine-based, injected into a vein for vessels, organs and tumours; causes a warm flush and a metallic taste. Metformin may be paused around the scan.
    Risks
    A small increase in lifetime cancer risk with each scan, weighed against the benefit; contrast reactions (rare) and a temporary drop in kidney function in those already at risk. Avoided in pregnancy unless essential.

    Common uses

    RegionTypical question
    HeadHead injury, sudden severe headache, first seizure, suspected stroke or bleed
    ChestLung cancer staging, pulmonary embolism (CTPA), fibrosis, complicated pneumonia
    AbdomenSevere pain, suspected appendicitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, cancer staging
    HeartCT coronary angiography for stable chest pain; calcium scoring
