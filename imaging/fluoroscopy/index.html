    Fluoroscopy and angiography

    Also known as: live X-ray, barium swallow, barium enema, angiogram, coronary angiography, interventional radiology

    Continuous, low-dose X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing, guide catheters and joint injections and follow contrast through the gut or vessels.

    Open in the 3D explorerCoronary vessels anatomy

    How it works

    A continuous or pulsed X-ray beam produces a moving image on a screen, so the radiologist can watch swallowing, bowel movement, joint motion or the passage of contrast through blood vessels in real time. Angiography injects iodine contrast through a fine catheter threaded into an artery, mapping the vessel tree and allowing treatment (angioplasty, stents, coiling, clot removal) in the same session.

    What it shows

    • Swallowing problems and reflux (barium or water-soluble swallow)
    • Bowel narrowing, fistulas, leaks after surgery
    • Blood vessel narrowing, blockage, aneurysms and bleeding points
    • Coronary arteries during cardiac catheterisation
    • Guiding the placement of tubes, lines, stents and orthopaedic hardware

    Best for

    • Anything that must be watched moving
    • Combining diagnosis and treatment in one sitting (interventional radiology and cardiology)
    • Checking that a catheter, wire or stent is in the right place

    Not the right tool for

    • Routine diagnosis where a still image will do (X-ray, CT) because screening time adds dose
    • People who cannot cooperate with swallowing or positioning instructions

    What to expect

    Preparation
    Fasting for gut studies and angiography; kidney function and clotting checked; blood thinners managed; a cannula for sedation.
    Duration
    From 10 minutes for a swallow to 1-3 hours for complex interventional procedures.
    Radiation
    Highly variable: a barium swallow around 1.5 mSv, a diagnostic coronary angiogram about 7 mSv, long interventions much more. Skin can be injured after very prolonged screening.
    Contrast
    Barium for the gut (or a water-soluble alternative if a leak is suspected); iodine for vessels, joints and the urinary tract.
    Risks
    Radiation dose that rises with screening time; contrast reactions and kidney strain; for angiography, bleeding or damage at the artery puncture site, and rarely stroke or vessel injury.

    Common uses

    RegionTypical question
    HeartCoronary angiography for heart attack and angina, with angioplasty and stenting
    BrainCerebral angiography for aneurysms; mechanical thrombectomy in stroke
    GutBarium swallow for dysphagia; contrast studies after bowel surgery
    LimbsPeripheral angiography and angioplasty for blocked leg arteries
