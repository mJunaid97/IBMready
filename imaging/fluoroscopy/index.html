    Fluoroscopy and angiography

    Also known as: live X-ray, barium swallow, barium enema, angiogram, coronary angiography, interventional radiology

    Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing, guide catheters and joint injections and follow contrast through the gut or vessels.

    Open in the 3D explorerCoronary vessels anatomy

    How it works

    A continuous or pulsed X-ray beam produces a moving image on a screen, so the radiologist can watch swallowing, bowel movement, joint motion or the passage of contrast through blood vessels in real time. Angiography injects iodine contrast through a fine catheter threaded into an artery, mapping the vessel tree and allowing treatment (angioplasty, stents, coiling, clot removal) in the same session. The beam is switched on only while the operator needs to see, and the FDA's guidance is that fluoroscopy is always performed with the lowest acceptable exposure for the shortest time necessary, because dose rises with screening time. For angiography the NHS describes numbing the skin at the groin or wrist, passing a thin catheter into the artery (the person may feel some pushing and pulling but it should not hurt), then injecting contrast while a series of X-rays is taken as it flows through the vessels; adults are usually awake, while young children may have a general anaesthetic. A barium swallow works the same way for the gullet: the person drinks a white barium liquid and the radiologist watches it pass in real time, and a videofluoroscopy (modified barium swallow) records foods and drinks of different textures mixed with barium, usually with a speech and language therapist present.

    What it shows

    • Swallowing problems and reflux (barium or water-soluble swallow)
    • Bowel narrowing, fistulas, leaks after surgery
    • Blood vessel narrowing, blockage, aneurysms and bleeding points
    • Coronary arteries during cardiac catheterisation
    • Guiding the placement of tubes, lines, stents and orthopaedic hardware
    • Swallowing in detail: videofluoroscopy records how foods and drinks of different textures are swallowed and whether any enters the airway

    Best for

    • Anything that must be watched moving
    • Combining diagnosis and treatment in one sitting (interventional radiology and cardiology)
    • Checking that a catheter, wire or stent is in the right place
    • Guiding orthopaedic work such as joint replacement and fracture fixation, and placing stents and other devices

    Not the right tool for

    • Routine diagnosis where a still image will do (X-ray, CT) because screening time adds dose
    • People who cannot cooperate with swallowing or positioning instructions
    • Pregnancy, unless the information is urgently needed: anyone who might be pregnant tells staff before the procedure

    What to expect

    Modality
    Fluoroscopy
    Preparation
    Fasting for gut studies and angiography; kidney function and clotting checked; blood thinners managed; a cannula for sedation. Before angiography the team takes a medical history, asks about allergies and current medicines, and says which, if any, should be stopped beforehand; anyone who might be pregnant tells staff in advance. Barium studies need an empty stomach, and a known barium allergy is declared; the appointment letter carries the specific instructions for each study.
    Duration
    From around a quarter of an hour for a simple barium swallow (some gut studies take images at intervals over several hours) to 1-3 hours for complex interventional procedures. After angiography the person rests in a recovery area while the puncture site settles and usually goes home the same day, though an overnight stay is sometimes needed.
    Radiation
    Highly variable and governed by screening time. The FDA notes that fluoroscopy can deliver relatively high doses, especially in complex interventional procedures that need long fluoroscopy times, whereas the NHS describes the dose from a standard angiogram as small. UKHSA's patient-dose tables place barium studies of the bowel among the higher-dose diagnostic X-ray examinations, and UKHSA runs national dose surveys of fluoroscopy examinations so that departments can compare their practice against national reference levels. Skin can be injured after very prolonged screening.
    Contrast
    Barium for the gut (or a water-soluble alternative if a leak is suspected); iodine for vessels, joints and the urinary tract. Barium is not digested, so stools are paler for a few days and constipation can follow, which is why plenty of fluids are advised afterwards. Iodine contrast can give a brief warm flush or a feeling of needing to pass urine, and because blood vessels do not show on ordinary X-rays no angiogram is possible without it.
    Risks
    Radiation dose that rises with screening time: the FDA identifies two concerns, radiation injury to the skin and underlying tissue (a burn-like effect after long procedures) and a small possibility of a radiation-induced cancer later in life, adding that the probability of either from a single procedure is statistically very small, that the radiation risk is usually far smaller than the other risks of the procedure such as sedation, anaesthesia or the treatment itself, and that where there may be a high dose to the lens of the eye cataract is a further risk. Contrast reactions (a mild itchy rash is the usual form; anaphylaxis is very rare) and a usually temporary strain on the kidneys. For angiography, bruising, soreness and a small lump at the puncture site for days to weeks are common; less often the artery is damaged with internal bleeding that occasionally needs surgical repair, a pseudoaneurysm (a blood-filled bulge at the entry site) forms later and needs treatment, and rarely a heart attack or stroke occurs. Afterwards, bleeding from the puncture site that does not stop with pressure, severe pain, spreading redness or swelling, or a limb that turns pale or cold are reasons to contact the care team urgently.

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.
