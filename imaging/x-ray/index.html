    X-ray

    Also known as: plain radiograph, chest X-ray, CXR, radiograph, plain film

    The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures, chest infections and dental checks.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    How it works

    A brief pulse of X-rays passes through the body onto a digital detector. Dense tissue such as bone absorbs most of the beam and appears white; air absorbs almost none and appears black; soft tissue and fluid are shades of grey in between. The image is a two-dimensional shadow of everything in the beam's path. The person lies or stands so that the body part is positioned against the detector, is asked to keep still and sometimes to hold their breath for a moment, and feels nothing: the exposure lasts a fraction of a second and the whole procedure usually takes a few minutes. The radiographer steps behind a screen to take the image, and machines are set to deliver the smallest amount of radiation needed to produce it; a lead apron or shield may be placed over parts of the body not being imaged, particularly in children. A radiologist or a reporting radiographer then reviews the images and sends a report to the clinician who requested them. Most X-rays are taken in hospital; dental X-rays are usually taken at the dental surgery.

    What it shows

    • Bone: fractures, dislocations, arthritis, bone tumours and infection
    • Chest: pneumonia, fluid, collapsed lung, heart size, tumours
    • Abdomen: bowel obstruction, free air from a perforation, some stones
    • Teeth and jaw
    • Foreign bodies made of metal or glass
    • Dental: bitewing, periapical and panoramic views for decay, impacted wisdom teeth, jaw problems and implant planning; digital dental imaging uses less radiation than older film methods

    Best for

    • A first look at bones and the chest: fast, cheap and available everywhere
    • Following fracture healing
    • Bowel obstruction and perforation as a screen

    Not the right tool for

    • Soft tissues such as ligaments, cartilage, brain and spinal cord
    • Early stress fractures and small bone injuries (MRI or CT)
    • Subtle lung disease (CT)
    • Anyone who is or might be pregnant unless it is an emergency, and repeat imaging in children without a clear clinical reason, since the young are more sensitive to radiation

    What to expect

    Modality
    X-ray (radiography)
    Preparation
    None for a standard X-ray. Jewellery, hair clips, bras and clothing with zips or metal poppers are removed from the area because metal shows on the image, and a gown is provided if needed. Anyone who is or might be pregnant, or who is breastfeeding, tells the radiographer beforehand, as does anyone with allergies or a previous reaction to contrast, since the NHS does not usually recommend X-rays in pregnancy except in an emergency. For contrast studies such as barium examinations the person may be asked to fast for a few hours or to stop certain medicines, and will be told in advance.
    Duration
    A few minutes in the room; the exposure itself lasts a fraction of a second. There are no after-effects from a standard X-ray and normal activities resume straight away; the requesting clinician explains when and how the result will be given.
    Radiation
    Low. UKHSA publishes typical effective doses for each X-ray examination together with the equivalent period of natural background radiation and the associated lifetime risk: a chest film is among the lowest-dose examinations, equivalent to a few days of the background radiation everyone receives from the ground, building materials, food and space, while films of the hip, lumbar spine and pelvis deliver more. UKHSA has run national surveys of patient doses from radiography and fluoroscopy since 1986 and sets national diagnostic reference levels that departments compare themselves against. The FDA notes that a CT delivers a far higher dose than a conventional radiograph.
    Contrast
    Barium swallows and enemas coat the gut to outline it on fluoroscopy; iodine contrast is used for joints (arthrogram) and blood vessels (angiography). For these studies the NHS asks about allergies and any previous reaction to barium or contrast medium, and may ask the person to fast or to stop certain medicines beforehand.
    Risks
    The radiation dose from a single film is tiny and the main theoretical risk is a very slightly increased chance of cancer later in life: X-rays are ionising radiation with enough energy to damage DNA, so the FDA treats every exposure as adding a small amount to lifetime risk while judging that, when an examination is appropriate, the benefit far outweighs it. The risk is lower for a chest, arm or foot X-ray than for a hip, spine or pelvis film, is greater the younger the patient (children and the unborn are more radiosensitive, and women receiving the same dose carry a somewhat higher risk than men), and is managed by justifying each request, keeping doses as low as reasonably achievable and shielding where useful. The abdomen and pelvis are avoided in pregnancy unless the examination is essential. There are no after-effects from a standard X-ray.

    Conditions

    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.

    Symptoms

    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.
    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.
    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
