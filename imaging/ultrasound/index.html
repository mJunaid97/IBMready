    Ultrasound

    Also known as: sonography, ultrasound scan, Doppler, echo, point-of-care ultrasound, POCUS

    High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography), blood vessels and guiding needles.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy

    How it works

    A hand-held probe sends pulses of high-frequency sound into the body and listens for echoes from tissue boundaries. The depth of each echo builds a live moving image. Doppler ultrasound measures the frequency shift from moving blood to show flow direction and speed in colour. It is safe, portable and real-time, but sound cannot cross bone or air. Because the images are captured in real time, the FDA notes, ultrasound also shows movement: the beating heart, bowel and blood flowing through vessels. The scan is usually performed by a sonographer or a radiologist. A clear, water-based gel is spread on the skin so the probe makes good contact, the room is dimmed to make the screen easier to see, and the operator presses and angles the probe and may ask the person to turn on their side, breathe in or hold their breath for a better view. For the pelvis an internal (transvaginal) probe may be offered for more detailed images, with consent and a chaperone present. A report is written afterwards and sent to the doctor who requested the scan; results are not usually given during the appointment, except for some pregnancy scans.

    What it shows

    • Abdomen: gallstones, liver size and texture, kidney obstruction, aortic aneurysm, fluid
    • Pelvis: pregnancy, ovaries and uterus, bladder volume, prostate (transrectal)
    • Vessels: deep vein thrombosis, carotid narrowing, varicose veins, arterial blockages
    • Heart: the echocardiogram
    • Soft tissue: thyroid nodules, breast lumps, lymph nodes, tendons, muscle tears, abscesses
    • Guidance for needles: biopsies, drains, injections, cannulas
    • Pregnancy: how far along the pregnancy is, the fetal heartbeat, the number of babies and, at the NHS 20-week screening scan, the baby's anatomy

    Best for

    • First test for the gallbladder, kidneys, thyroid, scrotum, pregnancy and leg veins
    • Any test that must be repeated or done at the bedside
    • Guiding procedures in real time
    • Children and pregnancy

    Not the right tool for

    • Anything behind bone (brain in adults, spinal cord) or air (lungs beyond the surface, gas-filled bowel)
    • Deep structures in larger patients (image quality falls with depth)
    • A comprehensive survey: it shows what the operator points at, so quality depends on skill
    • Non-medical 'keepsake' fetal videos, which the FDA discourages

    What to expect

    Modality
    Ultrasound
    Preparation
    Depends on the area. For the abdomen, liver or gallbladder the person is usually asked not to eat for several hours beforehand (water is allowed) so the gallbladder is full and the stomach empty; for the pelvis, kidneys and bladder a full bladder is needed, so a set amount of water is drunk before arriving and not passed until after the scan; early pregnancy scans need a full bladder while later ones need none. Scans of the neck, limbs, veins and soft tissue need no preparation. The appointment letter gives the specific instructions.
    Duration
    Typically 10 to 40 minutes depending on the area examined; Doppler studies of blood flow and some pregnancy scans take longer. The gel is wiped off at the end and the person can eat, drink and carry on as normal.
    Radiation
    No ionising radiation. The FDA notes that diagnostic ultrasound has been in medical use for decades and, being non-ionising, does not carry the risks of X-ray-based imaging; the NHS states that standard diagnostic ultrasound has no known harmful effects.
    Contrast
    Microbubble contrast (tiny gas bubbles injected into a vein) improves characterisation of liver and kidney lesions.
    Risks
    None known at diagnostic settings; brief discomfort from probe pressure on a tender area, and some discomfort from a full bladder or an internal probe. The FDA notes that ultrasound can heat tissue slightly and in some cases produce small gas pockets in fluids or tissue (cavitation), that the long-term consequences of these effects are not known, and that it is therefore considered safe when used prudently by trained operators; it advises prudent use in pregnancy and discourages non-medical 'keepsake' fetal videos. The main practical limitation is that image quality depends on the operator and on the person's build.

    Conditions

    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.

    Symptoms

    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone that begins to be made in the days after fertilisation, as the embryo implants in the womb.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.
    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.
    • Chest masculinisation (top surgery) Removal of most of the breast tissue and excess skin, with resizing and repositioning of the nipples, to give a flat chest with a male contour.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.
    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Phalloplasty and metoidioplasty Construction of a penis, most often as gender-affirming surgery for trans men and some non-binary people assigned female at birth.

    Guides and news

    • Bleeding after menopause or between periods Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be checked by a GP.
    • Blood in urine: causes and what happens next Blood in the urine is not usually serious, but the NHS advises seeing a GP every time, even if the amount is small.
    • Found a breast lump: what happens next Most breast lumps are harmless, but the NHS advises seeing a GP about any new lump so it can be checked.
    • Echocardiogram: types, what happens and results An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to picture the heart.
    • Heavy periods: causes, tests and treatment Heavy periods are common and often have no clear cause, but fibroids, endometriosis and other conditions can be behind them.
    • Kidney stones: symptoms and treatment Kidney stones form when chemicals in urine build up; small ones may cause no symptoms, while larger ones can cause severe pain.
    • Pelvic pain in women: causes and when to act Pelvic pain in women often comes from period pain, ovarian cysts or endometriosis, but a few causes are serious.
    • Pregnancy scans and tests on the NHS NHS pregnancy care includes booking blood tests, a dating scan and a mid-pregnancy anomaly scan, plus optional screening for Down's.
    • Swollen ankles and legs: causes and red flags Swollen ankles and legs often come from sitting or standing for long periods, but can signal a clot or a heart, kidney or liver problem.
    • Ultrasound scan: what happens and is it safe An ultrasound scan uses sound waves, not radiation, to make live pictures.
