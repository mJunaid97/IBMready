    MRI scan

    Also known as: magnetic resonance imaging, MR scan, MR angiography, functional MRI, cardiac MRI

    Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord, joints, muscles and organs.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    How it works

    A powerful magnet aligns the hydrogen nuclei in the body's water and fat. Radio pulses knock them out of alignment and, as they relax back, they emit faint signals that differ by tissue type. Different pulse sequences (T1, T2, diffusion, fat suppression) emphasise different properties, giving unrivalled soft-tissue contrast without ionising radiation. The NHS likens the magnet's effect to a compass: lying inside the scanner lines the body's protons up, brief radio pulses knock them out of line, and because protons in different tissues realign at different speeds the returning signals let the computer tell tissues apart. The FDA notes that MRI can image any part of the body in any plane and separates fat, water, muscle and other soft tissue more clearly than CT, which is usually better for bone. The person lies on a narrow table that slides into the tunnel of the scanner, hears loud thumping and knocking during each sequence, and can speak to the radiographer through an intercom at any time; the operator can see and hear them throughout.

    What it shows

    • Brain and spinal cord: stroke within minutes (diffusion), multiple sclerosis, tumours, epilepsy foci, dementia patterns
    • Spine: disc herniation, nerve compression, infection, cord injury
    • Joints: cartilage, menisci, ligaments, tendons, bone marrow oedema (early stress fractures)
    • Heart: muscle scar, cardiomyopathy, congenital disease
    • Liver, pancreas, bile ducts (MRCP), prostate, pelvis, breast

    Best for

    • Any question about the brain, spinal cord or nerves
    • Ligament, cartilage and tendon injuries of the knee, shoulder and ankle
    • Characterising liver, pancreatic and prostate lesions
    • Children and pregnancy, where radiation is avoided

    Not the right tool for

    • Emergencies needing speed (a scan takes 20-60 minutes and the environment is hard to monitor)
    • Lungs and calcified structures (few hydrogen nuclei; CT is better)
    • People with certain implants, severe claustrophobia, or who cannot lie still
    • People who cannot be screened reliably for metal or implants, since the static field pulls ferromagnetic objects with force and some implants may heat, move or malfunction

    What to expect

    Modality
    MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)
    Preparation
    A detailed safety questionnaire about implants, metal fragments, pacemakers and previous surgery; all metal removed; ear protection for the loud knocking. Fasting for some abdominal scans. Everything metal comes off before entering the scan room: jewellery, hearing aids, watches, phones, keys, coins and wallets, and clothing with metal poppers or zips is swapped for a gown. The team is told in advance about any pacemaker or implant, metal fragments such as shrapnel or metal in the eye, kidney disease (relevant if gadolinium contrast is planned), tattoos (older inks may contain metal that heats or pulls) and any possibility of pregnancy. People who fear enclosed spaces say so beforehand: a sedative, or an open scanner, may be offered.
    Duration
    20-60 minutes lying still inside a tube; each sequence lasts a few minutes. Longer examinations deposit more radiofrequency energy, so scan time is itself part of safety planning.
    Radiation
    No ionising radiation. The NHS notes there is no conclusive evidence that exposure to the scanner's magnetic field causes harm or side effects, and the FDA states that there are no known cumulative risks from routine MRI, while adding that the long-term effects of very high field strengths are not yet known.
    Contrast
    Gadolinium, injected into a vein, highlights tumours, inflammation and blood vessels; rare allergic reactions. Kidney function is asked about beforehand because gadolinium is avoided or used cautiously in severe kidney disease.
    Risks
    Ferromagnetic objects become projectiles; older pacemakers, cochlear implants, aneurysm clips and metal in the eye can be dangerous. Gadolinium contrast is avoided in severe kidney disease and pregnancy. Claustrophobia is the commonest problem. The FDA lists further hazards that screening and good practice are designed to prevent: anything ferromagnetic, from keys to oxygen cylinders, becomes a projectile in the static field; devices or materials that conduct electricity (metallic objects, pulse oximeter leads, ECG leads and some tattoo pigments) can cause burns; radiofrequency energy warms the body, more so in long scans; the rapidly switching gradient fields are loud enough to damage hearing without ear protection and can produce a harmless light-touch or twitching sensation on the skin; and some people feel transient dizziness, nausea, headache, a metallic taste or flashes of light that fade on leaving the magnet. Scanners also hold large volumes of liquid helium, so scan rooms are fitted with oxygen monitors. Pregnant people tell the team before scanning so that the need for the scan can be weighed.

    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Breast lump A new lump, or an area of thickening that feels different from the rest of the breast, anywhere in the breast tissue.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.
    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.
