    PET-CT scan

    Also known as: positron emission tomography, PET scan, FDG-PET, nuclear medicine scan, bone scan, SPECT

    Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active, usually combined with CT to stage cancer and check treatment response.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    How it works

    A tiny amount of a radioactive tracer is injected and the scanner detects where it accumulates. The commonest tracer, FDG, is a glucose analogue, so tissues that burn a lot of sugar (most cancers, infection, inflammation, the brain and heart) light up. The PET image is fused with a CT taken at the same time to show exactly where the activity sits. Other nuclear medicine scans use tracers that go to bone, the thyroid, the heart muscle or the kidneys. MedlinePlus describes PET as a nuclear imaging test that shows how well tissues and organs are working rather than only what they look like, and the National Cancer Institute defines a nuclear medicine scan as one in which a radioactive substance locates and binds to specific cells or tissues, including cancer cells, so that a camera can map it. FDG is labelled with fluorine-18, which emits positrons; the scanner detects the resulting radiation and a computer turns it into three-dimensional pictures. Because tumours usually metabolise glucose faster than normal tissue they take up more FDG and stand out, but inflamed tissue also accumulates it, so the scan is read alongside the CT and the clinical history. During the scan the person lies on a narrow table that moves slowly through the ring of the scanner and must keep very still, since movement blurs the images; an intercom allows them to speak to staff at any time. Nuclear medicine also treats disease: radioactive substances are used to treat some thyroid cancers and lymphomas.

    What it shows

    • Cancer: staging, finding spread, judging response to treatment, distinguishing scar from active tumour
    • Infection and inflammation of unknown source, vasculitis, infected grafts
    • Brain: dementia patterns, epilepsy foci
    • Heart: viable versus scarred muscle
    • Bone scan: metastases, stress fractures, infection, Paget disease
    • Thyroid uptake scan: overactive nodules versus thyroiditis
    • Cancer in practice: in non-small-cell lung cancer, integrated PET-CT has become a standard test for staging the primary tumour, lymph nodes and distant spread, and for assessing response to treatment

    Best for

    • Whole-body cancer staging in lung cancer, lymphoma, melanoma, oesophageal and head and neck cancer
    • Deciding whether a lung nodule is likely malignant
    • Fever of unknown origin

    Not the right tool for

    • Cancers with low glucose uptake (some prostate, kidney, low-grade and well-differentiated tumours; other tracers are used)
    • Very small tumours, for which sensitivity falls sharply, partly because of breathing movement during the scan
    • Brain metastases (the brain glows with normal uptake; MRI instead)
    • Telling cancer from inflammation on its own, since FDG also accumulates in inflamed and infected tissue
    • Uncontrolled diabetes, which spoils FDG uptake

    What to expect

    Modality
    PET (positron emission tomography)
    Preparation
    Fast for 6 hours, drinking only water, avoid exercise for 24 hours, keep warm; blood glucose checked, and people with diabetes may need their medicines adjusted because they interfere with the result. Metal items such as jewellery are left off. About an hour before the scan the tracer is injected into a vein in the arm or hand (a brief sting from the needle; the scan itself is painless) and the person then rests quietly while it distributes; some centres ask them not to read, talk or use a phone during this period because muscle activity changes where the tracer goes.
    Duration
    About 2-3 hours in the department. The tracer is given about an hour before imaging; the NHS puts the scan itself at around 30 to 60 minutes, with the person lying still for about 30 minutes of that. Results usually reach the referring doctor within 1 to 2 weeks after a radiologist has reported the images.
    Radiation
    Moderate. MedlinePlus puts the radiation from a PET scan at about the same amount as most CT scans, and the CT component adds to it (a low-dose localising CT contributes less than a full diagnostic CT). The NHS describes the tracer as a small amount of radiation that drops to a safe level quickly and has gone from the body within a few hours; it leaves in the urine and stool, which is why fluids are encouraged afterwards.
    Contrast
    The radiotracer itself; the CT component may be low-dose without contrast or a full diagnostic scan with iodine.
    Risks
    Radiation as above; the NHS and MedlinePlus advise limiting close contact with pregnant women and small children for a period after the tracer, usually up to a day. Allergic reactions to FDG are very rare. There is no recovery time unless a sedative was given, and most people go home soon after the scan. As with any sensitive test, uptake in inflammation or infection can mimic cancer and lead to further tests.

    Conditions

    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Cervical cancer A malignant tumour of the cervix, the neck of the uterus, that develops almost always from persistent infection with high-risk types of human…
    • Lymphoma (Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin) Lymphoma is a cancer of the lymphatic system, and doctors group it into Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
    • Pancreatic cancer Pancreatic cancer is a cancer that starts in the pancreas, an organ that helps with digestion.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.

    Medical tests

    • Biopsy Removal of a small piece of tissue or a few cells so a pathologist can examine them under the microscope.

    Imaging

    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
