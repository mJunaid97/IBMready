    Reflexes

    Fast, automatic responses wired through the spinal cord or brainstem that protect the body before the brain has time to decide.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrainstem anatomy

    The knee-jerk is the simplest: tapping the patellar tendon stretches the quadriceps, a sensory neuron fires straight onto a motor neuron in the spinal cord, and the muscle contracts. Withdrawal from a hot object uses one more neuron and also inhibits the opposing muscles. Brainstem reflexes include the pupil's response to light, the gag and cough reflexes and the baroreceptor reflex that steadies blood pressure.

    Clinicians grade tendon reflexes because they map the nervous system: a lost ankle reflex points to the S1 nerve root, brisk reflexes with a spreading pattern point to damage above the spinal cord, as after a stroke.

    Reflexes can be modulated: the brain can suppress a withdrawal to keep hold of a hot cup, and spinal cord injury releases reflexes from that control, producing spasticity.

    The textbook example of a withdrawal reflex is a hand touching a hot stove. Heat receptors in the skin fire, the signal enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root and excites a motor neuron in the ventral horn, and that neuron drives the biceps to flex the elbow and pull the hand away. A collateral branch of the same sensory fibre inhibits the motor neurons of the triceps so that the antagonist does not fight the movement, and in the legs a crossed extensor reflex stiffens the opposite limb so that the person does not fall when one foot is lifted from a sharp object. The stretch reflex works the other way round, resisting change: when a muscle is lengthened, its muscle spindle fires onto the motor neuron of that same muscle, which contracts, a loop that holds posture steady and is what a tap on the patellar tendon reveals. These are the simplest circuits in the somatic nervous system, needing only one sensory fibre entering the cord and one motor neuron leaving it.

    Testing uses a reflex hammer swung in an arc onto the tendon while the limb is relaxed; the knee and ankle reflexes are the most often examined in the legs, with biceps, triceps and supinator in the arms. The examiner compares left with right and arms with legs, because the pattern tells more than any single response: diminished or absent reflexes suggest a problem in the peripheral nerves or roots, whereas exaggerated reflexes point to damage above the spinal motor neurons, as in stroke, spinal cord injury or compression of the cord in the neck. The assessment is qualitative, judged by eye, and depends on technique and positioning; a tense patient, an awkward posture or a thick limb can all blunt the response and mislead.

    Reflex testing is therefore interpreted alongside power, tone and sensation rather than alone, and it has a wider role: the pupillary light and corneal reflexes test the brainstem in unconscious patients, and the absence of brainstem reflexes forms part of the criteria for diagnosing death by neurological criteria.
