    Gut motility

    Coordinated waves of smooth muscle contraction (peristalsis) move contents along the tract, run by the gut's own nervous system and tuned by the vagus nerve and hormones.

    Open in the 3D explorerOesophagus anatomy

    Swallowing is voluntary at the start, then reflex: the oesophagus carries a bolus to the stomach in about ten seconds, and the lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes to let it through and then closes to keep acid out. The stomach mixes with slow waves three times a minute; the small intestine segments to mix and propels to move; between meals a housekeeping wave sweeps residue toward the colon every 90 minutes or so.

    The colon moves contents slowly, with a few strong mass movements a day, often after meals, that push stool into the rectum and trigger the urge to defecate. The internal anal sphincter relaxes automatically; the external sphincter and puborectalis are under voluntary control.

    Motility disorders cause reflux, gastroparesis, constipation or irritable bowel symptoms; a mechanical block (obstruction) produces cramping pain, vomiting and absence of gas.

    Two patterns of movement serve different purposes. Peristalsis propels: in the stomach the mixing waves begin gently near the top and grow stronger as they travel toward the pylorus, softening food and blending it with gastric juice into chyme. Segmentation mixes without propelling: rings of smooth muscle in the small intestine alternately contract and relax, moving contents back and forth, blending chyme with digestive juices and pressing it against the mucosa for absorption; it is most rapid in the duodenum and slower in the ileum. Between meals the small intestine switches to migrating motility complexes, the housekeeping waves that clear residue. Slowing transit is deliberate: segmentation and a reduced rate of peristalsis buy time for digestion and absorption, and sympathetic stimulation, as in anxiety, slows digestive activity further, while parasympathetic (vagal) activity speeds secretion and peristalsis.

    Gastroparesis is the clearest motility disorder: movement of food from the stomach into the small intestine slows or stops although nothing is physically blocking it. Diabetes is the most common known cause, with surgery another, but in most people no cause is identified. Those affected feel full soon after starting a meal and long after finishing it, with nausea, vomiting, bloating, belching, upper abdominal pain, heartburn and a poor appetite; weight loss without trying signals malnutrition, and in diabetes erratic stomach emptying makes blood glucose swing unpredictably.

    Motility is assessed by measuring how fast contents move. Clinicians combine the history, examination and symptoms with tests of stomach emptying, usually a scintigraphy study in which a labelled meal is tracked through the stomach, and a diagnosis of gastroparesis requires both delayed emptying and moderate to severe symptoms, because many of the symptoms are non-specific and can be mistaken for indigestion. Upper endoscopy is used first to exclude an obstruction, and wireless motility capsules are used in research to time transit through the whole gut. Guidance advises seeking care for prolonged vomiting or signs of dehydration or malnutrition.

    Conditions

    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.

    Symptoms

    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.

    Drug classes

    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.

    Guides and news

    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
