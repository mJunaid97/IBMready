    The cardiac cycle

    One heartbeat is a sequence of filling and ejection: diastole, when the ventricles relax and fill, and systole, when they contract and pump.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Each cycle lasts about 0.8 seconds at rest. During diastole the mitral and tricuspid valves are open and blood flows passively from the atria into the ventricles; the atria then contract to add the final fifth. Ventricular systole begins as pressure rises, the mitral and tricuspid valves snap shut (the first heart sound, 'lub'), and once ventricular pressure exceeds aortic pressure the aortic and pulmonary valves open and blood is ejected.

    As the ventricles relax, pressure falls, the aortic and pulmonary valves close (the second heart sound, 'dub') and the cycle repeats. The valves make flow one-way; the papillary muscles and chordae tendineae stop the mitral and tricuspid leaflets bulging back into the atria.

    The fraction of the end-diastolic volume that is ejected, the ejection fraction, is normally 55 to 70% and is the everyday measure of how well the left ventricle pumps.

    Each half of the cycle begins with a phase in which the ventricle changes pressure but not volume. In isovolumetric contraction, the first phase of systole, the rising pressure slams the atrioventricular valves shut but has not yet opened the semilunar valves, so no blood leaves; ejection follows once ventricular pressure exceeds that in the aorta and pulmonary trunk. Diastole likewise opens with isovolumic relaxation, when all four valves are closed and pressure falls until the atrioventricular valves reopen. The ventricle never empties completely: the volume left after ejection is the end-systolic volume, and stroke volume is the difference between the end-diastolic and end-systolic volumes. The two heart sounds mark the valve closures: S1 (lub) is the atrioventricular valves shutting at the start of systole and S2 (dub) is the semilunar valves shutting at the start of diastole, which is why a murmur heard between S1 and S2 arises during ejection.

    Cardiac output is stroke volume multiplied by heart rate, and stroke volume depends on three things: preload, the stretch on the ventricle before it contracts (another expression of end-diastolic volume, set by filling time and venous return); contractility, the force of the contraction itself, which the autonomic nerves and hormones such as adrenaline increase; and afterload, the pressure the ventricle must overcome to open the semilunar valves, which rises with high blood pressure. In exercise, heart rate and stroke volume both increase, so output rises several-fold in a healthy young adult; in heart failure the heart cannot pump enough blood for the body's needs, either because the weakened ventricle ejects a reduced fraction or because a stiff ventricle fills poorly while ejecting a preserved fraction, the latter driven mainly by high blood pressure and other conditions that make the heart work harder.

    The cycle is assessed non-invasively with echocardiography, which uses sound waves to produce moving images showing the size and shape of the heart, how well it is pumping and how the valves open and close; it is the test that produces the ejection fraction and classifies heart failure as reduced or preserved. Blood levels of brain natriuretic peptide, released by stretched ventricles, rise in heart failure, and cardiac MRI, CT or nuclear scans give further measures of pumping when needed. Faulty valves, an irregular rhythm and inherited or congenital heart disease are among the structural causes that these tests uncover.

    Conditions

    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.

    Medical tests

    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.

    Biomarkers

    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Procedures

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
