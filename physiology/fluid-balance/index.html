    Fluid balance

    Water in equals water out: thirst, antidiuretic hormone and the kidneys keep the body's 40 litres of fluid within a litre of its set point.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    An adult is about 60% water: two thirds inside cells, the rest in the tissue spaces and in blood plasma. Intake of about 2.5 litres a day (drink, food and metabolism) matches loss in urine, sweat, breath and stool. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus sense a rise in blood concentration of only 1 to 2% and trigger both thirst and antidiuretic hormone, which makes the kidneys retain water.

    Volume is sensed separately by stretch receptors in the heart and pressure receptors in the arteries; a fall activates the sympathetic system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone cascade, which retains salt and water and constricts vessels. Heart failure and cirrhosis activate these pathways inappropriately and cause swelling (oedema).

    Dehydration presents with thirst, dark urine, dizziness on standing and a rising heart rate; in infants and the elderly it can progress quickly.

    The water-conserving loop runs through the posterior pituitary. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus are especially sensitive to the concentration of sodium and other solutes; when osmolarity rises, during dehydration or after a very salty meal, they trigger thirst and signal the posterior pituitary to release antidiuretic hormone (ADH). ADH acts on the principal cells lining the collecting ducts, inserting aquaporin water channels into their membranes so that water leaves the filtrate osmotically and the urine becomes concentrated; when ADH falls, fewer channels are inserted and the urine is dilute. The loop is self-correcting: as osmolarity falls back, the osmoreceptors reduce ADH secretion. Angiotensin II, generated when blood volume or pressure is low, adds a second drive to the thirst centre. The system fails in diabetes insipidus, where chronic under-production of ADH leaves a person constantly thirsty yet unable to retain the water they drink, and it is suppressed by alcohol, which inhibits ADH release and increases urine output, part of the mechanism of a hangover.

    Dehydration arises when intake does not keep pace with losses from diarrhoea, vomiting, heavy sweating, fever or excessive urination, the last often caused by diabetes or diuretic medicines; heat, heavy alcohol use and old age raise the risk, because some people lose their sense of thirst as they age. Adults notice thirst, dark yellow urine passed less often, dizziness or light-headedness, headache, tiredness and a dry mouth, lips and eyes. In babies the signs are a sunken fontanelle, sunken eyes, few or no tears when crying, fewer wet nappies and drowsiness or irritability. Severe dehydration is life-threatening and shows as confusion, a rapid heartbeat and breathing, shock and little or no urine.

    Assessment is mostly clinical. Examination looks for low blood pressure or a fall in pressure on standing, slow capillary refill in the fingertips and skin that springs back slowly when pinched; a blood or urine test checks the balance of salts. NHS guidance advises urgent GP or NHS 111 contact for unusual tiredness, dizziness on standing that does not settle, very dark or much reduced urine or a fast heart rate or breathing, and an emergency call when skin is pale, grey or blue and cold, breathing is difficult or the person is confused or hard to wake.

    Conditions

    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
