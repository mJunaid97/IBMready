    Electrolytes

    Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride and bicarbonate carry charge, set the water distribution between compartments and make nerves and muscles excitable.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    Sodium is the main ion outside cells and, with its water, sets blood volume; the kidneys and aldosterone keep its concentration near 140 mmol/L. Potassium is concentrated inside cells; its blood level, normally 3.5 to 5, must stay in a narrow band because both high and low values disturb the heart's rhythm.

    Calcium is regulated by parathyroid hormone and vitamin D and is needed for muscle contraction, clotting and nerve signalling; low levels cause tingling and cramps, high levels stones, bone pain and confusion. Magnesium is a cofactor for hundreds of enzymes and follows potassium.

    Vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics, kidney disease and hormone disorders are the usual causes of imbalance, detected on a urea and electrolytes (U&E) blood test.

    Six ions do most of the work: sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate, calcium and phosphate. Between them they set nerve and muscle excitability, control the release of hormones, govern the permeability of cell membranes, buffer the body fluids and determine where water sits between the compartments. Sodium, the dominant cation outside cells, accounts for about half of the osmotic pressure difference between the inside and outside of cells, so where sodium goes, water follows; Western diets supply far more sodium chloride than the body needs. Potassium is kept high inside cells by the sodium-potassium pump, which spends ATP to move sodium out and potassium in. The kidney is the main site of regulation: aldosterone and angiotensin II control the exchange of sodium for potassium in the collecting tubule, and the kidneys excrete most of the ions the body needs to lose, with smaller amounts leaving in sweat and faeces.

    Calcium behaves differently because most of the body's store is locked in bone, which acts as a mineral reserve for the rest of the tissues. In blood a little more than half of the calcium is bound to proteins and the remainder is ionised, and it is the ionised fraction that supports muscle contraction, enzyme activity and clotting. Parathyroid hormone, calcitriol (active vitamin D) and calcitonin together regulate calcium and phosphate. Bicarbonate is the chief buffer of the extracellular fluid, and chloride helps maintain blood volume and pressure.

    The electrolyte panel, a routine blood test, measures sodium, potassium, chloride and bicarbonate; magnesium and calcium are measured separately or as part of a wider metabolic panel, and the anion gap, calculated from the panel, helps identify the cause of a metabolic acidosis. An imbalance simply means that one or more electrolytes is too high or too low, and it usually follows a change in body water, since in health the water taken in matches the water lost. Heavy sweating during prolonged exercise or heat exposure, vomiting and diarrhoea, diuretics and kidney or hormonal disease all upset the balance, and because potassium and calcium alter cardiac and neuromuscular excitability, severe derangements are treated as emergencies and monitored with an ECG.

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Electrolytes.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.

    Medical tests

    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.

    Biomarkers

    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
