    Absorption

    Nutrients cross the lining of the small intestine into blood and lymph; the colon reclaims water and salts.

    Open in the 3D explorerSmall intestine anatomy

    The small intestine's folds, finger-like villi and microscopic microvilli multiply its surface area to around 30 square metres. Sugars and amino acids are pumped into the cells and pass into capillaries that drain to the portal vein and the liver. Fats are rebuilt inside the cells into chylomicrons and enter the lymphatic lacteals, bypassing the liver at first.

    Iron and calcium are absorbed mainly in the duodenum, most other nutrients in the jejunum, and vitamin B12 and bile salts only in the terminal ileum, which is why disease or surgery there causes specific deficiencies.

    About nine litres of fluid enter the gut daily (drink plus secretions); the small intestine absorbs most and the colon nearly all the rest, leaving about 100 ml in stool. Diarrhoea is a failure of this reclamation.

    Absorption depends on chemical digestion having gone first. Enzymes split large molecules by hydrolysis into subunits small enough to cross the lining: pancreatic proteases, amylases and lipases break down proteins, starches and fats respectively, and enzymes anchored in the brush border of the intestinal cells finish the job, lactase for example splitting lactose into glucose and galactose. Bile salts emulsify fat into tiny droplets so that lipase has a far larger surface to work on. Most nutrients then cross the apical surface of the enterocyte through specific transport proteins; lipids, the fat-soluble vitamins and most water-soluble vitamins are the exceptions and use other routes. The folds, villi and microvilli give the small intestine an absorbing surface roughly six hundred times its outer surface, and a large complex meal takes about nine hours to pass through it.

    The stomach absorbs very little apart from some drugs such as alcohol and aspirin, and the large intestine secretes mucus but no digestive enzymes, so whatever chemical breakdown happens there is the work of colonic bacteria. Nerves, hormones, bacteria and the blood supply all coordinate the process: hormones released from the gut lining pace the delivery of enzymes and bile, and the rich capillary and lymphatic networks beneath the epithelium carry the products away.

    Clinically, absorption is judged indirectly. Weight loss, pale bulky stools, anaemia and specific deficiencies point to malabsorption, and the pattern of deficiency indicates the site: iron and folate problems suggest the upper small bowel, vitamin B12 the terminal ileum. Coeliac disease flattens the villi, pancreatic insufficiency removes the enzymes, and resection or Crohn's disease of the ileum removes the only site for B12 and bile salt uptake. Blood tests for the nutrients concerned, faecal fat or elastase measurements and endoscopic biopsy of the duodenum are the usual investigations.

    Conditions

    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.
    • 25-hydroxyvitamin D The storage form of vitamin D that the liver makes, the best single measure of vitamin D status and the value the vitamin D test reports.

    Procedures

    • Colonoscopy A flexible camera is passed around the whole large intestine after laxative bowel preparation to find and remove polyps.

    Medications

    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).
