    Electrical conduction of the heart

    A wiring system of specialised muscle cells spreads each heartbeat from the sinoatrial node through the atria, the atrioventricular node and the bundle branches to the ventricles.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    The impulse starts in the sinoatrial node, sweeps across both atria (the P wave of the ECG) and reaches the atrioventricular node, which delays it for about a tenth of a second so the atria finish emptying. It then races down the bundle of His and the right and left bundle branches to the Purkinje fibres, depolarising the ventricles from the apex upward (the QRS complex). Recovery of the ventricles produces the T wave.

    Because the wave follows a fixed path, its timing on an ECG reveals blocks (a long PR interval or a bundle branch block), extra pathways and the chaotic atrial activity of atrial fibrillation. Damaged muscle after a heart attack changes the shape of the QRS and ST segments.

    Pacemakers replace a failing sinoatrial node or a blocked atrioventricular node; ablation destroys tissue that generates abnormal rhythms.

    Cardiac muscle is autorhythmic: unlike skeletal or smooth muscle it generates its own electrical signal at a set rate. Two kinds of cell share the work. Conductive cells, a small minority, set the rate and spread the impulse; contractile cells do the pumping. The pacemaker cells of the sinoatrial node never rest at a stable voltage: a slow inward leak of sodium (the prepotential) drifts the membrane toward threshold, rapid calcium entry then fires the action potential and potassium exit resets it, so the node fires rhythmically without any external command. The heart nonetheless receives neural and endocrine input: sympathetic nerves and adrenaline steepen the prepotential and speed the rate during exercise, stress or fever, while parasympathetic (vagal) activity slows it at rest and in sleep, which is why heart rate is a sensitive barometer of the body's state.

    Disorders fall into two groups. In arrhythmias the heart beats too fast (tachycardia), too slowly (bradycardia) or irregularly, because abnormal extra signals arise, signals are blocked or slowed, or they travel along new pathways; many are harmless, but atrial fibrillation, in which the atria beat chaotically and out of step with the ventricles, is the commonest serious type, and ventricular fibrillation stops effective pumping and causes sudden death unless a shock is delivered immediately. In conduction disorders (heart block) the signal is not produced properly or does not travel through the heart as it should; in a bundle branch block the signal travels more slowly down one side than the other. Inherited channel disorders such as long QT syndrome arise from ion channels that work poorly or are too few, and are unmasked by low potassium, magnesium or calcium and by medicines including some antibiotics and antidepressants.

    The electrocardiogram remains the central test because it records the electrical activity directly. Intermittent symptoms may need several ECGs over days or weeks or a Holter monitor worn continuously; an exercise stress test shows how the rhythm behaves when the heart is working hard; blood tests check electrolytes and thyroid hormone, which alter conduction; an echocardiogram looks at the heart's size and structure, since damage from high blood pressure or coronary disease underlies many arrhythmias.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Ambulatory ECG monitoring A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during…
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.

    Biomarkers

    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.

    Procedures

    • Cardiac catheterisation Thin catheters passed from the wrist or groin to the heart measure chamber pressures, film the coronary arteries with contrast dye and.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.
