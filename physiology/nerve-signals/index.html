    Nerve signals

    Information travels as brief electrical pulses, action potentials, whose rate carries the message; at synapses it becomes chemical.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    An action potential is a wave of sodium ions rushing into the axon followed by potassium flowing out, restoring the resting state within a millisecond. Because each pulse is identical, a stronger stimulus is signalled by more pulses per second, not bigger ones.

    Sensory signals travel from receptors through the spinal cord and thalamus to the cortex; motor commands travel down the corticospinal tract, cross to the opposite side in the medulla, and leave through spinal nerves. This crossing is why a stroke in the left hemisphere weakens the right side of the body.

    Local anaesthetics block sodium channels so pain signals never start; multiple sclerosis strips myelin so signals slow or fail; nerve compression, as in carpal tunnel syndrome, produces tingling and numbness along the nerve's territory.

    The machinery is a set of channel and pump proteins in the membrane. The sodium-potassium pump, an ATPase, spends energy to move sodium out of the cell and potassium in, holding sodium high outside and potassium high inside; passive channels then let the ions leak back selectively, and it is the combination of pumps and channels that creates the resting potential and makes the action potential possible. Some channels are non-specific and pass any small cation, while the voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels that generate the spike open and close in a fixed sequence in response to the membrane voltage itself, which is why the impulse is all-or-nothing and travels in one direction.

    A neuron has a cell body containing the nucleus, branching dendrites that receive messages and a single axon that carries them onward; axons range from less than a hair's width to the length of the spinal cord, and a fatty myelin sheath insulates them so that signals travel faster and farther. At the end of the axon the electrical signal must become chemical. The arriving impulse makes small sacs called vesicles release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft; the molecules cross the gap and bind receptors on the next cell, which is either excited or suppressed depending on the receptor. Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter in the brain, acetylcholine drives muscle contraction, and inhibitory transmitters damp activity. Synapses are not fixed: their strength changes with recent use, and this plasticity underlies learning and memory. The adult brain contains more than 80 billion neurons communicating across trillions of synapses.

    Clinically, conduction is tested by nerve conduction studies, which stimulate a nerve electrically and time the response, and by electromyography; slowed conduction points to loss of myelin, as in Guillain-Barré syndrome, while reduced signal size points to loss of axons. Fever, low sodium or calcium and many drugs change excitability, which is why confusion, cramps or seizures can accompany metabolic illness.

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Nerve signals.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Herniated (slipped) disc Protrusion of the soft nucleus of an intervertebral disc through its fibrous outer ring, most often at the lower lumbar levels.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Symptoms

    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Medications

    • Amitriptyline Amitriptyline is a prescription-only medicine from a group called tricyclic antidepressants, used for nerve pain and to help prevent migraine.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Gabapentin A prescription medicine used to treat epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • Mirtazapine Mirtazapine is an antidepressant, prescribed mainly for depression and sometimes for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and anxiety.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Pregabalin Pregabalin is a prescription medicine used for nerve pain, epilepsy and generalised anxiety disorder.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

    Drug classes

    • Gabapentinoids Gabapentinoids are the group that contains pregabalin and gabapentin, which are used for epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • NaSSA antidepressants The NHS groups antidepressants into types such as SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and MAOIs.
    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.
    • SNRIs SNRIs are a group of antidepressants whose NHS examples are duloxetine and venlafaxine.
    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
    • Tricyclic antidepressants Tricyclic antidepressants are a group of medicines that includes amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine and clomipramine.

    Guides and news

    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
