    Liver functions

    The liver processes everything absorbed from the gut, stores and releases fuel, makes most blood proteins, detoxifies drugs and produces bile.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy

    Portal blood arrives loaded with nutrients and anything else absorbed. Liver cells store glucose as glycogen and release it between meals, make new glucose from amino acids when needed, build and break down fats and cholesterol, and manufacture albumin and the clotting factors. Ammonia from protein breakdown is converted to urea for the kidneys to excrete.

    Enzyme systems (the cytochrome P450 family) transform drugs, alcohol and toxins into water-soluble forms for excretion; this is why liver disease changes drug doses and why paracetamol overdose is so dangerous. Old red cells are broken down and their haem becomes bilirubin, which the liver conjugates and excretes in bile; when this fails, bilirubin accumulates and the skin turns yellow (jaundice).

    Bile, about half a litre a day, is stored in the gallbladder and released to emulsify dietary fat. The liver can regenerate after losing more than half its mass.

    Structurally the liver is a filter placed in series with the gut. Nutrient-rich venous blood from the small intestine drains into the hepatic portal vein, which enters the liver at the porta hepatis alongside the hepatic artery; both empty into the hepatic sinusoids, leaky capillaries running between rows of hepatocytes, so that most absorbed nutrients, other than fats carried in lymph, pass a liver cell before reaching the general circulation. Hepatocytes make up most of the organ's volume, and the bile they secrete is collected in tiny canaliculi between them before flowing out through the bile ducts to the duodenum, with the gallbladder storing and concentrating it between meals.

    The liver switches role with each meal. In the absorptive state, insulin drives glucose into storage as glycogen in liver and muscle and converts the excess to fat; in the post-absorptive state, as blood glucose falls, glucagon from the alpha cells of the pancreas makes the liver break glycogen back down to glucose (glycogenolysis) and, if fasting continues until glycogen is exhausted, to make new glucose and fuel from fatty acids and amino acids. This buffering is why prolonged exercise or a missed meal does not cause hypoglycaemia in a healthy person, and why liver failure and some glycogen storage disorders do.

    Liver function is assessed by blood tests that read these jobs. Bilirubin, the yellow pigment from red cell breakdown, rises when the liver cannot process it or the bile ducts are blocked, and produces jaundice; a raised unconjugated fraction points to haemolysis or impaired conjugation, as in the harmless Gilbert's syndrome, whereas a raised conjugated fraction suggests liver cell disease or biliary obstruction. Albumin falls when the liver's protein synthesis is significantly impaired, but it is an unreliable gauge of severity because sepsis, inflammation, kidney protein loss, malabsorption and malnutrition lower it too. Clotting tests (prothrombin time or INR) lengthen when fewer clotting proteins are made or vitamin K is lacking, which is why clotting is checked before invasive procedures such as liver biopsy. The enzymes ALT and AST signal liver cell injury while ALP and GGT point to bile flow, and a low platelet count is a common sign of advanced chronic disease. An abnormal result is not a diagnosis: it is interpreted against previous results, history and current illness, since conditions outside the liver also change these values.

    Conditions

    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Hepatitis B tests A set of blood tests that shows whether someone has hepatitis B now, had it in the past, is immune from vaccination, or carries the virus long term.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) A membrane enzyme of bile-duct and liver cells that rises with cholestasis, alcohol and many medicines.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.

    Procedures

    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.

    Medications

    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Simvastatin Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes.
    • Warfarin The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

    Drug classes

    • Biguanides Metformin is the only biguanide in routine use and the first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it lowers glucose without causing hypoglycaemia or…
    • Non-opioid analgesics First-line painkillers without opioid or anti-inflammatory action: paracetamol (acetaminophen) above all.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.

    Guides and news

    • Alcohol units: the UK low-risk drinking advice The UK Chief Medical Officers advise adults not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.
