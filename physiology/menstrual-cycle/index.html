    The menstrual cycle

    A roughly monthly cycle in which one ovary matures and releases an egg while the uterus builds a lining and then sheds it as a period if no pregnancy follows, timed throughout by hormones.

    Open in the 3D explorerOvaries & fallopian tubes anatomy

    The menstrual cycle is the roughly monthly sequence in which one ovary matures and releases an egg while the uterus prepares a lining and then sheds it if no pregnancy occurs. Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, and the average cycle is about 28 days: the NHS counts anything from 21 to 40 days as normal as long as the pattern is regular for that person. Two cycles run in step, one in the ovary and one in the uterus, and both are driven by the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis: the hypothalamus releases gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses, the pituitary answers with follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH), and the ovary with oestradiol, progesterone and inhibin, which feed back on both.

    In the follicular phase the FSH rise that follows the hormone fall at the end of the previous cycle recruits a group of follicles, whose granulosa cells make oestradiol; rising oestradiol and inhibin then suppress FSH, so only the most sensitive follicle survives. When oestradiol stays high for a couple of days the feedback turns positive, the pituitary releases a surge of LH and the follicle ruptures about a day later: ovulation, around day 14 of a 28-day cycle. The emptied follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which makes progesterone for a fairly constant 14 days, so differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase. Without fertilisation it degenerates and both hormones fall; if an embryo implants, its human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) keeps it working, so a missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy.

    The uterus follows the ovary. Falling progesterone constricts the spiral arteries of the endometrium, so the functional layer becomes ischaemic and is shed as the menstrual flow, usually over about 5 days, with 3 to 8 days counted as normal; bleeding that interferes with daily life counts as heavy, whatever the measured volume. Oestradiol then regrows the lining from the basal layer through the proliferative phase and makes the cervical mucus clear and stretchy at ovulation. In the secretory phase progesterone makes the glands secrete glycogen-rich fluid ready for implantation, thickens the mucus again and raises the basal body temperature slightly. Period pain comes from prostaglandins released by the shedding lining, which make the myometrium contract.

    Not every cycle is ovulatory: anovulatory cycles are common in the first year or two after the first period (menarche) and again in the perimenopause, and are often irregular and sometimes heavy, because oestradiol builds the lining with no progesterone to stabilise it. Hormonal contraception acts on the same axis: combined methods suppress the LH surge so that no follicle ovulates, and the bleed in the hormone-free interval is a withdrawal bleed rather than a true period, while progestogen-only methods thicken the cervical mucus and thin the lining, and some also stop ovulation. Testosterone taken by trans men and non-binary people usually stops periods within a few months, but it is not contraception, and bleeding that returns needs assessment.

    Key facts

    FactValue
    Cycle lengthabout 28 days; regular cycles of 21–40 days are normal
    Ovulationaround day 14 of a 28-day cycle, about a day after the LH surge
    Luteal phaseabout 14 days, fairly constant
    Bleedingusually about 5 days; average loss 30–40 mL
    Heavy menstrual bleedingbleeding that interferes with daily life; 80 mL or more per period is the older research definition

    Conditions

    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Cervical cancer A malignant tumour of the cervix, the neck of the uterus, that develops almost always from persistent infection with high-risk types of human…
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • Ovarian cancer A malignant tumour of the ovary or, as is now recognised for many cases of the commonest type, of the adjacent fallopian tube.

    Symptoms

    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Cervical screening (smear test) A sample of cells brushed from the transformation zone of the cervix.

    Biomarkers

    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Hysterectomy Removal of the uterus.

    Drug classes

    • Oestrogens Steroid hormones that act on the oestrogen receptor.
