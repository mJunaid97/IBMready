    Tubular reabsorption and secretion

    The tubules reclaim over 99% of the filtered water and salt and all the glucose, and add wastes and excess ions to what remains.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    The proximal tubule recovers about two thirds of the filtered water and sodium and all the glucose and amino acids; when blood glucose is high, as in uncontrolled diabetes, the transporters are overwhelmed and glucose spills into the urine. The loop of Henle builds a salt gradient in the medulla that allows water to be drawn out later. The distal tubule and collecting duct fine-tune sodium, potassium and acid under hormonal control.

    Aldosterone increases sodium reabsorption (and potassium loss); antidiuretic hormone opens water channels in the collecting duct to concentrate urine. Diuretic drugs block specific transporters: loop diuretics in the thick ascending limb, thiazides in the distal tubule.

    Secretion works the other way: the tubules actively pump potassium, hydrogen ions, drugs and organic acids into the urine, which is how penicillin and many other drugs are cleared.

    Each kidney contains about a million nephrons, and the division of labour within each is simple: the glomerulus filters the blood and the tubule returns what the body needs while leaving wastes behind. The final adjustment is made in the collecting ducts, which under the influence of ADH can recover almost all of the water passing through them during dehydration or almost none during over-hydration. ADH does this by stimulating the insertion of aquaporin water channels (and urea transporters) into the cell membranes; when ADH falls the aquaporins are withdrawn and water stays in the urine. Aldosterone acts on the same segments to recover sodium, and because sodium reabsorption is coupled to potassium secretion, a high aldosterone level causes potassium loss. Renin acts indirectly: when blood pressure falls, sympathetic nerves stimulate its release, angiotensin II is generated and aldosterone production rises, so more sodium and water are retained and blood volume is restored.

    The kidney also protects reabsorption by holding filtration steady. Two internal mechanisms, the myogenic response of the arterioles and tubuloglomerular feedback, keep the glomerular filtration rate roughly constant across a range of blood pressures without any outside instruction: when the macula densa cells of the distal tubule sense a change in the filtrate flowing past them, they release ATP and adenosine, which constrict the afferent arteriole and reduce filtration. This autoregulation is why kidney function survives everyday swings in blood pressure during exercise and sleep, and why it can fail abruptly in shock or severe dehydration, when pressure drops below the range the kidney can compensate for.

    Reabsorption cannot be measured directly in a patient, so it is assessed by what reaches the urine and what remains in the blood. Glucose in the urine signals that blood glucose has exceeded the tubule's transport capacity, as in diabetes mellitus, though it is no longer used to diagnose diabetes. A standard kidney work-up pairs a urinalysis and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio with blood creatinine (from which the estimated glomerular filtration rate is calculated), urea and electrolytes, so disordered sodium, potassium or water handling shows up as abnormal blood electrolytes alongside an inappropriately concentrated or dilute urine.

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Tubular reabsorption and secretion.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    Medical tests

    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Medications

    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
