    Hormone regulation: the hypothalamic-pituitary axis

    The hypothalamus tells the pituitary, the pituitary tells the glands, and the glands' hormones report back: a chain of command that links the brain to the endocrine system.

    Open in the 3D explorerPituitary gland anatomy

    Releasing hormones from the hypothalamus travel down a private portal system to the anterior pituitary, which secretes the tropic hormones: thyroid-stimulating hormone, adrenocorticotropic hormone, the gonadotropins, growth hormone and prolactin. The posterior pituitary stores two hormones made in the hypothalamus itself, antidiuretic hormone and oxytocin.

    Each axis is a loop: for the thyroid, TRH drives TSH, TSH drives thyroxine, and thyroxine suppresses both. So a failing thyroid produces a high TSH, while a pituitary problem produces low TSH with low thyroxine; the pair of results tells you where the fault lies.

    Stress, sleep, light and season act on the hypothalamus, which is why cortisol peaks in the early morning, growth hormone surges in deep sleep and the menstrual cycle can stop under severe stress.

    Anatomically the two lobes of the pituitary are different tissues. The posterior lobe is neural tissue, an extension of the hypothalamus down the infundibulum (pituitary stalk), whereas the anterior lobe is glandular tissue that develops from the roof of the primitive digestive tract. The hypophyseal portal system is a network of vessels that carries hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting hormones straight into the anterior lobe without them entering the general circulation, so tiny quantities reach their target undiluted. Cortisol illustrates how feedback is layered: it inhibits both the hypothalamus and the pituitary, so when the hypothalamus detects a rising level of a target-gland hormone it tells the pituitary to stop releasing the corresponding tropic hormone.

    Because secretion is pulsatile and follows a daily rhythm, a single random hormone level is often uninformative. Clinicians therefore sample at the right time (cortisol early in the morning, when it is expected to peak) and, when the question is whether a gland can respond, use dynamic tests that stimulate or suppress the axis and watch the response. A flat response of ACTH and cortisol to stimulation points to pituitary disease, whereas a delayed but exaggerated response suggests the fault lies in the hypothalamus; a suppression test checks that cortisol production can still be switched off. Paired measurements of the tropic hormone and the gland hormone, such as TSH with thyroxine, or ACTH with cortisol, locate the fault in the same way.

    Pituitary disease is often a mass effect: a tumour can damage the normal hormone-releasing cells so the gland under-produces some or all of its hormones, and a mass is suspected when a deficiency of one axis is accompanied by deficiencies of other pituitary hormones or a raised prolactin. The axis is also vulnerable to dysregulation under sustained physical or psychological stress, and results can be distorted by medicines, obesity, depression and acute illness, so abnormal findings are usually repeated and interpreted alongside imaging of the pituitary.

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Hormone regulation: the hypothalamic-pituitary axis.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.

    Medications

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.

    Drug classes

    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.
