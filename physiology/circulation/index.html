    Circulation

    Two circuits in series: the pulmonary circulation loads blood with oxygen in the lungs, the systemic circulation delivers it to every organ.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    The right heart pumps deoxygenated blood through the pulmonary arteries into the lungs, where it picks up oxygen and unloads carbon dioxide before returning through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium. The left heart pumps it into the aorta and on through arteries, arterioles and capillaries, where exchange with the tissues happens, then back through venules and veins to the right atrium.

    Pressure falls along the way: from about 120 mmHg in the aorta to a few mmHg in the great veins. Veins hold about two thirds of the blood volume at any moment and return it to the heart with the help of valves, the muscle pump of the legs and the suction of breathing.

    Special circuits exist: the hepatic portal system carries gut blood through the liver first, and the coronary circulation supplies the heart muscle itself during diastole.

    Tracing the route in detail: blood low in oxygen and high in carbon dioxide reaches the right atrium through the superior and inferior venae cavae and the coronary sinus, which drains the heart muscle itself. The right ventricle ejects it into the pulmonary trunk, past the pulmonary semilunar valve that stops it falling back during diastole; the trunk divides into the right and left pulmonary arteries, the only arteries in the adult body carrying deoxygenated blood. Oxygen-rich blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium and the left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, which has an ascending part giving off the coronary arteries, an arch supplying the head and arms, and a descending part with thoracic and abdominal segments supplying everything else. Four chambers working in pairs and four valves keep the flow in one direction.

    Control of where the blood goes lies in the arterioles. Their narrow lumens make them the resistance vessels: a slight dilation greatly increases the flow to an organ and a slight constriction greatly reduces it, so during exercise the arterioles of the working muscles open while those of the gut and kidneys narrow, and cardiac output rises severalfold to feed the muscles. Constriction of veins raises the pressure within them and helps return blood to the heart, which matters when standing up or after blood loss. The pulse felt at the wrist is the expansion and recoil of an artery with each beat and so reflects the heartbeat.

    Circulation is assessed by the pulse, the blood pressure measured with an arm cuff, the warmth and colour of the skin and how quickly it refills after pressure, and the urine output, which falls early when organs are underperfused. If disease or injury weakens the heart the organs no longer receive enough blood to work normally: in heart failure fluid backs up into the lungs and legs, in shock the skin becomes cool and mottled and the brain and kidneys fail, and in peripheral arterial disease narrowed leg arteries cause pain on walking. Echocardiography, Doppler ultrasound of the vessels and angiography show where in the circuit the problem lies.

    Conditions

    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.

    Biomarkers

    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • Cardiac troponin A protein of the contractile apparatus of heart muscle cells that leaks into blood when those cells are injured.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Clopidogrel An antiplatelet medicine that stops platelets clumping by blocking their P2Y12 receptor, taken to prevent clots after a heart attack, stent.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Sildenafil Sildenafil is a medicine used to treat erection problems and, sometimes, pulmonary hypertension.
    • Tadalafil Tadalafil is a medicine mainly used to treat erection problems.
    • Warfarin The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

    Drug classes

    • Anticoagulants Medicines that slow the clotting cascade to prevent and treat clots in veins and the heart: strokes in atrial fibrillation.
    • Antiplatelet drugs Medicines that stop platelets clumping into the clots that block arteries.
    • Calcium channel blockers Medicines that relax the muscle in artery walls (and, for some members.
    • Nitrates Vasodilators that relieve angina within minutes by relaxing veins and coronary arteries.
    • PDE5 inhibitors PDE5 inhibitors are medicines that include sildenafil and tadalafil, used mainly for erection problems.
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
