    Blood pressure

    The pressure blood exerts on artery walls: systolic at the peak of ejection, diastolic between beats.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Blood pressure depends on how much blood the heart pumps each minute (cardiac output) and how hard it is to push that blood through the small arteries (peripheral resistance). The elastic aorta smooths the pulse; the muscular arterioles set resistance and are the main site of control.

    Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch report pressure to the brainstem, which adjusts heart rate and vessel tone within seconds. Over hours and days the kidneys take over, adjusting salt and water excretion through the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.

    Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic in millimetres of mercury. Values around 120/80 mmHg are typical; sustained readings of 140/90 mmHg or more define hypertension in most guidelines.

    Blood pressure is a form of hydrostatic pressure: the force the blood exerts on the walls of the vessels and the chambers of the heart. Two derived numbers matter clinically. Pulse pressure, the difference between systolic and diastolic, reflects how much the arteries are stretched by each ejection; it widens harmlessly after strenuous exercise but a persistently wide pulse pressure can indicate stiff or resistant arteries. Mean arterial pressure is the average pressure across the cycle and is the pressure that actually drives blood into the tissues. Five variables govern pressure and flow in the systemic circulation: cardiac output, the compliance of the vessels, the blood volume, the viscosity of the blood, and the length and diameter of the vessels. Of these, arteriolar diameter is the most responsive: the arterioles have such narrow lumens that a slight dilation greatly lowers resistance and a slight constriction greatly raises it, which is why they are called resistance vessels. Constriction of veins also raises pressure within them and helps return blood to the heart.

    Measurement uses a cuff on the upper arm. The traditional sphygmomanometer combines a cuff, a pump, a dial and a stethoscope: the cuff is inflated until it stops the flow, then slowly released, and the first sound heard through the stethoscope (the first Korotkoff sound) marks the systolic pressure while the point at which the sounds disappear marks the diastolic. Automatic devices sense the pulse through the cuff and show both numbers on a digital display. For a reliable reading the person rests for several minutes, sits with the back supported and legs uncrossed, keeps the arm supported at heart level and free of tight clothing, and stays still and silent; readings are repeated because the first is often highest.

    A single clinic reading is only a snapshot. Pressure rises with exercise, pain, cold, a full bladder and anxiety, and readings taken in a clinic can run higher than at home, the so-called white coat effect. For this reason diagnosis is usually confirmed with home monitoring using a validated upper-arm device, or with ambulatory monitoring, in which a portable cuff records pressure automatically through the day and night. Persistently raised pressure strains the heart, arteries, kidneys and brain and is a major treatable risk factor for stroke and heart attack, while an abrupt fall in pressure, as in haemorrhage or sepsis, deprives the organs of flow.

    Conditions

    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Erectile dysfunction Erectile dysfunction, also called impotence, is being unable to get or keep an erection.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.

    Medications

    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Sildenafil Sildenafil is a medicine used to treat erection problems and, sometimes, pulmonary hypertension.
    • Tadalafil Tadalafil is a medicine mainly used to treat erection problems.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Calcium channel blockers Medicines that relax the muscle in artery walls (and, for some members.
    • Nitrates Vasodilators that relieve angina within minutes by relaxing veins and coronary arteries.
    • PDE5 inhibitors PDE5 inhibitors are medicines that include sildenafil and tadalafil, used mainly for erection problems.

    Guides and news

    • ACE inhibitors, ARBs and amlodipine compared ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.
    • Home blood pressure checks: how they work The NHS says blood pressure can be checked at home with a monitor, with the cuff on the upper arm and the person sitting with their back supported.
    • High blood pressure: what happens next High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms and is found by measuring it; treatment starts with lifestyle changes and may include medicine.
    • Lowering blood pressure with lifestyle changes The NHS advises everyone with high blood pressure to make lifestyle changes, such as eating less salt, being more active and cutting back on alcohol.
    • Stress: how it shows up and where to get help Stress is a reaction to mental or emotional pressure that can affect mood, body and behaviour.
