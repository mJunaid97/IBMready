    Sensory systems

    Receptors convert light, sound, chemicals, pressure and temperature into nerve signals that the thalamus and cortex turn into perception.

    Open in the 3D explorerEye anatomy

    The eye focuses light onto the retina, where rods handle dim light and cones colour; signals cross partly at the optic chiasm so each visual cortex sees the opposite half of the world. The ear turns vibration into fluid waves in the cochlea, where hair cells fire in proportion to pitch; the neighbouring semicircular canals and otolith organs sense rotation and gravity for balance.

    Smell receptors in the roof of the nose project directly to the olfactory cortex and limbic system, which is why odours evoke memory so strongly; taste buds detect sweet, salt, sour, bitter and umami. Skin carries receptors for touch, vibration, temperature and pain, with the fingertips and lips most densely supplied and therefore over-represented in the sensory cortex.

    Pain is a protective signal, not a measurement of damage: it is amplified by inflammation and modulated by the brain, which explains phantom limb pain and the relief of distraction.

    Sensation and perception are different steps. Sensation is the activation of a receptor by a stimulus; perception is the central processing that turns the resulting signals into a meaningful pattern. Receptors are classified by structure (free nerve endings, encapsulated endings or specialised receptor cells), by position relative to the stimulus (interoceptors for the body's interior, exteroceptors for the outside world, proprioceptors for position) and by the kind of energy they transduce. Transduction begins when the stimulus changes the membrane potential of the receptor: chemoreceptors for taste and smell are transmembrane proteins that bind a ligand, a molecule in food for instance, while mechanoreceptors open ion channels when they are physically deformed. Receptors also adapt, so a constant stimulus such as clothing on skin stops being perceived, whereas a change is signalled immediately.

    Hearing illustrates transduction in detail. Sound funnelled down the ear canal vibrates the eardrum, the malleus, incus and stapes amplify the vibration and pass it to the fluid of the cochlea, and a travelling wave along the basilar membrane bends the stereocilia on the hair cells against an overlying membrane; this opens channels at their tips, ions rush in and an electrical signal passes to the auditory nerve. Hair cells near the wide base of the cochlea respond to high pitches and those toward the centre to low ones. Inner hair cells relay the signal while the more numerous outer hair cells amplify quiet sounds, and because human hair cells cannot regenerate, damage from loud noise, disease, injury or ageing causes permanent hearing loss.

    In the central nervous system, somatosensory input enters through the spinal cord and most other senses through the brainstem, and all except smell relay in the thalamus before reaching the cortex. Fine touch and position travel up the dorsal columns, cross in the medulla and ascend in the medial lemniscus; pain and temperature cross within the spinal cord itself in the spinothalamic tract. Both reach the postcentral gyrus, where conscious perception of touch occurs. Vision is organised by visual field rather than by eye, with axons from the nasal retina crossing at the optic chiasm, and sound is localised partly from the microseconds by which it reaches one ear before the other. Because these pathways cross at predictable levels, the side and type of a sensory loss tell the clinician where a lesion lies, and the sensory examination tests touch, pain, vibration and position sense in turn.

    Conditions

    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Autism A lifelong difference in how a person communicates, behaves and reacts compared with people who are not autistic.

    Symptoms

    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.

    Procedures

    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.

    Medications

    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Chlorphenamine A first-generation antihistamine, sold over the counter, that relieves hay fever.

    Drug classes

    • Antihistamines Medicines that block histamine's H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and itching.

    Guides and news

    • Hearing loss: causes, tests and what helps Gradual hearing loss is common and a GP can arrange hearing tests.
    • Protecting your hearing from loud noise The WHO and NHS sources say noise-related hearing loss is permanent but can be prevented, mainly by lowering volume.
