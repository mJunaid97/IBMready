    Neurons

    The nerve cell: a body with branching dendrites that receive signals and a single axon that carries them onward, sometimes for a metre.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    The brain holds about 86 billion neurons, each connected to thousands of others through synapses. Dendrites collect incoming signals; if their sum crosses a threshold at the axon hillock, the neuron fires an all-or-nothing action potential down its axon. Axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty insulation that lets the impulse jump between gaps and travel up to 120 metres per second.

    Neurons communicate chemically: an arriving impulse releases a neurotransmitter such as glutamate, GABA, dopamine or acetylcholine into the synaptic gap, and the receiving cell is excited or inhibited depending on its receptors. Most drugs acting on the nervous system change this step.

    Glial cells are about as numerous as neurons and feed, insulate and protect them. Unlike most cells, adult neurons rarely divide, which is why injury to the brain and spinal cord is so hard to repair.

    At rest the inside of a neuron is negative relative to the outside. A stimulus that depolarises the membrane to a threshold voltage opens voltage-gated sodium channels and the action potential follows; a stronger stimulus does not produce a bigger spike, so information is coded in the frequency of firing rather than its size. Potassium leaving the cell then restores the resting voltage, with a brief overshoot of hyperpolarisation, and during the absolute refractory period that follows the sodium channels are inactivated and no new impulse can be generated, which limits the firing rate and keeps impulses travelling in one direction.

    Conduction speed depends on insulation and calibre. In unmyelinated axons the impulse spreads continuously along the membrane, whereas in myelinated axons the voltage-gated channels are clustered at the nodes of Ranvier, short unmyelinated gaps where the impulse is regenerated, so it appears to jump from node to node (saltatory conduction). Wider axons also conduct faster because ions meet less resistance. In demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis, current leaks from axon segments that were previously insulated and conduction slows, which is one reason symptoms fluctuate and spread.

    Neurons are supported by glia: astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in the brain and spinal cord supply nutrients, insulate axons and shape the chemical environment around synapses. As a person learns, neurons form new circuits that act as information highways between brain regions. Disease often targets particular populations: in Parkinson's disease the dopamine-producing neurons of the basal ganglia die, and many neurological and psychiatric disorders involve too much or too little transmission at the synapse. Researchers have identified new neurons forming in the adult human brain, but how much this contributes to repair and function remains an open question.

    Conditions

    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Medications

    • Amitriptyline Amitriptyline is a prescription-only medicine from a group called tricyclic antidepressants, used for nerve pain and to help prevent migraine.
    • Mirtazapine Mirtazapine is an antidepressant, prescribed mainly for depression and sometimes for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and anxiety.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

    Drug classes

    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
