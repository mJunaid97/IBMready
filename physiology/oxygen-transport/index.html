    Oxygen transport

    Almost all oxygen travels bound to haemoglobin in red cells; how tightly it binds changes with the tissue's needs.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    Each haemoglobin molecule carries four oxygen molecules, and a litre of arterial blood holds about 200 ml of oxygen, over 98% of it bound. The binding curve is S-shaped: haemoglobin loads fully in the lungs yet releases oxygen readily where partial pressure is low. Acid, heat and carbon dioxide, all produced by working tissue, shift the curve to release more (the Bohr effect).

    Pulse oximeters estimate the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen; 95 to 100% is normal at sea level. Anaemia lowers the amount of haemoglobin, so total oxygen delivery falls even when saturation reads normal, which is why a saturation reading alone can mislead.

    Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin about 240 times more tightly than oxygen, and standard oximeters cannot tell the difference.

    The exchange itself happens across the alveolar wall, which is extraordinarily thin and wrapped in pulmonary capillaries. Oxygen diffuses from the air sacs into the blood, where red cells bind it to haemoglobin, a metalloprotein whose iron-containing haem groups each hold one oxygen molecule; carbon dioxide travels the opposite way to be exhaled. Only a small fraction of oxygen, about 1.5%, is carried dissolved in the plasma. Binding is cooperative: once the first oxygen molecule attaches, haemoglobin changes shape so that the second binds more readily, and so on, which produces the S-shaped curve and lets haemoglobin become almost fully saturated in the lungs yet give up a large share of its load in the tissues.

    Pulse oximetry is the routine way of following this. A small clip-like sensor on a fingertip (or an infant's foot) shines red and infrared light through the tissue and calculates, from the difference in absorption by oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, the percentage of haemoglobin that is carrying oxygen; it also displays the pulse rate. The test is painless and non-invasive and is used in clinics, hospitals and at home for conditions that affect lung function such as COPD, asthma, heart failure, lung cancer and COVID-19, for sleep apnoea, and before, during and after surgery. In a healthy person with normal haemoglobin the reading sits in the high nineties.

    Oximetry has limits that matter in illness. It reports saturation, not content: a person with severe anaemia may show a normal percentage while delivering far too little oxygen, and carbon monoxide poisoning reads falsely reassuring because the device cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin. Poor peripheral circulation, cold hands, movement and nail varnish degrade the signal, and pulse oximeters can overestimate oxygen saturation in people with darker skin, so a normal reading does not exclude low oxygen in someone who is unwell. A pulse oximeter also cannot detect carbon monoxide poisoning, which is an emergency. When precision matters, an arterial blood gas measures the dissolved oxygen directly.

    Conditions

    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Vitamin B12 and folate Two blood tests, usually ordered together, for the vitamins the bone marrow needs to make normal red cells and the nervous system needs to keep…

    Biomarkers

    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Folate The B vitamin from green vegetables and fortified foods that cells need to make DNA; deficiency causes anaemia with large red cells and.
    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Lymphocytes The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells).
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Vitamin B12 The vitamin, absorbed with intrinsic factor in the last part of the small intestine.

    Procedures

    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.

    Guides and news

    • Altitude sickness symptoms and prevention Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.
