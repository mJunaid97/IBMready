    Brain functions

    Different regions specialise: the cortex for perception, movement, language and thought; deep nuclei for movement and emotion; the brainstem for life support; the cerebellum for coordination.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    The frontal lobe plans, decides and, through the precentral gyrus, commands movement; Broca's area in the left inferior frontal gyrus produces speech. The parietal lobe handles touch and space, the temporal lobe hearing, memory and language comprehension (Wernicke's area), and the occipital lobe vision. The insula senses the body's internal state.

    Beneath the cortex, the basal ganglia select and smooth movements, the thalamus relays almost every signal to the cortex, the hypothalamus runs hormones, temperature, hunger and sleep, and the hippocampus lays down new memories. The brainstem keeps breathing and the heart going and houses the cranial nerve nuclei; the cerebellum times and coordinates movement.

    Because functions are localised, the pattern of a deficit locates a lesion: right-sided weakness with lost speech points to the left middle cerebral artery territory, unsteadiness to the cerebellum, and memory loss with preserved alertness to the medial temporal lobes.

    Anatomists divide the adult brain into four regions, cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem and cerebellum, and the forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain of development map onto them. The basal nuclei beneath the cortex do not command movement directly: they compare what the cortex is planning with the general state of activity in the nervous system and so influence whether a movement is likely to happen, which is why their degeneration produces the slowness of Parkinson's disease rather than paralysis. The nearby basal forebrain modulates cortical activity in attention and memory. Within the brainstem, the midbrain controls some reflexes and is part of the circuit for eye movements, while the pons and medulla hold the centres that regulate the cardiovascular and respiratory systems and the nuclei of the cranial nerves serving the head and neck. The cerebellum, connected to the brainstem mainly through the pons, initiates and times movements using information about balance and body position; it is engaged whenever a learned skill such as playing the piano or hitting a tennis ball is performed, and it builds the automatic programmes that let complex movements run without conscious thought.

    The lobes carry out higher functions in a distributed way. The frontal lobes do much of the work of planning a schedule, imagining the future and reasoning; the parietal lobes, beyond touch and space, support reading and arithmetic; the occipital lobes process images from the eyes and link them with images stored in memory; and the temporal lobes form and retrieve memories, including those tied to music. Long-term episodic memory depends on the hippocampus and the surrounding medial temporal structures, whereas short-term working memory is localised to the prefrontal cortex.

    The neurological examination is built around this map: its major sections correspond to the major regions of the central nervous system. The mental status examination tests the cerebrum in four groups, orientation and memory, language and speech, sensorium, and judgement and abstract reasoning, with bedside tasks such as recalling three words after a delay or reciting the months backwards. Losses of language (aphasias) point to Broca's or Wernicke's areas in the left hemisphere; difficulty recognising objects by touch or figures traced on the skin points to the parietal cortex; impaired planning and judgement point to the prefrontal cortex. The coordination examination, including gait, tests the cerebellum, and the cranial nerve examination the brainstem. Imaging with CT or MRI then confirms the location and nature of a lesion the examination has predicted.

    Conditions

    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Parkinson's disease A progressive disorder in which dopamine-producing neurons in the substantia nigra of the midbrain are lost, causing slowness, stiffness and tremor.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.
    • Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) is a mental health condition in which a person has obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviours.

    Imaging

    • MRI scan Magnetic resonance imaging uses a strong magnet and radio waves, not radiation, to produce detailed pictures of soft tissue: the brain, spinal cord.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.
    • PET-CT scan Positron emission tomography tracks a mildly radioactive sugar or tracer to show where cells are most active.

    Medications

    • Amitriptyline Amitriptyline is a prescription-only medicine from a group called tricyclic antidepressants, used for nerve pain and to help prevent migraine.
    • Citalopram A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily.
    • Duloxetine Duloxetine is an antidepressant medicine that is also used for anxiety and nerve pain.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
    • Gabapentin A prescription medicine used to treat epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • Mirtazapine Mirtazapine is an antidepressant, prescribed mainly for depression and sometimes for obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and anxiety.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Pregabalin Pregabalin is a prescription medicine used for nerve pain, epilepsy and generalised anxiety disorder.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.

    Drug classes

    • Gabapentinoids Gabapentinoids are the group that contains pregabalin and gabapentin, which are used for epilepsy and nerve pain.
    • NaSSA antidepressants The NHS groups antidepressants into types such as SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and MAOIs.
    • Diagnostic radiopharmaceuticals Radioactive medicines given in tiny amounts so that a gamma camera or PET scanner can show where they accumulate: bone turnover.
    • SNRIs SNRIs are a group of antidepressants whose NHS examples are duloxetine and venlafaxine.
    • SSRIs The most widely used antidepressants (sertraline, citalopram, escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine), also first-line for anxiety disorders, panic.
    • Tricyclic antidepressants Tricyclic antidepressants are a group of medicines that includes amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine and clomipramine.

    Guides and news

    • Anxiety: signs, treatment and getting help Anxiety becomes a health problem when worry is hard to control and affects daily life.
    • Dementia: early signs and how it is assessed Early signs of dementia include memory loss, trouble with familiar tasks and confusion about time or place.
    • Depression: signs and getting help Depression is more than feeling unhappy for a few days.
    • Grief and bereavement: what to expect Grief is a natural response to loss, and the NHS says feelings usually ease gradually.
    • Head injury and concussion: when to get help Most head injuries are not serious, but the NHS says being knocked out and not waking, a fit or being unable to stay awake need a 999 call.
    • Loneliness and health: effects and what helps Severe or long-lasting loneliness can affect mental and physical health, according to the NHS.
    • NHS talking therapies and how to self-refer NHS talking therapies offer CBT and counselling for anxiety and depression.
    • Panic attacks: what they are and what helps A panic attack is a rush of intense fear with physical symptoms such as a racing heartbeat.
    • Sleep problems: what helps and when to get help The NHS says insomnia usually improves by changing sleeping habits, and that a GP can arrange CBT when habits alone do not help.
    • Supporting someone with a mental health problem Listening, asking open questions and offering practical help are the NHS's main suggestions for supporting someone with a mental health problem.
