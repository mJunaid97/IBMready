    Sex hormones and puberty

    Oestradiol, progesterone and testosterone are steroid hormones made mainly by the ovaries and testes under the control of the hypothalamus and pituitary; they drive puberty, maintain the reproductive organs and act on bone, muscle, fat, skin, blood and brain throughout life.

    Open in the 3D explorerOvaries & fallopian tubes anatomy

    The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis controls the sex hormones. The hypothalamus releases gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses and the pituitary answers with luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). In the ovaries FSH drives follicle growth and oestradiol, while LH supports the corpus luteum and its progesterone; in the testes LH drives testosterone and FSH supports sperm production. Inhibin from the gonads suppresses FSH, and the sex steroids feed back negatively on the hypothalamus and pituitary; the one exception is the sustained mid-cycle rise in oestradiol, which switches to positive feedback and triggers the LH surge that causes ovulation. Anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) from small growing follicles is used as a marker of how many follicles remain.

    All of them are steroids made from cholesterol that pass into cells and bind nuclear receptors, the oestrogen, androgen and progesterone receptors, switching genes on or off over hours to days. Both sexes make both hormones: adult men have much higher testosterone and adult women have higher oestradiol for most of the cycle, and men make oestradiol mainly by converting testosterone with the enzyme aromatase. Aromatase is also active in adipose tissue, which is the main source of oestrogen after the menopause. Oestrogen promotes bone density and closes the growth plates in both sexes, so bone is lost when it falls at the menopause; androgens build muscle and strength, stimulate red cell production and, as dihydrotestosterone (DHT), drive body hair and male-pattern hair loss. Progesterone from the corpus luteum turns the proliferative endometrium into a secretory lining ready for implantation, raises basal body temperature by a few tenths of a degree and, if pregnancy follows, holds the pregnancy until the placenta takes over.

    Puberty has two parts. Adrenarche, from about the age of six to eight, is the adrenal glands beginning to make androgens, which later give pubic and underarm hair. Gonadarche follows when the GnRH pulses reawaken and LH and FSH rise: in girls puberty normally begins between 8 and 13, the first sign being breast budding, followed by pubic hair, the growth spurt and a first period about two years later; in boys it normally begins between 9 and 14 with enlargement of the testes, then penile growth, pubic hair, voice deepening and a later growth spurt. Clinicians stage these changes with the Tanner scale, from 1 to 5. Oestrogen, made from testosterone in boys, contributes to the growth spurt alongside testosterone and growth hormone, and is what finally fuses the epiphyseal plates. This is the usual course of puberty in people assigned female or male at birth; gender-affirming hormone therapy produces a different set of changes, and puberty in trans and non-binary young people is managed by specialist services.

    Levels are not fixed. Testosterone peaks in the morning, so it is sampled then; oestradiol is lowest in the early follicular phase and highest just before ovulation; progesterone rises only after ovulation, so a mid-luteal sample confirms that ovulation happened. Oestradiol falls and FSH rises at the menopause. A result is read with LH and FSH and with the timing of the sample: a low sex steroid with high LH and FSH points to the gonad, one with low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH to the hypothalamus or pituitary. This is the axis that hormonal contraception, hormone replacement therapy and gender-affirming hormone therapy act on.
