    Urine formation and excretion

    Filtration, reabsorption and secretion leave one to two litres of urine a day, carried by the ureters to the bladder and released under voluntary control.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    Urine is mostly water carrying urea, creatinine, uric acid, salts and any drug metabolites the body needs to lose. Its colour reflects concentration; its composition, read by a dipstick, reveals infection (leucocytes, nitrites), blood, protein, glucose or ketones.

    Peristalsis in the ureters delivers urine to the bladder, which stretches to hold 400 to 600 ml. Stretch receptors signal fullness at around 200 ml; the micturition reflex contracts the detrusor and relaxes the internal sphincter, while the external sphincter is released voluntarily. Children learn this control between two and four years; strokes, spinal injury and prostate enlargement disturb it.

    Kidney stones form when salts crystallise in concentrated urine; keeping urine dilute is the simplest prevention.

    The first step is filtration in the glomeruli, which together form filtrate at a rate called the glomerular filtration rate; of the enormous volume filtered each day, about ninety-nine per cent is reabsorbed back into the blood along the tubules, so that only one to two litres leave as urine. Reabsorption is selective: glucose, amino acids and most of the salt and water are reclaimed, while urea, creatinine and many drugs are left behind or actively secreted into the tubule. The final composition is adjusted in the collecting ducts under the control of antidiuretic hormone, which decides how much water is kept, and aldosterone, which decides how much sodium.

    Emptying is a spinal reflex with a cortical veto. Stretch receptors in the bladder wall signal through the sacral spinal cord; the resulting parasympathetic outflow contracts the detrusor muscle and relaxes the involuntary internal urethral sphincter, while at the same time the cord inhibits the somatic motor neurons so that the skeletal muscle of the external sphincter also relaxes. Conscious effort can keep the external sphincter closed and defer voiding until the volume becomes large enough to override it. The reflex runs unchecked in infants; children learn to assert external sphincter control, and the control is lost again after strokes, spinal cord injury or dementia.

    The kidneys also carry out the final step in making active vitamin D, which regulates calcium, and secrete erythropoietin, which drives red cell production, so failing kidneys cause bone disease and anaemia as well as fluid overload. Kidney function is followed with blood creatinine and the estimated filtration rate derived from it, with urine dipstick and microscopy for protein, blood, cells and crystals, and with ultrasound of the kidneys and bladder, including measurement of the volume left after voiding when retention is suspected. Urinary problems range from the inconvenience of incontinence to the life-threatening loss of filtration in kidney failure.

    Conditions

    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Medical tests

    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Dapagliflozin Dapagliflozin is a prescription tablet from the SGLT2 inhibitor group, used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Tamsulosin Tamsulosin is an alpha blocker prescribed mainly for the urinary difficulties caused by an enlarged prostate gland.
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Drug classes

    • SGLT2 inhibitors SGLT2 inhibitors are tablets used for type 2 diabetes, heart failure and chronic kidney disease.
    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.
