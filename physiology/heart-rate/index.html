    Heart rate

    How often the heart beats, set by the sinoatrial node and adjusted second by second by the autonomic nervous system and hormones.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    The sinoatrial node in the wall of the right atrium fires spontaneously about 100 times a minute if left alone. At rest the vagus nerve slows it to a typical 60 to 100 beats per minute; sympathetic nerves and adrenaline speed it up during exercise, fear or fever.

    Trained athletes often have resting rates in the 40s because each beat pumps more blood. Rates persistently above 100 (tachycardia) or below 60 (bradycardia) are only abnormal in context: a rate of 45 in a marathon runner is normal, in a fainting older adult it may signal heart block.

    Heart rate is one of the vital signs and is measured at the radial pulse, on an ECG, or by pulse oximeters and wearables.

    Each beat begins as an electrical signal in the pacemaker cells of the sinoatrial node in the right atrium. The signal spreads first through the atria, which contract and push blood into the ventricles, and then through the ventricles, which contract and pump blood out of the heart; the closing of the aortic and pulmonary valves after ventricular ejection produces the second heart sound. Nerve signals and hormones set how fast and how hard this sequence repeats. During exercise the heart rate rises to carry more oxygen to the working muscles, and a trained heart, being larger, can deliver the same blood flow with fewer beats.

    Checking the pulse by hand remains a basic clinical skill. The NHS describes finding it on the inside of the wrist at the base of the thumb, or at the side of the neck beside the windpipe, pressing lightly with the index and middle fingers rather than the thumb, which has a pulse of its own. For a resting value the person sits quietly for a few minutes first and avoids smoking or caffeine beforehand; beats are counted over a full minute, or over half a minute and doubled. Feeling whether the beats arrive at even intervals checks the rhythm as well as the rate: occasional skipped beats are common, but a persistently irregular pulse can be a sign of atrial fibrillation and is checked with an ECG.

    Rate and rhythm together are what clinicians interpret. Fitter people rest at lower rates, so a slow pulse in someone who feels well is rarely a concern, whereas a persistently fast or slow resting pulse in someone who feels unwell, or an irregular one, prompts medical review. Fever, pain, dehydration, blood loss, anaemia and an overactive thyroid all raise the resting rate; heart block, hypothyroidism and some medicines such as beta blockers lower it.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Ambulatory ECG monitoring A portable recorder worn for 24 hours to two weeks (or an implanted loop recorder for up to three years) that captures the heart's rhythm during…
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.

    Medications

    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
