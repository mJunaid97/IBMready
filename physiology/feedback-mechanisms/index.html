    Feedback mechanisms and homeostasis

    Negative feedback, where a rise in the output switches off its own production, holds temperature, glucose, blood pressure and hormones near their set points.

    Open in the 3D explorerBrain anatomy

    A thermostat is the model: a sensor measures the variable, a control centre compares it with a set point, and an effector corrects the error. Body temperature is held near 37 °C by the hypothalamus, which orders sweating and skin vasodilation when hot and shivering and vasoconstriction when cold; fever is a deliberate resetting of the set point by inflammatory signals.

    Glucose is held by the insulin-glucagon pair, calcium by parathyroid hormone, blood pressure by baroreflexes and the kidneys, oxygen and carbon dioxide by the breathing centres. Diseases are often a broken loop: in type 1 diabetes the effector (insulin) is missing; in Cushing's disease the control centre ignores the feedback.

    Positive feedback is rare and used for events that must run to completion: the surge of oxytocin in labour, blood clotting and the sodium rush of an action potential.

    Every regulated variable has a set point, the value around which its normal range fluctuates, and a negative feedback loop has three components: a sensor that measures the variable, a control centre that compares the reading with the set point, and an effector that reverses the deviation. The loop's purpose is as much restraint as correction: it prevents an excessive response and winds down as the stimulus is removed. The endocrine system shows the same logic at the level of whole glands: when the hypothalamus detects a rising level of hormone from a target organ, it signals the pituitary to stop releasing the hormone that drives that organ. Positive feedback, by contrast, intensifies a change until an end point is reached; in labour, oxytocin strengthens uterine contractions, which stretch the cervix further and release more oxytocin, a cycle that stops only when the baby is born.

    Temperature control is the textbook negative feedback system. The thermostat is the preoptic area of the hypothalamus, which receives input from peripheral receptors in the skin, reporting surface temperature, and central receptors in the viscera, spinal cord and hypothalamus itself, reporting core temperature. When heat production exceeds need, as during exercise, the hypothalamus opens the skin circulation so heat escapes and drives sweating; in the cold it does the reverse, constricting skin vessels, and if that is insufficient it triggers shivering and activates brown fat to generate heat.

    Fever is not a failure of the thermostat but a change in its setting. Infection or inflammation releases cytokines, among them interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and tumour necrosis factor-alpha, which drive production of prostaglandin E2 in the hypothalamus and shift the set point upward; the body then behaves as if it were cold, so a person feels chilled and shivers while their temperature climbs. This distinguishes fever from hyperthermia, in which core temperature rises beyond the set point and beyond the hypothalamus's ability to regulate it, as in heat stroke, which is why hyperthermia is more dangerous than fever at a similar reading and why antipyretic drugs, which act on the prostaglandin step, lower a fever but not heat stroke. Clinically, feedback loops are assessed by measuring the variable and its controlling signal together: a temperature with the clinical context, or a hormone with the tropic hormone that drives it.

    Conditions

    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.

    Medications

    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Testosterone The main androgen, given as a gel, patch, injection or implant to replace what the testes cannot make in confirmed hypogonadism and as the…

    Drug classes

    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.
