    Pregnancy and birth: how the body changes

    From fertilisation in the fallopian tube to birth about 40 weeks later, pregnancy remodels almost every maternal system, from blood volume and kidney filtration to insulin sensitivity.

    Open in the 3D explorerUterus & cervix anatomy

    Pregnancy begins with fertilisation in the ampulla of the fallopian tube, about a day after ovulation; implantation into the progesterone-primed secretory endometrium follows about a week later. The implanting embryo secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), which keeps the corpus luteum making progesterone so that the lining is not shed; it is the hormone pregnancy tests detect. Nausea and vomiting are common in the first trimester and tend to ease as hCG falls, although the cause is not fully understood. By about 10 to 12 weeks the placenta has taken over the production of progesterone and oestrogens. Pregnancy is dated from the first day of the last period, so birth is expected at about 40 weeks.

    The placenta is both an exchange organ and an endocrine gland: maternal blood bathes the chorionic villi, so oxygen, glucose and antibodies cross to the fetus and waste passes back, without the circulations mixing. Blood volume rises by roughly 30 to 50%, plasma more than red cells, so the haemoglobin concentration falls (a physiological anaemia) as the need for iron rises, and cardiac output rises by about 30 to 50%. Progesterone relaxes vascular smooth muscle, so blood pressure falls in the first half of pregnancy and returns to its earlier level by term; a pressure that rises after 20 weeks, especially with protein in the urine, is the pattern of pre-eclampsia, so both are checked at every antenatal appointment.

    Renal blood flow and filtration rise by about half, while the ureters dilate and drain more slowly, so urinary infection reaches the kidneys more easily. Placental hormones make maternal tissues less sensitive to insulin so that more glucose reaches the fetus; gestational diabetes develops when the pancreas cannot compensate, and a glucose tolerance test is offered to those with risk factors; treatment starts with changes to diet and physical activity, with metformin and then insulin added if blood glucose targets are not met. Relaxin softens ligaments, which lets the pelvis give a little in labour but produces pelvic girdle and back pain, and clotting factors rise while venous return slows, so venous thrombosis is more likely, above all after birth.

    Labour is a positive feedback loop: the myometrium gains oxytocin receptors near term, prostaglandins efface the cervix, and pressure of the baby's head releases oxytocin from the posterior pituitary, which strengthens the contractions until birth. Labour has three stages: dilatation of the cervix, the birth itself, and delivery of the placenta, which ends with the uterus clamping its spiral arteries to limit bleeding. Prolactin then makes milk and oxytocin ejects it, the uterus involutes over about six weeks, and pelvic floor muscle training is advised. The same physiology applies to anyone with a uterus and ovaries, including trans men and non-binary people. Some changes are warnings: severe headache, visual disturbance, pain below the ribs, sudden swelling of the face or hands, a painful calf, vaginal bleeding or reduced fetal movements mean contacting a midwife or maternity unit the same day, and chest pain, severe breathlessness, fainting or heavy bleeding need emergency care.

    Conditions

    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Pelvic organ prolapse Descent of one or more of the pelvic organs (the bladder, uterus, rectum, small bowel or, after hysterectomy.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Symptoms

    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Pelvic pain Pain felt low in the abdomen, below the navel and between the hip bones.
    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.

    Medical tests

    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • Ferritin test A blood test that estimates the body's iron stores by measuring ferritin, the protein that holds iron inside cells.

    Biomarkers

    • hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin) The hormone made from implantation onwards by the trophoblast, the tissue that becomes the placenta.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.

    Imaging

    • Ultrasound High-frequency sound waves bounced off tissue give real-time, radiation-free images, used for pregnancy, the abdomen, the heart (echocardiography).

    Procedures

    • Caesarean section Birth of a baby through a cut in the lower abdomen and in the lower segment of the uterus.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.

    Guides and news

    • NHS antenatal care: appointments and scans The NHS offers antenatal appointments with a midwife, two ultrasound scans and optional screening tests.
    • Early pregnancy signs and contacting a midwife The NHS says a missed period is the earliest sign of pregnancy, and many areas let people refer themselves to a midwife.
    • Pregnancy scans and tests on the NHS NHS pregnancy care includes booking blood tests, a dating scan and a mid-pregnancy anomaly scan, plus optional screening for Down's.
    • WHO tracks extreme heat risks in pregnancy On 21 October 2026 the WHO released a new global framework of priority indicators for tracking the health risks of extreme heat for pregnant women.
    • WHO and Lancet series on bleeding after birth On 12 June 2026 the WHO publicised a Lancet Series on postpartum haemorrhage, severe bleeding after birth, and what it says can be done about it.
    • WHO roadmap on hypertension in pregnancy In October 2026 the WHO and partners launched a global roadmap for 2026 to 2035 on hypertensive disorders in pregnancy, including pre-eclampsia.
