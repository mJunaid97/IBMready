    Digestion

    Food is broken down mechanically by chewing and churning and chemically by enzymes, from starch in the mouth to proteins and fats in the small intestine.

    Open in the 3D explorerStomach anatomy

    Chewing mixes food with saliva, whose amylase starts on starch. The stomach adds hydrochloric acid, which unfolds proteins and kills microbes, and pepsin, which begins their breakdown; it churns the mix into a paste called chyme and releases it into the duodenum a few millilitres at a time.

    In the duodenum, bile from the liver emulsifies fat into droplets and pancreatic juice supplies the main enzymes: lipase for fat, amylase for starch and proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin) for protein. Enzymes on the intestinal lining finish the job, splitting sugars and peptides into single molecules ready for absorption.

    Digestion is regulated by nerves and hormones: the sight and smell of food prime the stomach (vagus nerve), gastrin drives acid, and cholecystokinin from the duodenum squeezes the gallbladder and slows the stomach when fat arrives.

    The small intestine is where most chemical digestion and almost all absorption take place, and the final steps happen on the surface of the lining itself. Fixed to the membranes of the microvilli, the brush border enzymes finish what the pancreas started: sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose, maltase converts maltose to glucose, and aminopeptidase and dipeptidase clip peptides down to single amino acids. The brush border also controls the pancreas's most dangerous products: enteropeptidase converts inactive trypsinogen to trypsin only once it reaches the intestine, and trypsin then activates the other pancreatic proteases, so the pancreas does not digest itself. Fat is handled differently: lingual and gastric lipases make a start in the mouth and stomach, but most triglyceride arrives in the duodenum undigested and depends on bile to emulsify it before pancreatic lipase can act.

    Control is shared between the nervous and endocrine systems. Nerves link the brain and spinal cord to the gut, so the brain can signal the salivary glands as soon as food is seen or smelled, and both arms of the autonomic nervous system influence the process, the parasympathetic nerves stimulating secretion and peristalsis and the sympathetic nerves tending to inhibit them. The lining of the stomach and small intestine also releases its own hormones, among them gastrin, secretin and cholecystokinin, and specialised enteroendocrine cells in the small intestine convert the detection of nutrients into hormone signals, including the incretins GLP-1 and GIP, that reach the brain and the pancreas. Signals also travel from gut to brain through the vagal and spinal nerves within fractions of a second, far faster than hormones diffusing through the blood.

    When the sequence is disturbed, symptoms follow the mechanism. After stomach surgery, food can move too quickly from the stomach into the duodenum, the gut releases more hormones than normal and the result is dumping syndrome, with cramps, faintness and diarrhoea after meals. Because digestion depends on nerves, hormones, gut bacteria, blood supply and the organs acting together, clinicians assess it by asking what fails: upper endoscopy to inspect the lining, tests of pancreatic enzyme output when fat is poorly digested, and breath or blood tests when a single brush border enzyme such as lactase is suspected to be deficient.

    Conditions

    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.

    Medical tests

    • Endoscopy (as a test) A flexible or rigid tube with a light and camera passed into a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Vitamin B12 and folate Two blood tests, usually ordered together, for the vitamins the bone marrow needs to make normal red cells and the nervous system needs to keep…

    Biomarkers

    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • Folate The B vitamin from green vegetables and fortified foods that cells need to make DNA; deficiency causes anaemia with large red cells and.
    • Vitamin B12 The vitamin, absorbed with intrinsic factor in the last part of the small intestine.

    Imaging

    • Fluoroscopy and angiography Continuous X-ray imaging shown as a live moving picture, used to watch swallowing.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Esomeprazole Esomeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes.
    • Lansoprazole Lansoprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers stomach acid, used for indigestion, heartburn, reflux disease and stomach ulcers.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Drug classes

    • Proton pump inhibitors The strongest acid-reducing medicines (omeprazole, lansoprazole, pantoprazole, esomeprazole).

    Guides and news

    • Colic in babies: what it is and what helps Colic is a baby crying a lot with no obvious cause; NHS guidance says it usually gets better by around 3 or 4 months.
    • The Eatwell Guide: a balanced diet explained The Eatwell Guide shows the proportions of food groups in a balanced UK diet, with fruit.
    • Gastroscopy: what happens and what to expect A gastroscopy uses a thin camera to look at the gullet and stomach.
