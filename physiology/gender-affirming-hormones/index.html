    Gender-affirming hormone therapy: what changes in the body

    Feminising therapy (oestradiol with testosterone suppression) and masculinising therapy (testosterone) shift the body's sex-hormone environment towards that of the affirmed gender, producing gradual changes in fat, muscle, skin, hair, breast tissue and the reproductive organs over months to years, some of them permanent.

    Open in the 3D explorerBreasts anatomy

    Gender-affirming hormone therapy is one of the medical options for trans women, trans men and non-binary people who experience gender incongruence, which the World Health Organization's ICD-11 classifies under conditions related to sexual health and not as a mental illness. Treatment follows the WPATH, Endocrine Society and UCSF guidelines; in England it is started by NHS gender dysphoria clinics, with prescribing and monitoring often shared with the GP. Both regimens reduce fertility, so fertility preservation (storing sperm or eggs) is discussed before starting. Whether to start, which preparation to use and for how long are individual decisions made with a specialist team, and some effects reverse if treatment stops while others, once established, are permanent.

    Feminising therapy uses oestradiol, given as a patch, gel, tablet or injection, with a medicine that suppresses testosterone (spironolactone, cyproterone acetate or a GnRH analogue), which is no longer needed once the testes have been removed. Ethinylestradiol, the oestrogen used in the combined contraceptive pill, is not used for this purpose because it carries a higher clot risk and its level cannot be monitored. The guideline timetables give reduced libido and fewer spontaneous erections within one to three months; softer skin, fat moving to the hips and thighs, loss of muscle mass and the start of breast growth at three to six months, with breast development at its maximum after two to three years; and shrinking testes with falling sperm production. Breast tissue that has developed does not disappear if oestradiol is stopped. Facial and body hair becomes finer and slower growing from about six to twelve months but rarely disappears, so many trans women also use electrolysis or laser hair removal. Oestradiol does not raise the pitch of the voice or alter the adult skeleton, and the prostate remains in place.

    Masculinising therapy uses testosterone, usually as a daily gel or as injections given at intervals from weekly to every few months depending on the preparation. Skin becomes oilier and acne is common early, periods usually stop within two to six months, and the clitoris enlarges while the vaginal lining thins and becomes drier. The voice deepens over the first year and the change is permanent; facial and body hair start to grow within about three to six months and keep increasing for three to five years; muscle grows and fat moves to the abdomen; male-pattern hair loss can develop. Facial and body hair growth and clitoral enlargement also persist if testosterone is stopped. Testosterone raises the red cell count and is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can still occur after periods stop. Height and bone structure do not change, and breast tissue may lose some fat but does not go away; it is removed only by chest surgery. The uterus, ovaries and cervix remain unless they are removed, so anyone with a cervix, including trans men and non-binary people, still needs cervical screening.

    Monitoring follows the guidelines: oestradiol and testosterone are measured every few months in the first year and then once or twice a year, aiming for the range typical of the affirmed gender, alongside blood pressure, lipids, glucose or HbA1c, weight and liver function. Potassium and kidney function are checked on spironolactone because it is potassium-sparing, haemoglobin and haematocrit on testosterone because a raised red cell mass (erythrocytosis) thickens the blood, and some guidelines (the Endocrine Society) also include prolactin, since oestrogen can raise it. Bone density is assessed after the gonads have been removed if hormones are not being taken. The main risk of oestrogen is venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism), highest with oral preparations, smoking and immobility, so transdermal oestradiol is preferred for people at higher risk. Sudden pain or swelling in a calf, or breathlessness or chest pain, needs urgent medical assessment, whatever the preparation. Screening invitations follow the gender recorded with the GP, so some trans people need to ask for the screening that matches their anatomy: breast screening for trans women on long-term oestrogen and for trans men and non-binary people who have not had chest surgery, and cervical screening for anyone who still has a cervix. The prostate is not removed in feminising surgery, so prostate symptoms are still assessed in the usual way and a PSA result is interpreted in the light of testosterone suppression.
