    Hormones

    Chemical messengers released into the blood that act on distant cells carrying the right receptor, coordinating growth, metabolism, stress and reproduction over minutes to years.

    Open in the 3D explorerPituitary gland anatomy

    Hormones come in families. Peptides and proteins (insulin, growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone) act on surface receptors and work within minutes. Steroids (cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, oestrogen) are made from cholesterol, pass into cells and switch genes on or off over hours. Amines include adrenaline, fast and short-lived, and thyroid hormone, slow and long-lasting.

    The same hormone can do different things in different tissues: adrenaline speeds the heart, dilates airways and releases glucose all at once, which is exactly what a fight-or-flight response needs. Concentrations are tiny, measured in picomoles, so small changes matter and assays must be precise.

    Because hormones reach everywhere, endocrine disease is multi-system: an overactive thyroid causes weight loss, tremor, palpitations, heat intolerance and anxiety together.

    Chemistry determines how a hormone travels and where it acts. Amine hormones are built from a single amino acid, tryptophan or tyrosine; peptide and protein hormones are chains of amino acids; steroids are derived from cholesterol. Steroid and thyroid hormones are lipid-soluble, so they diffuse through the cell membrane and bind receptors inside the cell, usually altering which genes are transcribed. Every other hormone is water-soluble and must bind a receptor on the cell surface, typically coupled to a G protein that triggers a cascade of second messengers inside the cell; this is why water-soluble hormones act quickly and lipid-soluble hormones act slowly but durably. A hormone only affects cells that carry its receptor, which is how a signal that reaches every tissue can have selective effects: aldosterone raises blood sodium while cortisol raises blood glucose.

    Hormone disorders arise when a level is too high or too low, or when the body no longer responds to a hormone normally; diabetes is the most common endocrine disease in the United States. Treatment works on the same logic in reverse: controlling how much hormone a gland makes when there is excess, or replacing the hormone when there is too little.

    Hormones are measured in blood, urine or saliva, and interpretation depends on timing and context. Cortisol varies through the day, so it is sampled early in the morning or collected over a full day; thyroid hormones can be measured as total (bound plus free) or as the free fraction that is biologically active, and a low free thyroxine may reflect an underactive thyroid but also severe acute illness or malnutrition. Medicines, obesity, depression and stress can all distort results, so an unexpected value is usually repeated and read together with the hormone that controls it before a diagnosis is made.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Acne Acne is a common skin condition in which spots, from a few small ones to deep painful lumps, appear mostly on the face, chest and back.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • BNP and NT-proBNP test A blood test for the natriuretic peptides the heart's ventricles release when they are stretched by high pressure or extra volume.
    • Pregnancy test Detects human chorionic gonadotropin, a hormone made by the placenta from about a week after implantation.
    • TSH test The single most sensitive blood test of thyroid function: TSH from the pituitary rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is…
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.
    • 25-hydroxyvitamin D The storage form of vitamin D that the liver makes, the best single measure of vitamin D status and the value the vitamin D test reports.

    Imaging

    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.
    • Mammography A low-dose X-ray of the compressed breast that can detect cancers years before they can be felt.

    Medications

    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Estradiol (oestrogen) The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the…
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Testosterone The main androgen, given as a gel, patch, injection or implant to replace what the testes cannot make in confirmed hypogonadism and as the…

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Androgens Steroid hormones that act on the androgen receptor.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Bisphosphonates Medicines that slow bone loss by disabling the cells that dissolve bone.
    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
    • Insulins Injected replacements for the hormone the pancreas no longer makes or cannot make enough of: essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2…
    • Thyroid hormones Synthetic thyroxine (levothyroxine) replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring metabolic rate.
