    Glomerular filtration

    Blood pressure forces water and small solutes out of the glomerular capillaries into the nephron, about 180 litres a day, while cells and proteins stay behind.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    Each kidney holds about a million nephrons. In the glomerulus a tuft of leaky capillaries sits inside Bowman's capsule; the pressure difference drives an ultrafiltrate of plasma through a three-layer sieve that blocks cells and almost all protein. The glomerular filtration rate (GFR), roughly 120 ml per minute in a healthy young adult, is the standard measure of kidney function.

    The kidney keeps GFR steady across a wide range of blood pressures by adjusting its afferent and efferent arterioles; drugs that act on these vessels (NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors) can drop filtration in a dehydrated patient.

    Because creatinine is filtered freely and barely reabsorbed, its blood level rises as GFR falls, which is how kidney disease is staged. Protein in the urine means the sieve is damaged.

    The sieve has three layers: the fenestrated endothelium of the glomerular capillary, the glomerular basement membrane and the podocytes whose interlocking foot processes form the final barrier, with the filtrate collecting in Bowman's space beyond. Filtration is by size and charge, so water, ions, glucose, urea and small peptides pass while cells and all but the smallest proteins are held back. The driving force is a balance of Starling forces: the hydrostatic pressure in the glomerular capillary pushes fluid out and is opposed by the osmotic pull of the plasma proteins and by the pressure already in the capsule; the net filtration pressure is the modest difference. Because the glomerulus sits between two arterioles, the kidney can set that pressure by adjusting their resistances, and two built-in mechanisms, the myogenic response of the afferent arteriole and tubuloglomerular feedback from the macula densa, hold renal blood flow and filtration steady across a wide range of arterial pressures.

    Nearly all of the filtrate is reabsorbed, so a huge filtration rate produces only a litre or two of urine. The rate is slightly higher in men than in women and declines slowly with age. It can be measured directly from the clearance of a marker that is freely filtered and neither secreted nor reabsorbed by the tubules, but in routine practice it is estimated from the serum creatinine, adjusted for age and sex, as the eGFR, and this estimate is what stages chronic kidney disease.

    The estimate has a well-known blind spot. When some nephrons are lost, the survivors filter harder, so the overall rate falls far less than the amount of damaged tissue would suggest and symptoms appear only when very little function remains. Dehydration, heart failure and drugs that relax the efferent arteriole or constrict the afferent one can all drop filtration abruptly, which is why kidney function is checked when these treatments are started, while protein or blood in the urine signals that the barrier itself is damaged, as in glomerulonephritis or diabetic kidney disease.

    Connected pages in detail

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Glomerular filtration.

    Conditions

    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Creatinine and eGFR The everyday measure of how well the kidneys filter.

    Biomarkers

    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) An estimate of how many millilitres of blood the kidneys filter each minute, calculated from creatinine together with age and sex.
    • Urea The nitrogen-containing waste the liver makes from protein breakdown, cleared by the kidneys.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Iodinated contrast agents The iodine-containing dyes injected for CT scans and angiography that make blood vessels, organs and tumours show up.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.
    • Contrast agents Substances given to make blood vessels, organs, the gut or joints stand out on imaging: iodinated agents for X-ray and CT, gadolinium agents for MRI.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.
