    Gas exchange

    Oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the thin wall between alveoli and capillaries by diffusion, driven by differences in partial pressure.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    The lungs contain about 300 million alveoli with a combined surface close to the size of a tennis court, separated from blood by a membrane under a micrometre thick. Oxygen diffuses into the blood because its partial pressure in alveolar air (about 100 mmHg) exceeds that in returning venous blood (about 40 mmHg); carbon dioxide moves the other way down a smaller gradient because it diffuses twenty times more readily.

    Efficient exchange needs ventilation and blood flow to be matched. Blood sent to unventilated alveoli (as in pneumonia) is not oxygenated, and ventilated alveoli without blood flow (as in pulmonary embolism) are wasted breath. The lungs partly correct this by constricting vessels in poorly ventilated regions.

    Thickening of the membrane (fibrosis) or fluid in the alveoli (pulmonary oedema) slows diffusion, and oxygen, with its lower solubility, is affected first.

    Two physical laws govern the exchange. Dalton's law states that each gas in a mixture exerts its own partial pressure independently of the others, and Henry's law that the amount of a gas dissolving in a liquid is proportional to its partial pressure; gas therefore always moves down its pressure gradient, from alveolar air into capillary blood for oxygen and in the opposite direction for carbon dioxide. Once in the blood, oxygen is bound to haemoglobin inside red cells, while most carbon dioxide travels as bicarbonate: it accumulates inside the red cell and then leaves for the plasma in exchange for chloride ions, with a smaller share carried bound to haemoglobin as carbaminohaemoglobin. Binding and release of both gases depend on their local partial pressures, so blood leaving the lungs holds less carbon dioxide than the tissues it will perfuse. In the fetus the same principles operate across the placenta, where the mother's blood plays the part of alveolar air.

    The lungs tune ventilation and perfusion to each other locally. Ventilation is regulated by the diameter of the airways and perfusion by the diameter of the blood vessels; a rise in carbon dioxide in the alveoli widens the bronchioles so that carbon dioxide is cleared faster, while a fall in alveolar oxygen constricts the arterioles supplying them, diverting blood to better-ventilated regions, complementing the constriction of vessels in poorly ventilated regions. Surfactant, secreted into the alveoli, lowers the effort needed to inflate the lungs and keeps the alveoli from collapsing on exhalation; without it the exchange surface shrinks, as in the premature newborn.

    Gas exchange is assessed from the blood it produces. Pulse oximetry uses a probe on the fingertip or ear to estimate, without a blood sample, the percentage of haemoglobin carrying oxygen, but it says nothing about carbon dioxide or acid-base balance. An arterial blood gas sample, usually taken in hospital, measures the oxygen and carbon dioxide dissolved in arterial blood together with its pH, and is the test used when the question is whether exchange is failing, for example in severe pneumonia or an exacerbation of COPD, where carbon dioxide retention changes management. Lung function tests, chest X-ray and CT then separate problems of ventilation, of the membrane and of blood flow.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).

    Medical tests

    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Biomarkers

    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.
    • CT scan A rotating X-ray tube and computer build cross-sectional slices of the body in seconds, showing bone, organs.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…

    Drug classes

    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.

    Guides and news

    • Stopping smoking: the support the NHS offers NHS stop smoking services offer nicotine replacement, medicines and professional support.
