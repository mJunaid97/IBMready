    Breathing (ventilation)

    Air moves in and out because muscles change the volume of the chest: the diaphragm and external intercostals enlarge it for inspiration; relaxation and elastic recoil empty it.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    When the diaphragm contracts its dome flattens and the ribs swing up and out, lowering pressure inside the chest below atmospheric pressure so air flows in. Quiet expiration is passive: the stretched lungs and chest wall recoil. Forced breathing recruits the abdominal muscles and internal intercostals to push air out, and the scalenes and sternocleidomastoids to pull the ribs up.

    The rhythm is generated in the medulla and pons and adjusted by chemoreceptors that sense carbon dioxide and, to a lesser extent, oxygen. A rising carbon dioxide level is the strongest normal drive to breathe.

    Airway resistance matters: narrowing the bronchi even slightly, as in asthma, greatly increases the work of breathing because resistance rises with the fourth power of the reduction in radius.

    The diaphragm is a dome-shaped sheet of muscle beneath the lungs. When it tightens, the dome flattens downward and the chest cavity enlarges; the muscles between the ribs help by lifting the rib cage. The lungs expand to fill the extra space and air is drawn in through the nose or mouth, down the windpipe and through the branching bronchial tubes to the alveoli, the tiny air sacs where exchange happens. Their walls are extremely thin and each is wrapped in pulmonary capillaries, so oxygen crosses into the blood and is bound by haemoglobin in red cells while carbon dioxide leaves the blood to be breathed out. Breathing out is normally passive: the diaphragm and rib muscles relax, the cavity shrinks and the elastic lungs recoil to push the air out, with the abdominal muscles joining in only during hard exercise, coughing or forced exhalation.

    The volume of each breath and the rate together set the ventilation, and both rise during exercise so that the extra carbon dioxide produced by the muscles is removed as fast as it is made; at rest the chemoreceptor response to carbon dioxide keeps the level remarkably constant. Residual air that stays in the lungs after the deepest expiration prevents the alveoli from collapsing between breaths, so each breath only has to top up a reservoir rather than inflate the lungs from empty.

    Breathing is assessed first by watching: rate, depth, use of the neck and shoulder muscles, and whether the person can speak in full sentences. Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast, and shows the narrowed airways of asthma or COPD as slow emptying; pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases show whether the exchange is keeping oxygen up and carbon dioxide down. Disorders of the pump, such as a weak diaphragm in neuromuscular disease, a stiff chest wall or depressed respiratory drive from opioids, reduce ventilation even when the lungs themselves are healthy, and the first sign is a rising carbon dioxide level.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Imaging

    • X-ray The oldest and most widely available imaging test: a brief beam of X-rays casts a shadow picture of bone, air and soft tissue, ideal for fractures.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Influenza vaccine The yearly flu vaccine: an inactivated or live attenuated vaccine reformulated each season to match circulating influenza strains.
    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Morphine and opioids The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating.
    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…

    Drug classes

    • Bronchodilators Inhaled medicines that relax the muscle around the airways within minutes (salbutamol, terbutaline) or for about 12 hours (salmeterol.
    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.

    Guides and news

    • Altitude sickness symptoms and prevention Altitude sickness is illness from being high up with less oxygen; the NHS says mild cases ease with rest, and going up slowly helps prevent it.
    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
    • Panic attacks: what they are and what helps A panic attack is a rush of intense fear with physical symptoms such as a racing heartbeat.
    • Reliever and preventer inhalers explained A reliever inhaler eases symptoms when they happen, while a preventer is taken every day to reduce airway inflammation.
    • Shortness of breath: causes and next steps Breathlessness has many causes, from asthma and infections to heart and lung disease, and severe difficulty breathing needs a 999 call.
    • Spirometry: the lung function test explained Spirometry measures how much air can be blown out and how fast.
