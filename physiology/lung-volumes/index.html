    Lung volumes and capacities

    The amounts of air the lungs move and hold, measured by spirometry and used to separate obstructive from restrictive disease.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy

    A quiet breath moves about half a litre (tidal volume). Above it lies the inspiratory reserve and below it the expiratory reserve; together with tidal volume these make the vital capacity, roughly 4 to 5 litres in an adult man. Even after the deepest expiration about 1.2 litres remains as residual volume, keeping the alveoli open.

    Spirometry records the forced vital capacity (FVC) and the volume blown out in the first second (FEV1). Obstructive disease (asthma, COPD) narrows the airways so FEV1 falls out of proportion to FVC and the FEV1/FVC ratio drops below about 0.7. Restrictive disease (fibrosis, chest wall problems) shrinks all volumes but keeps the ratio normal.

    Volumes fall with age and smoking and rise with height and training, so reference values are matched to age, sex and height; reference values are matched to age, sex, height and ethnicity.

    The volumes are defined by what a breath can add or remove. Tidal volume is the air that enters during quiet breathing; the inspiratory reserve volume is the extra that a deep breath can draw in beyond it, and the expiratory reserve volume the extra that can be forced out after a normal exhalation. Residual volume, the air left after the strongest possible expiration, cannot be measured by blowing into a spirometer at all, yet it is protective: it keeps the alveoli from collapsing between breaths so that the next inspiration does not have to reopen them. Capacities are sums of volumes. Inspiratory capacity is tidal plus inspiratory reserve, functional residual capacity is expiratory reserve plus residual, vital capacity is everything except the residual, and total lung capacity is the whole, larger in men than in women and in tall people than in short.

    Spirometry is the standard measurement. Before the test the clinician checks that it is safe, since it is not performed during a chest infection, with certain heart problems or uncontrolled high blood pressure, or soon after a heart attack, stroke or some operations; height and weight are measured because the predicted values depend on them, and the person is told whether to pause inhalers. During the test they sit beside the spirometer wearing a soft nose clip, breathe in fully, seal their lips around the mouthpiece and blow out as hard and fast as they can, repeating the manoeuvre several times until the results are consistent. The test is painless and is stopped if chest pain or dizziness occurs. A reversibility test repeats the measurement after an inhaled bronchodilator to see whether the airways open, which supports a diagnosis of asthma.

    Results are compared with the values expected for the person's age, sex and height and are usually reported later rather than on the day. Effort matters: a half-hearted blow mimics restriction, which is why technicians coach and repeat. Narrowed airways in asthma or COPD slow the first second of expiration, while stiff lungs or a weak chest wall shrink the volumes without slowing the flow; when spirometry is equivocal, full lung function testing that measures residual volume and gas transfer, or a bronchial challenge test, is used.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Full pulmonary function tests Spirometry plus measurements it cannot make: the total volume of the lungs including the air that cannot be blown out.

    Medications

    • Salbutamol The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack…

    Drug classes

    • Bronchodilators Inhaled medicines that relax the muscle around the airways within minutes (salbutamol, terbutaline) or for about 12 hours (salmeterol.
