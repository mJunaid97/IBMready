    Nutrition

    Also known as: diet, healthy eating, Mediterranean diet, fibre, salt, sugar

    Food supplies energy, the building blocks for every tissue, and the vitamins and minerals that enzymes need. The pattern that best protects the heart, brain and gut across large studies is built on vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish and olive oil, with little processed meat, refined sugar and salt.

    Open in the 3D explorerStomach anatomy

    Carbohydrates are broken down to glucose in the small intestine and absorbed into the portal vein to the liver; fibre is the part that reaches the colon undigested, where bacteria ferment it to short-chain fatty acids that feed the bowel lining, lower inflammation and help regulate appetite. Whole grains, beans and vegetables deliver glucose slowly; refined sugar and white flour deliver it in a surge that demands more insulin.

    Protein provides amino acids for muscle, enzymes, hormones and immune cells; adults need about 0.75-1 g per kilogram a day, more when older or training. Fats build cell membranes and carry vitamins A, D, E and K: unsaturated fats from olive oil, nuts and oily fish lower LDL cholesterol, while saturated and especially industrial trans fats raise it.

    Salt draws water into the blood and raises blood pressure: most adults eat 8-9 g a day against a 5-6 g limit, three-quarters of it hidden in bread, processed meat and ready meals. Micronutrients matter in specific ways: iron for haemoglobin, calcium and vitamin D for bone, folate before and during pregnancy, B12 for nerves (a risk on vegan diets and with metformin), iodine for the thyroid.

    Effects on the body

    SystemEffect
    DigestiveFibre feeds the microbiome, prevents constipation, lowers bowel cancer risk by about 10% per 10 g a day
    ArteriesUnsaturated fats and fibre lower LDL; salt reduction lowers blood pressure; Mediterranean diet cuts cardiovascular events by about 30%
    EndocrineSlow-release carbohydrate and healthy weight prevent and can reverse type 2 diabetes
    SkeletonCalcium, vitamin D and protein maintain bone; vitamin D deficiency is common in northern winters
    Nervous systemMediterranean-style eating is linked to slower cognitive decline and lower depression risk

    Guidance

    • At least 5 portions of fruit and vegetables a day (400 g), varied in colour
    • Base meals on whole grains and legumes; aim for 30 g of fibre a day
    • Two portions of fish a week, one oily; limit red meat to about 70 g a day and avoid processed meat
    • Salt under 6 g (2.4 g sodium) a day; free sugars under 30 g a day (about 7 teaspoons); sugary drinks are the easiest cut
    • Choose unsaturated oils and spreads; keep saturated fat under 10% of energy
    • Vitamin D 10 micrograms daily in autumn and winter in northern latitudes; folic acid 400 micrograms when planning pregnancy

    Conditions

    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.

    Medical tests

    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.

    Biomarkers

    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.

    Guides and news

    • Preparing for a blood test: fasting explained Some blood tests need fasting or a change to what you eat, drink or take beforehand; the person who arranges the test should say which applies.
    • High cholesterol: diet, tests and medicines The NHS says cutting down on saturated fat can help lower cholesterol.
    • Cholesterol test results explained A cholesterol test reports total, HDL and non-HDL cholesterol plus triglycerides, and doctors read them with other risk factors.
    • Eating well with type 2 diabetes Diabetes UK says there is no special diet only for type 2 diabetes, and NICE advises the same healthy balanced eating as for everyone.
    • The Eatwell Guide: a balanced diet explained The Eatwell Guide shows the proportions of food groups in a balanced UK diet, with fruit.
    • HbA1c test: what it shows and why it matters The HbA1c test shows average blood glucose over the past two to three months and is used to diagnose and monitor diabetes.
    • How to read food labels: traffic lights Red, amber and green traffic light labels show whether a food is high, medium or low in fat, saturates, sugars and salt.
    • Looking after your teeth and gums The NHS describes brushing with fluoride toothpaste twice a day, cleaning between the teeth and cutting down on sugary food and drink.
    • Lowering blood pressure with lifestyle changes The NHS advises everyone with high blood pressure to make lifestyle changes, such as eating less salt, being more active and cutting back on alcohol.
    • Preventing food poisoning at home: the 4 Cs NHS inform and the Food Standards Agency describe four steps for food safety at home: cleaning, cooking, chilling and avoiding cross-contamination.
