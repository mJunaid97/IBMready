    Alcohol

    Also known as: drinking, units of alcohol, alcohol dependence, binge drinking

    Alcohol is absorbed from the stomach and small intestine, distributed through body water and processed by the liver at roughly one unit an hour. In the short term it depresses the brain; over years it damages the liver, heart, pancreas, nerves and brain and causes seven types of cancer. Risk rises with every drink; there is no fully safe level, but keeping under 14 units a week spread over several days keeps it low.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy

    The liver converts alcohol to acetaldehyde, a toxic carcinogen, and then to acetate. Fat accumulates in liver cells within days of heavy drinking (fatty liver, reversible with abstinence); continued drinking inflames the liver (alcoholic hepatitis) and scars it (cirrhosis), which blocks blood flow from the gut, backs pressure into oesophageal veins that can bleed, and destroys the liver's ability to make proteins and clear toxins.

    In the brain, alcohol enhances GABA inhibition and blocks glutamate: judgement, coordination and memory fail in proportion to the blood level, and at high levels breathing and gag reflexes are suppressed, which is how binge drinking kills. Regular use makes the brain adapt, so stopping suddenly causes rebound overactivity: tremor, seizures and delirium tremens, which can be fatal without medical withdrawal. Thiamine deficiency in heavy drinkers damages the memory circuits (Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome).

    Alcohol raises blood pressure, causes atrial fibrillation ('holiday heart') and weakens the heart muscle (cardiomyopathy); it inflames the pancreas and the stomach lining; and acetaldehyde plus its effect on oestrogen and folate causes cancers of the mouth, throat, oesophagus, liver, bowel and breast. Its calories (7 kcal per gram) contribute to weight gain, and it disrupts sleep architecture even in moderate amounts.

    Effects on the body

    SystemEffect
    DigestiveFatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis; pancreatitis; gastritis and ulcers; cancers of the mouth to bowel
    Nervous systemIntoxication, blackouts, dependence, withdrawal seizures, peripheral neuropathy, dementia, depression
    HeartHypertension, atrial fibrillation, cardiomyopathy, stroke
    EndocrineLow blood sugar in binges, raised triglycerides, gout, reduced testosterone
    ReproductiveFetal alcohol spectrum disorder: no safe amount in pregnancy

    Guidance

    • No more than 14 units a week for both men and women, spread over 3 or more days, with several drink-free days; a unit is 10 mL of pure alcohol, roughly half a pint of ordinary beer, two thirds of a small (125 mL) glass of wine (a small glass is about 1.5 units), or a single 25 mL measure of spirits
    • Avoid binges (more than 6 units in one session); eat before drinking and alternate with water
    • None in pregnancy or when planning one; none before driving, operating machinery or swimming
    • Warning signs of dependence: needing a drink to start the day, shaking when stopping, drinking more than intended, others expressing concern; anyone drinking heavily every day should not stop abruptly without medical advice
    • Liver tests, blood pressure and support services (talking therapies, medication such as acamprosate or naltrexone) are available through primary care

    Conditions

    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease Reflux of stomach acid into the oesophagus often enough to cause symptoms or damage the lining.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Epilepsy A tendency to recurrent, unprovoked seizures: bursts of abnormal synchronised electrical activity in the brain.

    Symptoms

    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Vomiting Forceful emptying of the stomach through the mouth, a protective reflex run by the brainstem that closes the airway.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.

    Biomarkers

    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • GGT (gamma-glutamyl transferase) A membrane enzyme of bile-duct and liver cells that rises with cholestasis, alcohol and many medicines.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
    • Triglycerides The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone.

    Guides and news

    • Alcohol and antibiotics: what the NHS says It depends on the antibiotic: the NHS says moderate drinking is unlikely to cause problems with common ones, but firm warnings exist for a few.
    • Alcohol units: the UK low-risk drinking advice The UK Chief Medical Officers advise adults not to regularly drink more than 14 units a week, spread over three days or more.
    • General anaesthetic: before, during, after A general anaesthetic puts a person into controlled unconsciousness for surgery.
    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
    • High blood pressure: what happens next High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms and is found by measuring it; treatment starts with lifestyle changes and may include medicine.
    • Liver blood tests: what the results mean Liver blood tests can point towards a possible problem but do not name the cause, and results that are not normal are very common.
    • Lowering blood pressure with lifestyle changes The NHS advises everyone with high blood pressure to make lifestyle changes, such as eating less salt, being more active and cutting back on alcohol.
    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
    • Warfarin and apixaban: medicines and food Bleeding is the main risk of both, so the NHS says other medicines, alcohol and, for warfarin, some foods and INR blood tests all matter.
