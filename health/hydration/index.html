    Hydration

    Also known as: water intake, dehydration, fluids, drinking water

    Water makes up about 60% of an adult's body and is the medium for every chemical reaction, the carrier for every nutrient and waste product, and the body's coolant. The kidneys and the thirst centre match intake to loss with remarkable precision; problems arise at the extremes: dehydration in heat, illness and old age, and, more rarely, drinking so much that sodium is diluted.

    Open in the 3D explorerKidneys anatomy

    Water leaves the body as urine (about 1.5 litres a day), sweat, water vapour in breath and a little in stool: roughly 2-2.5 litres in temperate conditions, far more in heat or exercise. About 20% of intake comes from food; the rest from drinks of any kind, since tea, coffee and milk count. Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a rise in blood concentration of as little as 1-2% and trigger thirst and the release of antidiuretic hormone, which tells the kidney's collecting ducts to reabsorb water and concentrate the urine.

    Mild dehydration (1-2% of body weight) causes thirst, dark urine, headache, tiredness and poorer concentration; moderate dehydration lowers blood pressure, speeds the heart and reduces urine output; severe dehydration causes confusion, kidney injury and collapse. Older people feel thirst less and have less kidney reserve, babies lose water fast through their large surface area and vomiting or diarrhoea, and both need watching in hot weather and illness.

    Overdrinking is the rarer danger: drinking several litres in a short time, particularly during endurance events or with some psychiatric conditions and drugs, dilutes blood sodium (hyponatraemia), swelling brain cells and causing headache, confusion, seizures and death. Sports drinks with electrolytes are needed only for sustained exercise over about an hour; for everyone else, water is the right drink, and sugary drinks are the ones to cut.

    Effects on the body

    SystemEffect
    UrinaryAdequate intake dilutes urine and halves the recurrence of kidney stones; concentrated urine irritates the bladder and favours infection
    Nervous systemDehydration impairs concentration, mood and reaction time and triggers headaches and migraine
    HeartLow volume drops blood pressure and causes dizziness on standing, especially with diuretics and in the elderly
    DigestiveFluid keeps stool soft; constipation worsens with dehydration
    SkinSweating is the main heat loss mechanism and fails without water; heat illness follows

    Guidance

    • Around 6-8 glasses (1.5-2 litres) of fluid a day for most adults, more in heat, exercise, fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, pregnancy and breastfeeding
    • Use urine colour as the guide: pale straw is right, dark yellow means drink more; thirst is a late signal in older people
    • Water, lower-fat milk and unsweetened tea or coffee are the best choices; limit fruit juice and smoothies to 150 mL a day and avoid sugary drinks
    • In illness with vomiting or diarrhoea, small frequent sips of oral rehydration solution replace salts as well as water; seek help for a baby or older person who is not passing urine, is drowsy or has sunken eyes
    • During prolonged exercise drink to thirst and include sodium; do not force fluids beyond that

    Conditions

    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Symptoms

    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.

    Guides and news

    • Heat exhaustion and heatstroke: what to know Heat exhaustion usually eases with cooling and rest, while heatstroke is an emergency for which the NHS says to call 999.
    • What is jet lag and what helps? Jet lag is a temporary sleep disturbance after long flights across time zones; the NHS says it usually improves within a few days.
    • Kidney stones: symptoms and treatment Kidney stones form when chemicals in urine build up; small ones may cause no symptoms, while larger ones can cause severe pain.
    • Avoiding and managing travellers' diarrhoea Travellers can lower the risk of diarrhoea with careful food, drink and hand hygiene, and replacing lost fluid is the main way it is managed.
    • UTI and cystitis: what they are A urinary tract infection (UTI) can affect the bladder, urethra or kidneys; cystitis is a UTI of the bladder, and antibiotics are not always needed.
