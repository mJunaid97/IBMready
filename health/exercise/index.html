    Exercise and physical activity

    Also known as: physical activity, fitness, strength training, aerobic exercise, cardio

    Movement is the single intervention that improves almost every organ system at once: it strengthens the heart, lowers blood pressure and blood sugar, builds muscle and bone, lifts mood and sleep, and cuts the risk of the commonest causes of death by a quarter or more.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy

    Aerobic activity (walking, cycling, swimming, running) trains the heart to pump more per beat, so resting heart rate falls and the same effort costs less. Capillaries multiply in muscle, mitochondria increase, and the muscles' ability to take up glucose without insulin improves within a single session, which is why activity is a treatment for type 2 diabetes.

    Strength training loads muscle and bone. Muscle fibres thicken in response to progressive overload; bone responds to impact and pulling by laying down more mineral, the only lifestyle measure that reliably builds bone density. After the age of 30, adults lose 3-8% of muscle mass per decade unless they resist it, and that loss drives falls, frailty and loss of independence in old age.

    Activity also acts on the brain: it raises BDNF and blood flow to the hippocampus, improves memory and attention, is as effective as medication for mild to moderate depression, and lowers dementia risk. Sitting for long periods is a risk factor independent of exercise, so breaking up sitting matters too.

    Effects on the body

    SystemEffect
    HeartLower resting heart rate and blood pressure, higher stroke volume, better coronary blood flow; 20-35% lower risk of coronary disease
    EndocrineImproved insulin sensitivity, lower HbA1c, 30-40% lower risk of type 2 diabetes
    MusclesHypertrophy and strength; preserved mass and function with age
    SkeletonHigher bone density and lower fracture risk with weight-bearing and resistance exercise
    Nervous systemBetter mood, sleep and cognition; lower risk of depression and dementia
    Lymphatic & immuneLower risk of bowel, breast and other cancers, partly via weight, insulin and inflammation

    Guidance

    • Adults: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity (brisk walking, where you can talk but not sing) or 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week, spread across the week
    • Muscle-strengthening activity on at least 2 days a week, working all major groups
    • Over 65: add balance and strength work (tai chi, dancing, stairs) to reduce falls
    • Minimise long periods of sitting; any movement is better than none, and benefits start well below the target
    • Children 5-17: 60 minutes a day, including vigorous and bone-strengthening activity

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Alzheimer's disease The commonest cause of dementia: a progressive loss of memory and thinking ability caused by the build-up of amyloid plaques and tau tangles and the…
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Cardiac stress test The heart is made to work hard, by treadmill or bicycle exercise or by a drug that mimics exercise, while its electrical activity.

    Biomarkers

    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.

    Guides and news

    • Getting active with a long-term condition UK guidance describes building up activity gradually from the current level.
    • Bone health: what helps keep bones strong NHS pages link bone health to weight-bearing exercise, calcium, vitamin D, not smoking and keeping alcohol low.
    • Falls in older people and reducing the risk Strength and balance exercises and simple changes at home can lower the risk of falls in older people, according to the NHS.
    • High blood pressure: what happens next High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms and is found by measuring it; treatment starts with lifestyle changes and may include medicine.
    • Lowering blood pressure with lifestyle changes The NHS advises everyone with high blood pressure to make lifestyle changes, such as eating less salt, being more active and cutting back on alcohol.
    • How much exercise adults need each week The NHS says adults aged 19 to 64 should do at least 150 minutes of moderate activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity a week, plus strength work.
