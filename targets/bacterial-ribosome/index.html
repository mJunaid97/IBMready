    Bacterial ribosome (50S subunit)

    Also known as: 50S ribosomal subunit, bacterial 70S ribosome, 23S rRNA

    The protein-making machinery of bacteria, built differently enough from the human ribosome to be a selective drug target. Macrolides such as azithromycin bind the 50S subunit and stop the bacterium making proteins, halting its growth.

    Open in the 3D explorerLungs anatomy
    Kind
    other
    Also called
    50S ribosomal subunit, bacterial 70S ribosome, 23S rRNA
    Where it is found
    The cytoplasm of bacteria; not present in human cytoplasm (mitochondrial ribosomes are related but distinct).

    What it does

    Every cell makes protein on ribosomes, but bacterial ribosomes (70S, made of 30S and 50S subunits) differ from human cytoplasmic ribosomes (80S) in their RNA and proteins, so antibiotics can bind them selectively. Macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin, erythromycin) bind the 23S ribosomal RNA in the exit tunnel of the 50S subunit and block the growing peptide chain from leaving, which stops protein synthesis; the effect is mainly bacteriostatic, leaving the immune system to clear the organism. Because macrolides reach very high concentrations inside cells, they are effective against intracellular organisms such as Chlamydia, Mycoplasma and Legionella that penicillins cannot reach. Other classes target the ribosome at different sites: tetracyclines and aminoglycosides on the 30S subunit, clindamycin and linezolid on the 50S. Human mitochondria have ribosomes of bacterial ancestry, which may contribute to some antibiotic toxicities. Resistance follows methylation of the binding site or efflux pumps.

    Role in the body

    Bacterial protein synthesis (translation); inhibition halts bacterial growth.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.

    Symptoms

    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.

    Drug classes

    • Macrolides Antibiotics that stop bacteria making proteins by binding the bacterial ribosome, used for chest, ear.
