    Xanthine oxidase

    Also known as: xanthine oxidoreductase, XO, XDH

    The enzyme that makes uric acid from the purines released when cells and DNA break down. Allopurinol and febuxostat block it, lowering uric acid so that the crystals of gout dissolve and new attacks stop.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy
    Kind
    enzyme
    Also called
    xanthine oxidoreductase, XO, XDH
    Where it is found
    The cytoplasm of liver and gut cells, and vascular endothelium; also present in milk and the mammary gland.

    What it does

    Purines from the diet and from the turnover of the body's own cells are degraded to hypoxanthine and xanthine, and xanthine oxidase converts both, in two steps, to uric acid. Humans lack the enzyme most mammals use to break uric acid down further, so blood levels sit close to the point at which urate crystals form, especially in cool joints such as the big toe. Allopurinol, a purine analogue, is itself converted by xanthine oxidase into oxypurinol, which inhibits the enzyme for the whole day; febuxostat inhibits it non-competitively. Both lower serum urate within days, but dissolving existing crystals takes months, during which attacks can be provoked, so treatment is started low, raised gradually against the urate level and covered with an anti-inflammatory. Xanthine oxidase also metabolises azathioprine and mercaptopurine, so inhibiting it can raise those drugs to dangerous levels; the enzyme's production of reactive oxygen species links it to tissue injury after ischaemia.

    Role in the body

    Final steps of purine catabolism to uric acid; a source of reactive oxygen species.

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Xanthine oxidase.

    Conditions

    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.

    Medications

    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.

    Drug classes

    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.
    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
