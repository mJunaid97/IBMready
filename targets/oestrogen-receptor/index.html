    Oestrogen receptor

    Also known as: ER, ERα, ERβ, estrogen receptor, ESR1, ESR2

    The nuclear receptor through which oestradiol acts on the uterus, breast, bone, blood vessels, liver and brain. Estradiol replacement, hormone replacement therapy and feminising hormone therapy act through it, and most breast cancers carry it, which is why ER status is tested on every breast cancer and ER-positive tumours are treated with endocrine therapy.

    Where it is found
    Nuclei of cells in the endometrium and myometrium, breast ducts and lobules, ovarian granulosa cells, bone, liver, blood vessel walls, fat, hypothalamus and pituitary, and in men the testes and prostate; ERα predominates in uterus, breast, liver and bone, ERβ in ovary, prostate, lung and brain.

    What it does

    Oestradiol, the main oestrogen, is a lipid-soluble steroid that crosses the cell membrane and binds oestrogen receptors in the nucleus. The bound receptor pairs up, attaches to oestrogen response elements in DNA and recruits co-activators or co-repressors, switching genes on or off over hours to days. In the uterus oestradiol makes the endometrium proliferate and induces progesterone receptors in it; in the breast it grows the ducts; in bone it restrains osteoclasts and, at puberty, fuses the growth plates in girls and boys alike (boys make the oestradiol from testosterone with aromatase); in the liver it raises binding proteins and clotting factors and shifts cholesterol towards HDL; in vessels it promotes dilation; and in the hypothalamus it feeds back on GnRH and steadies the temperature-regulating centre, which is why its withdrawal at the menopause brings hot flushes, bone loss and vaginal thinning. Estradiol given as HRT or feminising therapy is a full agonist at the receptor. Tamoxifen and raloxifene are selective modulators that block it in the breast while stimulating it in bone, and aromatase inhibitors starve it of ligand. Most breast cancers express the receptor and grow in response to oestrogen, so ER status is measured on every breast cancer biopsy and ER-positive tumours are treated with endocrine therapy. Endometriosis and fibroids are likewise oestrogen-dependent and regress after the menopause.

    Role in the body

    Transcriptional control of the endometrium and menstrual cycle, breast development, bone density and growth plate closure in both sexes, vascular tone and lipid metabolism, hypothalamic feedback and temperature regulation.

    What each linked page on Anatomy Nexus covers, in a line, for Oestrogen receptor.

    Conditions

    • Breast cancer A malignant tumour arising from the cells that line the ducts or lobules of the breast.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Endometriosis A chronic condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) grows outside the uterus, most often on the ovaries.
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.

    Symptoms

    • Unusual vaginal bleeding Bleeding from the vagina at a time when a period is not due: between periods, after sex.

    Biomarkers

    • Oestradiol The main oestrogen of the reproductive years, made chiefly by the growing ovarian follicle and, in smaller amounts.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.
    • HDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by high-density lipoprotein, the particle that collects cholesterol from tissues and artery walls and returns it to the liver.
