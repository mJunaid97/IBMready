    Androgen receptor

    Also known as: AR, testosterone receptor, dihydrotestosterone receptor, NR3C4

    The nuclear receptor through which testosterone and dihydrotestosterone act on the genitals, prostate, muscle, bone, bone marrow, skin, hair and brain. Testosterone treatment for hypogonadism and masculinising gender-affirming therapy act through it; anti-androgens block it in feminising therapy and in prostate cancer.

    Also called
    AR, testosterone receptor, dihydrotestosterone receptor, NR3C4
    Where it is found
    Nuclei of cells in the prostate, seminal vesicles, testes (Sertoli and Leydig cells), penis and clitoris, skeletal muscle, bone, bone marrow, sebaceous glands and hair follicles, larynx, hypothalamus and brain, ovarian theca cells and the adrenal glands.

    What it does

    Testosterone, made by the Leydig cells of the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands, crosses into cells and binds the androgen receptor in the nucleus; in the prostate, skin and hair follicles it is first converted by 5-alpha-reductase to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which binds the receptor more tightly. The bound receptor attaches to androgen response elements in DNA and changes which genes are transcribed over hours to days. Before birth testosterone acting on this receptor forms the internal male ducts and, as DHT, the prostate and external genitalia. At puberty and afterwards it drives growth of the testes, penis and larynx, sperm production, muscle, bone density, red cell production in the marrow, sebum, beard and body hair, and libido. In women it mediates the smaller amounts of androgen from the ovaries and adrenals acting on libido, muscle and bone, and its overstimulation in polycystic ovary syndrome produces hirsutism and acne; DHT on scalp follicles causes male-pattern hair loss. Testosterone given for hypogonadism or masculinising therapy is a full agonist and produces the same changes in trans men and non-binary people. Anti-androgens block the receptor: cyproterone acetate and spironolactone in feminising therapy, bicalutamide in prostate cancer, which grows through this receptor; 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors such as finasteride cut DHT production.

    Role in the body

    Male development of the internal and external genitalia before birth, puberty and spermatogenesis, and lifelong maintenance of muscle mass, bone density, red cell production, sebum and hair growth, libido and prostate size.
