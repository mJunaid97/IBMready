    Beta-1 adrenergic receptor

    Also known as: β1 receptor, beta-1 adrenoceptor, ADRB1

    The receptor in heart muscle and the heart's pacemaker tissue through which adrenaline and noradrenaline speed the heart and strengthen its contraction. Beta blockers occupy it to slow the heart, lower its oxygen demand and protect it after a heart attack and in heart failure.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy
    Kind
    receptor
    Also called
    β1 receptor, beta-1 adrenoceptor, ADRB1
    Where it is found
    Sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes, atrial and ventricular myocardium, and the juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney.

    What it does

    Sympathetic nerves and the adrenal medulla act on the heart through beta-1 receptors on the sinoatrial node (faster rate), the atrioventricular node (faster conduction) and the muscle of the atria and ventricles (stronger contraction and faster relaxation). Beta-1 receptors on the kidney's juxtaglomerular cells also release renin. Blocking them reduces heart rate and contractility, which cuts the heart's oxygen consumption (the basis of angina relief), suppresses abnormal rhythms, lowers blood pressure partly through less renin, and in chronic heart failure protects the myocardium from sustained sympathetic overdrive. Cardioselective beta blockers such as bisoprolol and metoprolol prefer beta-1 over the beta-2 receptors of the airways, though selectivity is relative and asthma remains a caution. Sudden withdrawal after long use can rebound with tachycardia and angina because the receptors have become more numerous.

    Role in the body

    Increases heart rate, conduction velocity and contractility in response to catecholamines; stimulates renin release.

    Medicines that act on it

    Metoprolol and beta blockers

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
