    Warfarin

    The long-established oral anticoagulant that blocks vitamin K-dependent clotting factors; effective for mechanical heart valves and clots but needs regular INR blood tests and careful dosing. Its main serious harm is bleeding, which US labelling carries as a boxed warning, and because the dose each person needs varies with diet, illness, alcohol and a very long list of interacting medicines, treatment is organised around the INR test, an anticoagulant alert card and a steady routine rather than around a fixed dose.

    Brand names
    UK Marevan · US Coumadin, Jantoven
    Routes
    Oral
    Dosage forms
    Tablet, Suspension
    Usual preparations
    Tablets 0.5, 1, 3 and 5 mg (colour-coded), taken once daily at the same time (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Therapeutic after 3-5 days; effect persists 3-5 days after stopping. Reversed with vitamin K (slow) or prothrombin complex concentrate (immediate). Because the effect lingers for days, stopping is never done without the anticoagulant clinic's advice.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 11289 · ATC B01AA03 · FDA EPC Vitamin K Antagonist [EPC]
    Monitoring
    INR every few days at first, then every 4-12 weeks once stable, and after any new drug or illness; self-testing meters are available. The NHS describes the INR being checked often, sometimes every other day, until the dose is right and less frequently once stable, with the dose adjusted by the clinic until the INR sits in the target range; home INR meters can be used after training but are usually bought privately. Between tests people are told to report bleeding, head injuries and new medicines straight away. Under-anticoagulation leaves the person exposed to clots and over-anticoagulation to bleeding, so both directions matter.

    What it is used for

    • Mechanical heart valves (the only oral anticoagulant licensed)
    • Atrial fibrillation with moderate or severe mitral stenosis, and antiphospholipid syndrome
    • Venous thromboembolism where DOACs are unsuitable (severe kidney failure, cost)
    • Historically the standard anticoagulant, now largely replaced by DOACs for other indications; US information lists prevention and treatment of blood clots and prevention of complications in people with certain irregular heart rhythms, mechanical valves or a previous heart attack or stroke

    How it works in the body

    Blocks vitamin K epoxide reductase in the liver, so vitamin K cannot be recycled to activate clotting factors II, VII, IX and X. Existing factors take days to be cleared, so the effect starts after 2-3 days and heparin cover is needed for immediate protection. The dose needed varies enormously between people and with diet, illness and other drugs, so the INR blood test tunes it. StatPearls summarises the action as blocking the production of the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors, which lowers the blood's ability to clot, and describes warfarin as a narrow-therapeutic-index drug: the gap between too little effect (clots) and too much (bleeding) is small, which is why it is subject to so many clinically important interactions and why close INR monitoring is regarded as essential. The dependence on vitamin K explains the dietary advice: vitamin K-rich foods such as leafy green vegetables, chickpeas and liver push the INR down, so the NHS advises keeping intake consistent rather than avoiding these foods, while cranberry juice and grapefruit juice increase the blood-thinning effect and are avoided. The effect also outlasts the last dose by several days, so stopping or interrupting warfarin before operations and dental extractions is planned by the clinic, never by the person alone. The same mechanism is used against rodents: warfarin was first marketed as a rodenticide, and the far more potent 'superwarfarin' pesticides cause prolonged anticoagulation when ingested. A rare inherited warfarin resistance means some people need unusually high doses to reach the same effect.

    Common side effects

    • Bruising, bleeding gums, nosebleeds: the NHS lists unexplained bruising and nosebleeds among the signs of bleeding to report
    • Hair thinning, rash
    • Heavier menstrual bleeding

    Serious: seek help

    • Major bleeding, including intracranial: risk rises steeply when the INR is above 4. Warning signs are blood in the urine or stools, coughing up or vomiting blood, and a sudden severe headache with confusion or weakness, which can mean bleeding in the brain; any head injury on warfarin needs urgent assessment (NHS 111) even without visible bleeding
    • US labelling carries a boxed warning that warfarin can cause serious, sometimes fatal bleeding, more likely in older people, at higher doses and with long-term use; newer DOACs cause about half as many bleeds into the brain as warfarin
    • Skin necrosis in the first days (protein C deficiency), purple toe syndrome; a painful skin rash can also signal calciphylaxis, a rare condition in which small blood vessels calcify
    • Fetal harm: teratogenic in the first trimester and bleeding later; StatPearls notes it is generally avoided in pregnancy but considered in selected high-risk situations such as mechanical heart valves, and that it is compatible with breastfeeding because little passes into milk
    • Serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), rare

    Cautions and interactions

    • Hundreds of interactions: antibiotics, antifungals, amiodarone, NSAIDs, alcohol binges raise the INR; rifampicin, carbamazepine, large amounts of green vegetables lower it. The NHS names NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, aspirin, antibiotics and antidepressants among common interacting medicines, and US information adds herbal products; every new medicine or supplement is checked first
    • Keep vitamin K intake steady rather than avoiding it (leafy greens, chickpeas, liver); cranberry juice and grapefruit juice are avoided because they increase the effect; alcohol is kept within modest daily limits and binge drinking avoided
    • Carry an anticoagulant alert card; tell dentists and pharmacists. The card is shown before any operation, dental work (especially extractions), tattoo or piercing, because the clinic may need to lower or pause the dose beforehand; vaccinations are usually fine but staff are told
    • Target INR 2-3 for most indications, 2.5-3.5 for some mechanical valves
    • Unsuitable or used with great care in people with liver or kidney problems, uncontrolled high blood pressure, a recent stroke, recent or imminent surgery, pregnancy, or conditions that make bleeding more likely such as a stomach ulcer
    • Missed or extra doses are reported to the anticoagulant clinic, GP or NHS 111 rather than corrected by the person
    • Switching between warfarin and a DOAC is managed by the clinic, with an INR test used to time the change

    What is warfarin used for, and how is it monitored?

    Warfarin is an anticoagulant, often called a blood thinner, used to prevent and treat blood clots. It does not literally thin the blood; it makes clots form less readily. Because it lowers the chance of clots, the main risk that comes with it is bleeding.

    People taking warfarin are given an anticoagulant alert card. Regular blood tests measure how quickly the blood clots, and the result guides the warfarin dose. This test is called the international normalised ratio, or INR. Testing is usually more frequent while the right dose is being found.

    What are the side effects of warfarin?

    The main side effect of warfarin is an increased risk of bleeding. The NHS lists blood in the urine or poo, unexplained bruising or nosebleeds, and coughing up or vomiting blood among the signs of bleeding.

    The NHS says to get urgent advice from NHS 111 if there are signs of bleeding, after a head injury, or if another serious side effect is suspected. Severe bleeding that will not stop needs a 999 call or A&E. A sudden bad headache with confusion, slurred speech or difficulty moving the arms or legs can signal bleeding in the brain, which is an emergency needing a 999 call.

    A painful skin rash can be a sign of a rare condition called calciphylaxis. A severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is an emergency needing a 999 call. Both are listed on the NHS page.

    Can I eat and drink what I like on warfarin?

    Foods rich in vitamin K, such as leafy greens, affect how warfarin works. The NHS says they can still be eaten; keeping the amount steady matters more than avoiding them, and the warfarin clinic can adjust the dose to match. Cranberry juice and grapefruit juice are best avoided because they can increase the blood-thinning effect.

    The NHS advises never binge drinking on warfarin, and keeping regular drinking modest.

    Can I take other medicines, supplements or herbal remedies with warfarin?

    The NHS says some medicines, remedies and supplements may not mix well with warfarin, so a doctor should know about everything else a person takes. Painkillers such as ibuprofen, antibiotics, antidepressants and statins are among the medicines the NHS lists as possible problems. The NHS advises against St John's wort and vitamin K supplements while on warfarin. Illness with sickness or diarrhoea can change how warfarin works, so the anticoagulant clinic is told about illness as well as new medicines.

    Who needs extra care with warfarin, and what about pregnancy?

    The NHS says most people can take warfarin, but it is not suitable for everyone. It lists an allergy to warfarin, liver or kidney problems, a recent stroke, recent or planned surgery, and a condition that makes bleeding more likely.

    Warfarin is not usually used in pregnancy because it can affect the baby's development, though it is sometimes used after the doctor discusses risks and benefits. The NHS advises anyone who is pregnant or planning a pregnancy to speak to their GP or anticoagulant clinic, and not to stop warfarin unless a doctor says so. The NHS says warfarin can usually be taken while breastfeeding, after discussing it with a GP or midwife first.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does warfarin really thin the blood?

    Not literally; it is called a blood thinner, but it makes clots form less readily. The NHS describes it as a medicine used to prevent and treat blood clots, and says its main side effect is an increased risk of bleeding.

    What is an anticoagulant alert card?

    It is a card given to people taking warfarin, who may be at risk of bleeding.

    What should be done after a head injury on warfarin?

    The NHS says to get urgent advice from NHS 111 after a head injury while on warfarin, as well as for any signs of bleeding. A sudden bad headache with confusion, slurred speech or difficulty moving the arms or legs can signal bleeding in the brain and needs a 999 call.

    Do I have to avoid green vegetables on warfarin?

    No. Leafy greens are rich in vitamin K and affect warfarin, but the NHS says they can still be eaten and that a steady intake matters, so the clinic can adjust the dose. Cranberry juice and grapefruit juice are the drinks to avoid.

    Can warfarin be taken in pregnancy?

    The NHS says warfarin is not usually used in pregnancy because it can affect the baby's development, although it is sometimes used after the doctor talks through risks and benefits. Anyone pregnant or planning a pregnancy is advised to speak to their GP or anticoagulant clinic and not to stop it unless a doctor says so.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medical tests

    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.

    Procedures

    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
