    Insulin

    Also known as: insulin injection

    The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when other treatments are not enough. Preparations differ mainly in how quickly they start and how long they last, and are combined to copy the body's own pattern of a steady background supply plus bursts at meals; the price of that control is hypoglycaemia, the one side effect every insulin user learns to recognise and treat.

    Usual preparations
    Prefilled pens injected under the skin of the abdomen, thighs or buttocks, rotating sites; insulin pumps delivering rapid-acting insulin continuously; intravenous infusion in hospital; hybrid closed-loop systems with continuous glucose monitors (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Rapid-acting 10-20 minutes (peak 1-3 hours, lasts 3-5 hours); long-acting flat over 24 hours or more. A long-acting dose taken later than usual keeps working later than usual, which raises the risk of a hypo and is why timing is kept consistent.
    Terminology
    ATC A10A
    Monitoring
    Fingerprick or continuous glucose monitoring several times daily; HbA1c every 3-6 months; annual eye, foot and kidney checks. Monitoring is the basis of dose adjustment as advised by the diabetes team, and the NHS emphasises regular checking as the main way of preventing hypos; injection sites are inspected for lipohypertrophy at review.

    What it is used for

    • Type 1 diabetes: essential for life from diagnosis, usually as a long-acting insulin once or twice a day plus a rapid-acting insulin before meals (a basal-bolus regimen), or rapid-acting insulin alone through a pump
    • Type 2 diabetes when tablets no longer control glucose, in pregnancy, and during severe illness or surgery; regimens include intermediate-acting insulin once or twice daily, a long-acting insulin, or a mixed (biphasic) insulin before meals
    • Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar states (intravenous infusion)
    • Lowering dangerously high potassium (with glucose) in emergencies
    • Longer term, good glucose control with insulin lowers the risk of heart attack, stroke and the kidney, nerve, eye and gum complications of diabetes

    How it works in the body

    Replaces the hormone the pancreatic beta cells no longer make. Insulin binds receptors on muscle, fat and liver cells, moving GLUT4 transporters to the surface so glucose enters cells; it switches the liver from making glucose to storing glycogen, stops fat breakdown and ketone production, and promotes protein synthesis. Modern analogue insulins are engineered to act fast at meals (aspart, lispro) or flat and long as background (glargine, degludec), mimicking the natural pattern of a basal trickle plus mealtime bursts. The NHS groups preparations into five families by their time course: rapid-acting analogues (aspart, lispro, glulisine), short-acting soluble human insulin, intermediate-acting isophane (NPH) insulin, long-acting analogues (detemir, glargine, degludec) and biphasic mixtures that blend an intermediate insulin with a rapid- or short-acting one. Among the long-acting insulins, detemir is usually given twice a day whereas glargine and degludec are usually given once, and degludec is often chosen for people troubled by night-time hypoglycaemia; analogues may act more consistently than older human insulins for some people. Insulin is a protein, so it is destroyed by digestion and must be injected under the skin or infused into a vein; an inhaled insulin exists in some countries but can reduce lung function and is avoided in asthma and COPD. The prescriber chooses the types, dose and timing, and most people with type 1 diabetes use more than one type.

    Common side effects

    • Hypoglycaemia (sweating, trembling or shaking, a fast heartbeat, tingling lips, dizziness, hunger, unusually pale skin, and with longer-acting insulins anxiety, confusion, difficulty concentrating or blurred vision): treat with 15-20 g fast sugar such as fruit juice, a sugary drink, glucose tablets, jelly babies or glucose gel, recheck, then a starchy snack because the sugar wears off quickly
    • Hypos are provoked by too much insulin, a missed or delayed meal, too little carbohydrate, and alcohol, especially on an empty stomach
    • Weight gain
    • Lipohypertrophy (fatty lumps) at over-used injection sites, which absorb insulin erratically
    • Bruising, bleeding, stinging, itching or redness at the injection site

    Serious: seek help

    • Severe hypoglycaemia with seizure or coma, needing glucagon injection or intravenous glucose; the NHS advises calling 999 if the person is not responding normally or is unconscious and glucagon is not available, if they have not recovered soon after glucagon, or if alcohol is involved
    • Diabetic ketoacidosis if insulin is omitted in type 1 diabetes (never stop, even when not eating: adjust instead)
    • Hypokalaemia with intravenous infusion
    • Allergy, including anaphylaxis (very rare with modern insulins): skin, lips or tongue turning blue, grey or pale, sudden confusion or fainting

    Cautions and interactions

    • Doses are individual and adjusted for food, activity, illness and glucose readings; errors in units are a major cause of harm ('units' written in full). Brands are not interchangeable, because some are more concentrated than others
    • Sick-day rules: keep taking basal insulin, check glucose and ketones more often, drink fluids
    • Driving rules: check glucose before and every 2 hours; carry fast sugar
    • Store unopened insulin in the fridge; in use at room temperature for 4 weeks
    • Rotate injection sites to avoid lipohypertrophy, which makes absorption erratic; frequent hypos, particularly at night, prompt a review of the regimen rather than simply eating more
    • Some products are withdrawn or discontinued from time to time (the NHS flags Levemir), so people are switched under supervision rather than left without a basal insulin

    Conditions

    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Gestational diabetes Gestational diabetes is a form of diabetes that starts during pregnancy and typically clears up after the baby is born.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Numbness Reduced or absent feeling in part of the body.
    • Tingling (pins and needles) Prickling, buzzing or crawling sensations without an outside cause, produced when sensory nerves fire abnormally.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.

    Biomarkers

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Fluorodeoxyglucose F-18 (FDG) The radioactive sugar used in most PET scans: cells that use a lot of glucose, such as many cancers, take it up and light up on the image.
