    Bisoprolol

    A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina, high blood pressure and to control the rate in atrial fibrillation.

    What it is used for

    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, where it improves survival
    • Angina
    • High blood pressure, usually when other classes are unsuitable or as an add-on
    • Rate control in atrial fibrillation and other fast rhythms

    How it works in the body

    Bisoprolol blocks beta-1 adrenoceptors on heart muscle and the conducting system, so adrenaline and noradrenaline can no longer speed the heart or make it contract as forcefully. The heart beats more slowly, needs less oxygen and, in heart failure, is protected from the long-term damage of sustained sympathetic drive. It has little effect on the beta-2 receptors of the airways at usual doses, though it is still used with caution in asthma.

    Common side effects

    • Tiredness, cold hands and feet, a slow pulse
    • Headache, dizziness, feeling sick
    • Sleep disturbance

    Serious: seek help

    • Breathlessness or wheeze, particularly in asthma
    • Fainting or a very slow heartbeat
    • Worsening fluid retention early in heart failure treatment
    • Signs of an allergic reaction

    Cautions and interactions

    • Avoided in asthma unless a specialist judges the benefit greater; used cautiously in COPD
    • Not started during decompensated heart failure; introduced once the person is stable
    • Combined with verapamil or diltiazem it can slow the heart dangerously
    • Masks the warning signs of low blood sugar in diabetes
    • Never stopped abruptly: the dose is tapered

    What is bisoprolol used for?

    Bisoprolol is a beta blocker, a type of medicine used to treat high blood pressure and heart failure. It is also used to prevent the chest pain caused by angina, and to treat atrial fibrillation and other conditions that cause an irregular heartbeat. It is only available on prescription.

    The NHS says bisoprolol works as well as other beta blockers at lowering blood pressure but is less likely to cause side effects, because it acts mainly on the heart. The British Heart Foundation (BHF) calls this kind of beta blocker selective, meaning it mostly blocks the effects of adrenaline and noradrenaline in the heart, with less effect on other parts of the body. Manufacturer leaflets say it is not recommended for children or adolescents.

    How does bisoprolol work and how is it taken?

    The BHF explains that beta blockers slow the heart rate and reduce how hard the heart has to work. They do this by blocking the effects of the stress hormones adrenaline and noradrenaline, which make the heart work harder and faster.

    The NHS says bisoprolol tablets are swallowed with a drink of water and can be taken with or without food. It says treatment is usually long term, and that the very first dose can make some people feel dizzy. Doses differ between people and conditions, so none are given here. If someone feels dizzy or weak, the NHS advises stopping what they are doing and sitting or lying down until they feel better.

    What are the side effects of bisoprolol?

    The NHS lists headaches, tiredness, cold or tingly hands and feet, dizziness from low blood pressure, feeling or being sick, and diarrhoea or constipation as common side effects. They usually happen when treatment starts or when the amount is increased, and they often get better over time.

    Serious side effects are uncommon. The NHS lists a serious allergic reaction, liver inflammation, breathing problems, and an irregular heartbeat or worsening heart failure symptoms. For a serious allergic reaction, the NHS says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or if breathing is very fast or hard. It says to call a doctor or NHS 111 if the whites of the eyes or the skin turn yellow, which can be a sign of liver problems.

    Is it dangerous to stop bisoprolol suddenly?

    The NHS says people still get the benefits of bisoprolol when they feel well. It advises talking to a doctor if you want to stop, because stopping can make blood pressure rise, which raises the risk of heart attack and stroke.

    Manufacturer leaflets say that if bisoprolol is suddenly stopped, side effects are likely, and that treatment should not be stopped abruptly. Leaflets describe the amount being reduced gradually, and they say to speak to the prescriber before stopping.

    Taking bisoprolol with other medicines or alcohol

    The NHS says some medicines can interact with bisoprolol. Other medicines that lower blood pressure can sometimes push it too low, which may cause dizziness or fainting. Painkillers such as ibuprofen can make bisoprolol less effective. The NHS page also flags some decongestants and cold remedies, and medicines for asthma, COPD and diabetes. It says its list is not complete.

    For herbal remedies and supplements the NHS says there is very little information, and it advises telling a doctor or pharmacist about any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.

    On alcohol, the NHS says it is best to avoid drinking when first starting bisoprolol or when the amount is increased, until you see how the medicine affects you. It also says alcohol will make dizziness or weakness feel worse.

    Who needs extra care with bisoprolol?

    The NHS says most adults aged 18 and over can take bisoprolol, but it is not suitable for everyone. It lists a previous allergic reaction to bisoprolol or another medicine, low blood pressure, a slow heart rate, worsening heart failure, heart disease, a recent heart attack, severe circulation problems, metabolic acidosis, and lung disease or severe asthma among the reasons to check with a doctor first. Manufacturer leaflets also list severe asthma and an untreated tumour of the adrenal gland called phaeochromocytoma as reasons not to take it. Bisoprolol may make the warning signs of low blood sugar harder to recognise.

    The NHS says bisoprolol can be taken during pregnancy, but the mother and baby may be offered extra checks. Its pregnancy page says it can sometimes affect a baby's growth, so extra ultrasound scans may be needed. Taking it while breastfeeding may cause side effects in the baby, although a doctor may still recommend it if the benefits outweigh the risks. One UK product leaflet advises against use in pregnancy, which differs from the NHS advice.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does bisoprolol make you tired?

    The NHS lists tiredness as a common side effect of bisoprolol. Common side effects usually happen when treatment starts or the amount is increased, and they often get better over time. If someone feels dizzy or weak, the NHS advice is to stop what they are doing and sit or lie down until they feel better.

    Can I stop taking bisoprolol if I feel fine?

    The NHS says people still get the benefits of bisoprolol when they feel well, and that stopping can make blood pressure rise, raising the risk of heart attack and stroke. Leaflets say stopping suddenly is likely to cause side effects and that the amount is reduced gradually. The NHS advises talking to a doctor before stopping.

    Does bisoprolol cause breathing problems?

    The NHS lists breathing problems among the serious side effects of bisoprolol, which are uncommon. It says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or if breathing is very fast or hard. The NHS also lists lung disease or severe asthma among the conditions to check with a doctor before taking it.

    Can I drink alcohol while taking bisoprolol?

    The NHS says it is best to avoid alcohol when first starting bisoprolol or when the amount is increased, until you see how it affects you. It also says alcohol will make dizziness or weakness feel worse.

    Is bisoprolol the same as other beta blockers?

    Bisoprolol is one of several beta blockers. The NHS says it works as well as others for lowering blood pressure but is less likely to cause side effects because it acts mainly on the heart. The BHF says selective beta blockers mostly block adrenaline and noradrenaline in the heart, while non-selective ones also block them in other parts of the body.

    Conditions

    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Angina Angina is chest pain or discomfort that happens when the heart muscle does not get enough blood.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Electrocardiogram Ten sticky electrodes on the chest and limbs record the heart's electrical activity from twelve angles over ten seconds.
    • Echocardiogram An ultrasound of the beating heart.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…

    Medications

    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.

    Drug classes

    • Beta blockers Medicines ending in -olol that shield the heart from adrenaline, slowing it and reducing its oxygen demand.
