    Metronidazole

    An antibiotic used for bacterial infections of the skin, mouth and gums, and the vagina and pelvis, and the usual treatment for trichomoniasis. It comes as tablets, liquid, suppositories, gels and creams, and the NHS advises avoiding alcohol during treatment and for 2 days afterwards.

    What it is used for

    • Bacterial skin infections and rosacea
    • Mouth infections, including infected gums and dental abscesses
    • Bacterial vaginosis and pelvic inflammatory disease
    • Infected insect bites, skin ulcers, bed sores and wounds
    • Trichomoniasis, a sexually transmitted infection, which the NHS says is usually treated with metronidazole

    How it works in the body

    Metronidazole is an antibiotic that treats bacterial and parasitic infections. Laboratory studies describe it as being switched on inside susceptible organisms, many of which live without oxygen, by pathways that human cells do not have. Once switched on, it forms short-lived chemical products that bind to DNA and damage it.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling or being sick, diarrhoea and a slight metallic taste in the mouth are the common side effects of the tablets, liquid, suppositories and vaginal gel
    • Some people feel drowsy, so the NHS suggests checking reaction times before driving
    • Metronidazole gel or cream can cause dryness, itching or irritation of the skin

    Serious: seek help

    • Serious side effects are rare, affecting fewer than 1 in 1,000 people; yellow skin or eyes, which the NHS links with liver or gallbladder problems, needs prompt contact with the GP or NHS 111
    • The NHS lists meningitis and brain disease as very rare serious side effects, and sudden serious symptoms of this kind are an emergency: call 999 or go to A&E
    • Swelling of the lips, mouth or throat, or trouble breathing, can signal a serious allergic reaction and needs a 999 call
    • Numbness or tingling in the hands and feet can occur with longer courses, which is a reason to speak to the prescriber or GP

    Cautions and interactions

    • The NHS says to discuss metronidazole first if there has ever been an allergic reaction to it or another medicine, or if blood tests are planned, because it can interfere with some of them
    • The NHS advises not drinking alcohol while taking metronidazole tablets or liquid or using suppositories, and avoiding it for 2 days after finishing, as the combination can cause feeling or being sick, stomach pain, hot flushes, palpitations and headache; the NHS trichomoniasis page gives at least 24 hours and MedlinePlus at least 3 days, so advice differs between pages
    • The NHS also lists feeling unable to stop drinking alcohol during treatment as a reason to talk to the prescriber first
    • Liver problems, dialysis and diseases of the nervous system are further points to tell the prescriber about for tablets, liquid or suppositories
    • In pregnancy the NHS says metronidazole may be possible but a doctor or pharmacist should be asked first, while MedlinePlus, a US source, says it generally should not be used in the first trimester
    • The NHS says there is no evidence of an effect on fertility in men or women
    • The Specialist Pharmacy Service says usual courses can be used during breastfeeding; the NHS asks for the baby to be watched, with contact with a healthcare professional or NHS 111 if the baby feeds poorly, has an upset stomach or has oral thrush, and MedlinePlus says a doctor may advise expressing and discarding milk
    • The NHS says that if sickness or diarrhoea lasts more than 24 hours, the contraceptive pill may not protect against pregnancy

    What is metronidazole used for?

    Metronidazole is an antibiotic that the NHS describes as treating several bacterial infections. These include infections of the skin, rosacea, infections of the mouth such as infected gums and dental abscesses, bacterial vaginosis and pelvic inflammatory disease. It can also be used for infected insect bites, skin ulcers, bed sores and wounds. Trichomoniasis, a sexually transmitted infection, is treated with antibiotics, usually metronidazole. It comes as tablets, a liquid to drink, suppositories, skin gel, skin cream and vaginal gel. It can also be given by injection, but this is usually done only in hospital. For most infections, the NHS says people feel better within a few days.

    How does metronidazole work?

    Metronidazole is an antibiotic that treats bacterial and parasitic infections. It belongs to a group of medicines called nitroimidazole antimicrobials. Laboratory studies describe it as being switched on inside susceptible organisms, many of which live without oxygen. The switching-on pathways are absent in the aerobic cells of the human body, which helps explain why the medicine targets microbes. Once switched on, it forms short-lived products that bind to DNA and cause damage, including breaks in the DNA strands.

    What are the side effects?

    The NHS lists feeling or being sick, diarrhoea and a slight metallic taste in the mouth as common side effects of the tablets, liquid, suppositories and vaginal gel. Some people feel drowsy, so the NHS suggests checking reaction times before driving. Metronidazole gel or cream can cause dryness, itching or irritation of the skin. Serious side effects are rare and, according to the NHS, affect fewer than 1 in 1,000 people. Yellow skin or eyes, which the NHS links with liver or gallbladder problems, needs prompt contact with the GP or NHS 111. The NHS lists meningitis and brain disease as very rare serious side effects, and sudden serious symptoms of this kind are an emergency: call 999 or go to A&E. Swelling of the lips, mouth or throat, or trouble breathing, can signal a serious allergic reaction and also needs a 999 call. A hospital factsheet adds that numbness or tingling in the hands and feet can occur with longer courses, a reason to speak to the prescriber.

    Metronidazole and alcohol

    The NHS advises not drinking alcohol while taking metronidazole tablets or liquid or using suppositories. It says to keep avoiding alcohol for 2 days after treatment finishes, while the NHS trichomoniasis page gives at least 24 hours. The reason is that alcohol with metronidazole can cause feeling or being sick, stomach pain, hot flushes, palpitations and headache. The NHS also lists feeling unable to stop drinking alcohol during treatment among the reasons to talk to a doctor before using it. With the vaginal gel, the NHS says drinking can sometimes cause the same effects. MedlinePlus, a US source, gives a longer wait of at least 3 days after the last dose, so advice differs between publishers.

    Who needs extra care?

    The NHS page on who can take metronidazole mentions people who have ever had an allergic reaction to it or another medicine, people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, and people who are having blood tests. Liver problems, dialysis and diseases of the nervous system are further points to tell the prescriber about for tablets, liquid or suppositories. On pregnancy, the NHS says metronidazole may be an option but advises checking with a doctor or pharmacist first. It also says there is no evidence of an effect on fertility in men or women. The Specialist Pharmacy Service says usual courses can be used during breastfeeding, with the baby watched for poor feeding, stomach upset or oral thrush. The NHS says that if sickness or diarrhoea lasts more than 24 hours, the contraceptive pill may not protect against pregnancy.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can alcohol be drunk during metronidazole treatment?

    The NHS advises avoiding alcohol during treatment and for 2 days after finishing, although its trichomoniasis page gives at least 24 hours. Alcohol with metronidazole can cause feeling or being sick, stomach pain, hot flushes, palpitations and headache.

    Does metronidazole affect the contraceptive pill?

    The NHS says that if sickness or diarrhoea lasts more than 24 hours, the contraceptive pill may not protect against pregnancy. Anyone unsure is advised to ask a doctor or pharmacist.

    Can metronidazole be used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

    The NHS says it may be possible to use it in pregnancy but advises discussing it with a doctor first. The Specialist Pharmacy Service says usual courses can be used while breastfeeding, with the baby watched for feeding problems.

    Does metronidazole affect driving?

    The NHS says metronidazole can cause drowsiness in some people, so reaction times are worth checking before driving. Anyone who feels drowsy or dizzy may not be fit to drive.

    How quickly does metronidazole work?

    The NHS says people usually feel better within a few days for most infections. The full course is still finished as prescribed even when symptoms ease.
