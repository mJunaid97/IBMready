    Apixaban and DOACs

    A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism without routine blood monitoring. It starts to work within hours, is taken twice a day with or without food, and its main harm is bleeding, so people carry an anticoagulant alert card and tell every dentist and surgeon they see.

    What it is used for

    • Preventing stroke in atrial fibrillation (first choice over warfarin for most)
    • Treating and preventing deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism
    • Preventing clots after hip and knee replacement
    • Not used for mechanical heart valves or antiphospholipid syndrome (warfarin remains)

    How it works in the body

    Binds directly to activated factor X, the enzyme at the junction of the clotting cascade that converts prothrombin to thrombin. Less thrombin means less fibrin, so clots that form in the slow-flowing left atrial appendage or leg veins are prevented or stopped from growing. Unlike warfarin the effect is predictable, starts within hours, needs no blood-level monitoring and is little affected by diet. The NHS describes the same action in plain terms: apixaban stops a clotting factor called factor Xa from working, which makes the blood take longer to clot. That is also why its harms are the mirror image of its benefit: cuts bleed for longer, bruises appear more easily and fade more slowly, and bleeding inside the body, including into the brain, is the serious risk. Because the effect fades within a day or so of the last dose, protection depends on taking it consistently; the US product information carries a boxed warning that people with atrial fibrillation who stop apixaban face a higher risk of stroke, so a doctor who needs to stop it usually arranges another anticoagulant to cover the gap.

    Common side effects

    • Bleeding more easily: bleeding for longer than usual from cuts, nosebleeds, bleeding gums when brushing, bruises that come up more easily and take longer to fade, heavier and longer periods
    • Anaemia, which can cause tiredness and paler skin
    • Nausea, rash

    Serious: seek help

    • Major bleeding: gastrointestinal (blood in the stool, red or black tarry stools, blood in vomit), urinary (pink, brown or red urine), coughing up blood, intracranial (about half the rate of warfarin; a sudden severe headache with confusion, sensitivity to light or slurred speech), retroperitoneal
    • Bleeding after trauma or surgery: a fall or injury on apixaban, especially a blow to the head, needs urgent assessment even without visible bleeding; reversal agents (andexanet, idarucizumab for dabigatran) exist but are limited
    • Spinal haematoma with epidural or spinal anaesthesia or spinal injection, which can cause paralysis: weakness or numbness of the legs or feet, or loss of bladder or bowel control afterwards, is an emergency
    • Ongoing bleeding that does not stop on its own

    Cautions and interactions

    • Dose reduced with kidney impairment, low weight, age over 80 (apixaban), and some interacting drugs
    • Avoid NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) and aspirin unless specifically advised, and check before starting herbal products, which can also interact; alcohol in moderation, because heavy or binge drinking increases the effect of apixaban and the risk of bleeding
    • Before surgery or procedures the clinical team decides whether to pause apixaban and for how long, depending on bleeding risk and kidney function; it is not stopped unless the clinician says so; the anticoagulant alert card is shown to doctors and dentists before any surgery or dental treatment
    • Missed doses matter because the effect wears off quickly; do not double up. Treatment is not stopped without medical advice because the clot or stroke risk returns as the drug wears off

    What is apixaban used for?

    Apixaban is an anticoagulant, a medicine that helps prevent blood clots from forming. It is available in the UK only on prescription.

    The NHS says it is used to treat blood clots in the veins, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and in the lungs, called pulmonary embolism. It is also given to people at high risk of clots, such as those with atrial fibrillation (an irregular heartbeat), heart failure, or a past stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA). It is also given after a recent hip or knee replacement.

    How does apixaban work and how is it taken?

    Apixaban makes the blood take longer to clot, which lowers the chance of a harmful clot but also means bleeding can last longer. The tablets can be taken with or without food.

    The NHS says a person should not stop apixaban without talking to their doctor. If it is stopped, the way the blood clots goes back to what it was before treatment, usually within a day or two. The NHS gives missed-dose advice on its anticoagulant dosage page; it depends on how long it is until the next scheduled dose.

    What are the side effects of apixaban?

    The most common side effect is bleeding more easily than normal. This can show as nosebleeds, bleeding gums, bruising or heavier periods. The NHS describes this kind of bleeding as not dangerous, and says it should stop by itself. The doctor should be told if it bothers the person or does not stop.

    The NHS lists blood in urine or stools, and coughing up or vomiting blood, among the more serious effects. On its main apixaban page, the NHS says to call NHS 111 for symptoms of bleeding, a head injury, or other possible serious side effects.

    Very rarely, apixaban can cause bleeding in the brain. On its side-effects page the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E for a sudden severe headache unlike any before, fits, sudden loss of vision or blurred vision, or numbness or tingling in the arms or legs. The two NHS pages are worded differently: the main page names NHS 111 for bleeding and head injury, and the side-effects page names 999 or A&E for these brain-bleed signs.

    A serious allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face or throat or difficulty breathing, is a reason to call 999.

    Can I take other medicines or drink alcohol with apixaban?

    The NHS says some medicines may not mix well with apixaban, including other anticoagulants, some antidepressants, and painkillers or anti-inflammatories such as aspirin and ibuprofen. The NHS says not to take aspirin or ibuprofen unless a doctor has said it is fine, as they raise the chance of bleeding.

    The NHS asks people to tell their doctor, dentist or pharmacist about every medicine they take, including over-the-counter medicines, vitamins and herbal supplements. It names St John's wort, a herbal remedy for low mood, as one to avoid. It also says there is not enough information to say that other herbal remedies and supplements are safe to take with apixaban.

    The NHS says it is possible to drink alcohol while taking apixaban, but heavy or binge drinking raises the risk of bleeding.

    Apixaban, surgery and the alert card

    The NHS says people taking apixaban should carry an anticoagulant alert card and tell their surgeon or dentist that they take it. Because of the risk of bleeding, the dose may need to be lowered or stopped a few days before an operation, and the clinical team makes that decision.

    Who may not be able to take apixaban?

    The NHS lists reasons apixaban may not be suitable, including a past allergic reaction to apixaban or any other medicine, active heavy bleeding, a stomach ulcer, a recent bleed in the brain, and liver or kidney problems.

    The NHS says apixaban can be harmful to a baby, so pregnancy or trying to become pregnant is on its list of reasons. It also says apixaban should not be used while breastfeeding.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is apixaban an anticoagulant?

    Yes. The NHS describes apixaban as an anticoagulant that helps prevent blood clots. It is only available on prescription in the UK.

    What does the NHS say about a missed apixaban tablet?

    The NHS gives missed-dose advice on its anticoagulant dosage page. It depends on how long it is until the next scheduled dose, and this site does not give the timings.

    Can apixaban be stopped if someone feels fine?

    The NHS says not to stop apixaban without talking to the doctor first. If it is stopped, the way the blood clots returns to what it was before treatment, usually within a day or two.

    What does the NHS say about a head injury or bleeding on apixaban?

    On its main apixaban page, the NHS says to call NHS 111 for symptoms of bleeding, a head injury or other possible serious side effects. On its side-effects page, it says to call 999 or go to A&E for a sudden severe headache, fits, or sudden vision changes.

    Can alcohol be drunk while taking apixaban?

    The NHS says it is possible to drink alcohol while taking apixaban. Heavy or binge drinking raises the risk of bleeding.

    Conditions

    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Deep vein thrombosis A blood clot in a deep vein, most often of the calf or thigh.
    • Pulmonary embolism Blockage of a pulmonary artery by a clot that has travelled from a vein, usually in the leg or pelvis.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Prothrombin time and INR The prothrombin time measures how many seconds plasma takes to clot once the tissue-factor pathway is triggered.
