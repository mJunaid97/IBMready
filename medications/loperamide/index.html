    Loperamide

    Also known as: Imodium

    Loperamide is a medicine for diarrhoea that slows the activity of the bowel so that stools firm up. It can help with short-term diarrhoea and, in adults with a doctor's diagnosis, irritable bowel syndrome, and it is not suitable for everyone. The NHS says to contact 111 straight away if you take more than the recommended amount and get a fast or irregular heartbeat, because higher amounts can cause serious heart problems.

    What it is used for

    • Treating short-term diarrhoea.
    • Helping with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) in adults whose doctor has diagnosed it.
    • Treating longer-lasting diarrhoea from conditions such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, on prescription.

    How it works in the body

    The NHS says loperamide works by slowing the activity of the bowel. Product information for UK loperamide medicines says it lengthens the time contents spend in the gut and increases absorption of water and electrolytes, so stools become firmer. UK and US product information describes loperamide as an opioid (mu) receptor agonist that acts on the gut wall to reduce propulsive movement of the bowel.

    Common side effects

    • The NHS lists constipation, headache and feeling sick as side effects of loperamide; dizziness is also listed in NHS common questions.
    • The NHS says loperamide can cause dizziness, tiredness and drowsiness, and that drinking alcohol makes these more likely.
    • The NHS advises against driving or using machinery if affected.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS says swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, or very fast or struggling breathing, may be a serious allergic reaction, and to call 999.
    • MedlinePlus says taking more than the recommended amount of loperamide can cause serious heart problems that may be fatal.
    • The NHS says to contact 111 or a doctor straight away for fainting, stomach pain or tenderness with a high temperature, severe constipation or difficulty passing urine.
    • The US FDA says that at much higher than recommended amounts loperamide can cause severe heart rhythm problems and death.

    Cautions and interactions

    • Over the counter, the NHS says loperamide is for anyone aged 12 or over with short-term diarrhoea, and for adults with IBS only if a doctor has diagnosed it.
    • Children aged 11 or under and adults with long-lasting diarrhoea need a prescription, according to the NHS.
    • The NHS lists severe diarrhoea after antibiotics, a flare-up of ulcerative colitis or another inflammatory bowel condition, constipation or a swollen stomach, and blood in the stool with a high temperature among the situations where it is not suitable.
    • The NHS says loperamide is not usually recommended in pregnancy because there is not enough evidence about it, and that anyone pregnant should speak to a doctor first.
    • The NHS says breastfeeding is considered acceptable because only tiny amounts pass into breast milk and no side effects are expected in the baby.
    • The NHS says some medicines can affect how loperamide works, including clozapine, ritonavir, quinidine, itraconazole, gemfibrozil, desmopressin and other anti-diarrhoea or bowel medicines.
    • The NHS says loperamide can be taken alongside paracetamol or ibuprofen and does not stop contraceptive pills working, although severe diarrhoea can make the pill less reliable.

    What is loperamide used for?

    The NHS describes loperamide as a medicine to treat diarrhoea. It can help with short-term diarrhoea and with irritable bowel syndrome that a doctor has diagnosed in an adult. For longer-lasting diarrhoea, such as that from Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, it is available on prescription.

    How does loperamide work?

    Loperamide slows the activity of the bowel, so the contents move more slowly and stay in the intestine for longer. Product information for UK loperamide medicines says it lengthens the time contents spend in the gut and increases absorption of water and electrolytes, so stools become firmer. The NHS says it usually starts to improve diarrhoea within 1 hour.

    Side effects and when to get help

    The NHS lists constipation, headache and feeling sick as side effects of loperamide. Dizziness, tiredness and drowsiness are more likely with alcohol. A fast or irregular heartbeat after taking more than the recommended amount can be a sign of serious heart problems, and the NHS says to contact 111 straight away. Swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, or very fast or struggling breathing, may be a serious allergic reaction: call 999. Fainting, stomach pain with a high temperature, severe constipation or difficulty passing urine need medical advice straight away: contact NHS 111 or a doctor.

    Who should not use loperamide, and other medicines

    The NHS lists severe diarrhoea after antibiotics, a flare-up of ulcerative colitis or similar conditions, constipation or a swollen stomach, and blood in the stool with a high temperature as situations where it is not suitable. Pregnancy is a situation where the NHS says loperamide is not usually recommended and a doctor should be consulted first. Several medicines can change how loperamide works, including clozapine, ritonavir, quinidine, itraconazole, gemfibrozil and desmopressin.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can loperamide be bought without a prescription?

    Yes, for people aged 12 or over with short-term diarrhoea, according to the NHS.

    Can too much loperamide be harmful?

    MedlinePlus says taking more than the recommended amount can cause serious heart problems that may be fatal. The NHS says to contact 111 straight away if you have a fast or irregular heartbeat after taking more than the recommended amount.

    Is loperamide suitable in pregnancy and breastfeeding?

    The NHS says it is not usually recommended in pregnancy, and that breastfeeding is considered acceptable.
