    Methotrexate

    Also known as: Maxtrex, Metoject, Jylamvo, Methofill, Nordimet, Zlatal

    Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease, and sometimes for cancer. When it is used for autoimmune conditions such as these, it is taken once a week, not every day. Taking it daily by mistake instead of weekly has caused overdoses that were sometimes fatal. The NHS says to contact NHS 111 right away if more methotrexate than prescribed has been taken, including taking it daily instead of weekly.

    What it is used for

    • Rheumatoid arthritis
    • Psoriasis
    • Crohn's disease
    • Some cancers

    How it works in the body

    The NHS says it calms the immune system and helps reduce inflammation. Methotrexate blocks an enzyme called dihydrofolate reductase, which the body uses to make tetrahydrofolate, a form of folate needed to build DNA and RNA. In inflammatory conditions, how low-dose methotrexate works is less clear.

    Common side effects

    • The NHS says the skin may become very sensitive to sunlight. Hospital leaflets list nausea or stomach upset, loss of appetite, diarrhoea, headache and tiredness among the more common side effects.
    • Hair loss can happen and is not likely to be permanent, according to the NHS.
    • Versus Arthritis says folic acid tablets are usually prescribed to help reduce unpleasant effects of methotrexate.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS says serious side effects are rare.
    • The NHS says to contact a doctor or NHS 111 straight away about yellow skin or eyes, which can be a sign of liver problems, or a new cough, chest pain or breathlessness, which can be a sign of lung inflammation.
    • The same NHS list includes signs of infection, such as a high temperature or sore throat, and signs of a blood problem, such as bleeding gums, blood in the urine or unexplained bruising.
    • The NHS says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell or breathing becomes fast or difficult.
    • Versus Arthritis says methotrexate can affect the liver and lower the number of blood cells made, which is why regular blood tests are done.
    • The NHS says to contact NHS 111 right away if more methotrexate than prescribed has been taken, including taking it daily instead of weekly.

    Cautions and interactions

    • When it is used for autoimmune conditions such as these, it is taken once a week, not every day.
    • Taking it daily by mistake instead of weekly has caused overdoses that were sometimes fatal.
    • The MHRA says oral methotrexate products used once weekly now come with a patient card that prompts weekly dosing, records the chosen weekday, and is carried to show clinicians who do not know the person's treatment.
    • The NHS lists a past allergic reaction to methotrexate, severe liver or kidney disease, a blood disorder, an active infection, mouth or stomach ulcers, pregnancy and breastfeeding among reasons it may not be suitable.
    • The NHS says methotrexate may lower the number of white cells in the blood, which can make infections more likely.
    • The NHS says to ask a doctor for urgent advice after contact with someone who has chickenpox or shingles.
    • The NHS says methotrexate is not recommended in pregnancy because it can cause birth defects, and advises speaking to a doctor before trying to get pregnant.
    • The NHS pregnancy page says methotrexate may affect sperm in men and egg production in women.
    • NHS hospital guidance advises effective contraception during treatment and for some months afterwards, and the length of time differs between sources.
    • The NHS says tiny amounts of methotrexate pass into breast milk, and lists breastfeeding among situations where it may not be suitable.

    Methotrexate + Aspirin

    MajorSpecialist supervision or close monitoring

    Aspirin reduces the kidney's excretion of methotrexate, raising its levels and toxicity (bone-marrow suppression, mucositis, liver injury).

    Why it can occur
    renal clearance
    What official information says
    UK information lists methotrexate among medicines to tell a doctor about; the BNF advises avoiding aspirin with high-dose methotrexate and monitoring with low-dose.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official guidance: avoided with high-dose methotrexate; with weekly low-dose methotrexate the combination is used only with monitoring.
    What may be monitored

    What is methotrexate used for, and how does it work?

    Methotrexate is an immunosuppressant used for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis and Crohn's disease, and sometimes for cancer. It is available only on prescription. The NHS says it calms the immune system and helps reduce inflammation. It is not a painkiller, although there may be less pain as symptoms improve. The NHS says it can take a few months to notice any benefit, and that the medicine is likely to be working during that time.

    Methotrexate blocks an enzyme called dihydrofolate reductase, which the body uses to make tetrahydrofolate, a form of folate needed to build DNA and RNA. In inflammatory conditions, how low-dose methotrexate works is less clear. Proposed explanations include effects on adenosine release, inflammatory signals and the growth of lymphocytes.

    Why is weekly dosing so important?

    When it is used for autoimmune conditions such as these, it is taken once a week, not every day. Taking it daily by mistake instead of weekly has caused overdoses that were sometimes fatal. In 2020 the MHRA introduced new measures to reduce the risk of fatal overdose from accidental daily dosing. The MHRA says oral methotrexate products used once weekly now come with a patient card that prompts weekly dosing, records the chosen weekday, and is carried to show clinicians who do not know the person's treatment. Prescribers are asked to write the weekday in full on the prescription. The NHS says to contact NHS 111 right away if more methotrexate than prescribed has been taken, including taking it daily instead of weekly.

    What are the side effects?

    The NHS says the skin may become very sensitive to sunlight. Hospital leaflets list nausea or stomach upset, loss of appetite, diarrhoea, headache and tiredness among the more common side effects. Hair loss can happen and is not likely to be permanent, according to the NHS. Versus Arthritis says folic acid tablets are usually prescribed to help reduce unpleasant effects of methotrexate.

    The NHS says serious side effects are rare. The NHS says to contact a doctor or NHS 111 straight away about yellow skin or eyes, which can be a sign of liver problems, or a new cough, chest pain or breathlessness, which can be a sign of lung inflammation. The same NHS list includes signs of infection, such as a high temperature or sore throat, and signs of a blood problem, such as bleeding gums, blood in the urine or unexplained bruising. The NHS says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell or breathing becomes fast or difficult. Versus Arthritis says methotrexate can affect the liver and lower the number of blood cells made, which is why regular blood tests are done.

    Which tests are needed?

    Versus Arthritis says blood tests of the blood count, kidney function and liver function are needed before starting, are more frequent at first and become less frequent once treatment is steady. The NHS says a chest X-ray and breathing test may be done before starting. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service monitoring page lists hepatitis B among the checks before starting.

    The NHS says methotrexate may lower the number of white cells in the blood, which can make infections more likely. The NHS says to ask a doctor for urgent advice after contact with someone who has chickenpox or shingles.

    What about pregnancy and contraception?

    The NHS says methotrexate is not recommended in pregnancy because it can cause birth defects, and advises speaking to a doctor before trying to get pregnant. The NHS pregnancy page says methotrexate may affect sperm in men and egg production in women. NHS hospital guidance advises effective contraception during treatment and for some months afterwards, and the length of time differs between sources. The NHS says tiny amounts of methotrexate pass into breast milk, and lists breastfeeding among situations where it may not be suitable.

    Taking methotrexate with other medicines, alcohol and vaccines

    The NHS lists a past allergic reaction to methotrexate, severe liver or kidney disease, a blood disorder, an active infection, mouth or stomach ulcers, pregnancy and breastfeeding among reasons it may not be suitable. The NHS says it is usually fine to drink alcohol with a low weekly amount of methotrexate, and to ask a doctor if taking a higher amount. Live vaccines are not recommended during treatment. NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance says NSAIDs such as ibuprofen can increase the risk of methotrexate toxicity, so they are used only on specialist advice. Trimethoprim and co-trimoxazole are advised against because of the risk of severe bone marrow suppression.
