    Atorvastatin

    A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver, cutting the risk of heart attack and stroke in people at raised cardiovascular risk.

    How it works

    Blocks HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol. The liver responds by putting more LDL receptors on its surface to pull cholesterol out of the blood, so LDL falls by 40-55%. Statins also stabilise the fatty plaques in artery walls, making them less likely to rupture and cause a clot.

    Mechanism of action

    Atorvastatin is a competitive, reversible inhibitor of HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of hepatic cholesterol synthesis. Lower intracellular cholesterol activates SREBP-2, which increases LDL-receptor expression on hepatocytes, so LDL and VLDL remnants are cleared from plasma faster; LDL cholesterol falls by about 40-55% depending on dose (high-intensity at 40-80 mg), with modest reductions in triglycerides and a small rise in HDL. Beyond lipids, statins reduce inflammation in plaques and improve endothelial function, contributing to plaque stabilisation. Atorvastatin is metabolised by CYP3A4, so strong inhibitors (clarithromycin, itraconazole, ciclosporin, some HIV protease inhibitors, large amounts of grapefruit juice) raise exposure and myopathy risk. Its active metabolites give a long duration of effect, allowing dosing at any time of day.

    Pathway

    1. Atorvastatin

    Side effects

    Common

    • Muscle aches (real in a minority; most reported aches occur equally on placebo)
    • Headache, nausea, bowel changes
    • Small rise in blood glucose (slightly earlier diagnosis of diabetes in those already at risk)

    Serious: seek help

    • Myopathy and rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown, very rare), more with high doses, interacting drugs and hypothyroidism
    • Liver enzyme rise (rare, usually harmless), haemorrhagic stroke risk very slightly higher in those with a previous bleed

    Serious safety information

    • Special warning. Muscle effects: unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness, particularly with fever or dark urine, may indicate myopathy or rhabdomyolysis; UK information advises seeking urgent advice and a creatine kinase test. Risk rises with high doses, interacting drugs, older age, hypothyroidism and kidney impairment. UK
    • Special warning. Rare immune-mediated necrotising myopathy and interstitial lung disease have been reported with statins; persistent breathlessness or cough, or weakness that continues after stopping, needs assessment. UK

    Warnings and precautions

    • Liver. Liver enzymes can rise; liver function is measured before treatment, within 3 months and at 12 months, and treatment is reviewed if transaminases exceed three times the upper limit. UK
    • Pregnancy. Contraindicated in pregnancy: UK information advises stopping atorvastatin three months before trying to conceive and not taking it while pregnant or breastfeeding, because cholesterol synthesis is needed for fetal development. UK
    • Precaution. Blood glucose can rise slightly and diabetes can be diagnosed a little earlier in people already at risk; the cardiovascular benefit outweighs this and treatment is continued with glucose monitoring. UK

    Effects on tests and results

    • LDL cholesterol: LDL and total cholesterol fall by 40-55%, non-HDL cholesterol similarly; the intended effect, checked at 3 months. UK
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase): ALT and AST can rise, usually mildly and transiently; a rise above three times the upper limit prompts review. UK
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin): HbA1c and fasting glucose can rise slightly, particularly in people already at risk of diabetes. UK
    • Creatine kinase rises in statin myopathy; a level above five times the upper limit with symptoms is a reason to stop. UK

    Pharmacokinetics

    Absorption
    Rapidly absorbed; oral bioavailability about 12% because of extensive first-pass metabolism; food reduces the rate but not the extent of LDL lowering
    Peak
    1-2 hours
    Half-life
    About 14 hours for the parent drug, 20-30 hours for HMG-CoA reductase inhibitory activity because of active metabolites; can be taken at any time of day
    Metabolism
    CYP3A4 to active ortho- and para-hydroxylated metabolites; OATP1B1 transports it into hepatocytes
    Elimination
    Mainly in bile after metabolism; less than 2% in urine; not removed by dialysis

    What is atorvastatin used for?

    Atorvastatin is a cholesterol-lowering medicine called a statin. MedlinePlus says it belongs to a group of medicines known as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. The NHS says it is used to treat high cholesterol. High cholesterol can cause problems with the heart and blood vessels, known as cardiovascular disease. Atorvastatin is also used to help prevent heart attacks and strokes.

    The NHS says a doctor decides how much to prescribe, based on why it is needed, the cholesterol level and the other medicines a person takes. The NHS also says a person still gets the benefits of the medicine when they feel well. If atorvastatin is stopped, cholesterol levels may go up again. The NHS therefore says to talk to a doctor first if stopping is being considered because of a suspected side effect.

    How does atorvastatin work and how quickly?

    Atorvastatin works by stopping the liver from making cholesterol, which lowers the amount of cholesterol in the blood. LDL is one of the types of cholesterol carried in the blood. The NHS says cholesterol levels should fall within 4 weeks if the medicine is taken regularly as prescribed.

    The NHS says routine liver blood tests are offered during the first 12 months of treatment. These are done to check that the liver is working properly. The NHS says atorvastatin can be taken with or without food. The NHS says that if it is stopped, cholesterol levels may go up again. It also says a person who suspects a side effect should talk to a doctor first, because the doctor can check whether atorvastatin is the cause or whether the problem is unrelated.

    What are the side effects of atorvastatin?

    The NHS lists headache, dizziness, feeling sick, muscle aches with swollen joints, and sleep problems among the common side effects. The NHS says atorvastatin can sometimes cause serious side effects, although most of them are rare. These include inflammation of the liver or pancreas, and inflammation and damage to the muscles.

    Muscle problems can cause cramps, severe pain and weakness, and sometimes a high temperature and red or brown pee. Inflammation of the liver can cause yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes, which may be harder to see on brown or black skin. Inflammation of the pancreas can cause severe stomach pain.

    A serious allergic reaction is an emergency. The NHS says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or if breathing or swallowing becomes difficult, and not to drive to A&E. For other suspected side effects, the NHS says to talk to a doctor first, who can check whether atorvastatin is the cause.

    Taking atorvastatin with other medicines, grapefruit or alcohol

    NHS 111 Wales says statins can react with some other substances, which can raise the risk of serious side effects such as muscle damage. A patient leaflet for atorvastatin lists the antibiotic fusidic acid as a medicine that can cause serious muscle breakdown when taken with atorvastatin.

    The NHS says too much grapefruit juice can raise the level of atorvastatin in the blood and increase the risk of side effects. The British Heart Foundation says people taking atorvastatin can have grapefruit occasionally, but not in large amounts. It says the advice differs between statins, and that people taking simvastatin are advised to avoid grapefruit altogether.

    The NHS also lists regularly drinking a lot of alcohol among the reasons a person may not be suitable for atorvastatin.

    Who needs extra care with atorvastatin?

    The NHS says most adults can take atorvastatin. It may not suit people who have had an allergic reaction to atorvastatin or another statin, or who have liver or kidney problems. An underactive thyroid, past muscle side effects from a statin and muscle conditions such as myasthenia gravis are also on the list.

    The NHS advises against atorvastatin in pregnancy and advises stopping it some months before trying to conceive. On breastfeeding, the NHS says a person may be able to take it but should check with a pharmacist or doctor first. NHS 111 Wales is stricter and says statins should not be taken by anyone who is pregnant or breastfeeding. The British Heart Foundation also says statins should not be taken during pregnancy.

    Medical tests

    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.

    Biomarkers

    • LDL cholesterol The cholesterol carried by low-density lipoprotein, the particle that deposits cholesterol in artery walls and drives atherosclerosis.
    • Total cholesterol The sum of all the cholesterol carried in blood by lipoproteins: LDL, HDL and the remainder.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.
    • AST (aspartate aminotransferase) An enzyme found in liver cells but also in heart, skeletal muscle, kidney, brain and red cells.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Simvastatin Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes.
