    Sertraline and SSRIs

    A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder; it takes several weeks to work fully. The first weeks are often the hardest, with nausea, headache, poor sleep and sometimes worse anxiety before mood improves, so early review matters; once established it is usually continued for months after recovery and then reduced gradually rather than stopped suddenly.

    Monitoring
    Mood and suicidality at review; sodium in the elderly; ECG for citalopram at high doses. Early reviews check for worsening anxiety or agitation, new or increased thoughts of self-harm, sleep and appetite, and whether side effects are settling; later reviews judge response and decide how long to continue after recovery and when to begin a gradual reduction. No routine blood tests are needed for sertraline itself.

    What it is used for

    • Moderate to severe depression, alongside psychological therapy
    • Anxiety disorders: generalised anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, PTSD, OCD; US labelling also covers premenstrual dysphoric disorder
    • Sertraline is a first choice after a heart attack because of its cardiac safety
    • One of the better-studied antidepressants in pregnancy, so it is often the SSRI chosen when treatment is needed during pregnancy

    How it works in the body

    Blocks the serotonin transporter that pumps serotonin back into the nerve ending after release, so more remains in the synapse. The immediate rise in serotonin is not itself the cure: over weeks, receptors adapt, and growth factors such as BDNF and neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex increase, which is thought to underlie the delayed improvement in mood and anxiety. The NHS describes it for the reader as increasing the levels of a mood-enhancing chemical called serotonin in the brain, and US information as increasing the amount of serotonin, a natural substance that helps maintain mental balance. The gap between the chemical effect (hours) and the clinical effect (weeks) explains the characteristic early course: side effects such as nausea, headache and disturbed sleep appear first, some people, especially those treated for panic, find their anxiety worse for the first few weeks, and most people start to feel better within the first couple of weeks although the full benefit takes longer. Serotonin also acts outside the brain, on the gut (nausea and diarrhoea), on platelets (easier bleeding, especially with anti-inflammatory painkillers or anticoagulants) and on sexual function, which accounts for the main side effects. Because the brain adapts to the raised serotonin, stopping abruptly produces discontinuation symptoms, so the dose is reduced gradually under supervision, and combining it with other serotonin-raising medicines can tip the balance into serotonin syndrome.

    Common side effects

    • Nausea, diarrhoea, headache, sweating, insomnia or drowsiness in the first weeks; the NHS adds dry mouth and weight gain, notes that most of these ease after a couple of weeks, and suggests taking the dose with food for nausea, in the morning if it disturbs sleep and in the evening if it causes drowsiness
    • Sexual dysfunction (reduced libido, delayed orgasm), which often persists
    • Increased anxiety and agitation at the start, particularly in people treated for panic attacks; this usually wears off after a few weeks
    • Dizziness or drowsiness, during which driving, cycling and using machinery are avoided

    Serious: seek help

    • Increased suicidal thoughts in the first weeks, particularly under 25: close follow-up. New or worsening thoughts of suicide or self-harm, or marked agitation, need urgent contact with a GP or NHS 111, and 999 or A&E if in immediate danger (Samaritans: 116 123, free, any time)
    • Bleeding risk, especially with NSAIDs, aspirin or anticoagulants (serotonin is needed for platelet function)
    • Low sodium in the elderly; serotonin syndrome with tramadol, triptans, MAOIs, St John's wort: a rare but serious reaction with a fast heartbeat, sweating, shaking, twitching muscles and confusion or agitation, which the NHS describes as needing urgent help
    • Discontinuation symptoms (dizziness, electric-shock sensations, irritability) if stopped abruptly: taper over weeks
    • Mania: feeling unusually high, full of energy or unable to sleep
    • Serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), which is rare

    Cautions and interactions

    • Review within 1-2 weeks of starting, sooner in young people
    • Do not stop suddenly: the NHS describes the doctor recommending a gradual reduction to prevent withdrawal side effects
    • Citalopram and escitalopram prolong the QT interval: dose limits and ECG in heart disease
    • Pregnancy: discuss risks and benefits; sertraline is among the better studied. The NHS states it can be used in pregnancy when needed; some studies suggest a possible small effect on the baby's heart development, although most babies are unaffected; use near delivery can cause short-lived withdrawal symptoms and very rarely breathing problems in the newborn, who is checked after birth, and use in the final month slightly raises the risk of bleeding after delivery, which is treatable and not a reason to stop
    • Grapefruit juice is avoided because it can increase the amount of sertraline in the body and the risk of side effects
    • SSRIs interact unpredictably with some other medicines, raising the risk of bleeding or serotonin syndrome, so new medicines, including over-the-counter and herbal products, are checked with a pharmacist first
    • Taken once a day, morning or evening; the liquid concentrate is diluted before use as directed

    What is sertraline used for and how does it work?

    Sertraline is an antidepressant from a group called selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or SSRIs. The NHS says it is often used for depression and sometimes for panic attacks, obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). MedlinePlus also lists social anxiety disorder among its uses. The NHS says children aged 6 to 17 can also take sertraline, but only for OCD. It comes as tablets.

    The NHS says it is not known exactly how antidepressants work. One view is that they increase the levels of brain chemicals called neurotransmitters, mainly serotonin and noradrenaline. The NHS says people may start to feel better after 1 to 2 weeks, but sertraline usually takes 4 to 6 weeks to work fully.

    What are the side effects of sertraline?

    The NHS lists headaches, feeling sick, dizziness or drowsiness, dry mouth, diarrhoea, problems sleeping and sexual problems among the common side effects of sertraline. The NHS says some of the common side effects gradually improve as the body gets used to the medicine. It also lists agitation, shakiness and anxiety among the early side effects of antidepressants, which generally improve with time.

    Serious reactions are rarer. The NHS lists serotonin syndrome and mania among them, and describes serotonin syndrome as an uncommon but potentially serious set of side effects linked to SSRIs.

    MedlinePlus warns that sertraline may raise the risk of suicidal thoughts and actions in children, teenagers and young adults, and that the risk may be higher in the first few months of treatment and when the dose is increased. It lists new or worsening depression, thoughts of harming yourself, extreme worry, panic attacks and severe restlessness as warning signs, and advises calling a doctor right away if they appear. The NHS says that anyone who may be about to harm themselves should call 999 or go to A&E. For urgent mental health help, NHS 111 has a mental health option (option 2), and Samaritans is free to call on 116 123 at any time. A GP can also be asked for an emergency appointment.

    Who needs extra care with sertraline, and what should it not be mixed with?

    The NHS says most adults can take sertraline. People who have had an allergic reaction to sertraline or another medicine may not be able to take it. The NHS also lists heart problems, glaucoma and epilepsy as reasons for extra care, because sertraline can affect heart rhythm, raise the pressure in the eye or increase the risk of a seizure. Sertraline can make blood sugar harder to control, so a doctor may suggest checking it more often at first for people with diabetes.

    The NHS says it is best not to drink alcohol while taking sertraline, because alcohol can stop the medicine working properly. Grapefruit juice can increase the amount of sertraline in the body and the risk of side effects. The NHS says not to take the herbal remedy St John's wort during treatment, because this increases the risk of side effects, and that there is very little information about other herbal remedies and supplements. The NHS flags medicines that affect the heartbeat, and other antidepressants, as ones to raise with a doctor first. The UK patient leaflet says sertraline must not be used with a group of antidepressants called MAOIs, such as selegiline or moclobemide, or with linezolid.

    Can sertraline be taken in pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

    The NHS says sertraline can be taken in pregnancy. Some studies have suggested that it might occasionally affect the development of a baby's heart. The NHS says that if there is any risk it is small, and that most babies born to women taking sertraline have a normal heart.

    When it is taken in the weeks before delivery, sertraline can sometimes cause short-term withdrawal symptoms in the baby and, very rarely, breathing problems. The NHS says the baby will be checked after birth and given extra care if needed. MedlinePlus advises telling a doctor about a pregnancy, a plan to become pregnant or breastfeeding.

    The NHS says that if a doctor or health visitor says the baby is healthy, it is OK to take sertraline while breastfeeding, and describes it as one of the preferred antidepressants during breastfeeding.

    How is stopping sertraline managed?

    The NHS says stopping sertraline should involve reducing the dose gradually, which helps to prevent withdrawal symptoms. The NHS says this is normally done over several weeks or months, and the pace depends on the medicine, the dose and how long it has been taken. The UK patient leaflet says not to stop taking sertraline unless a doctor says so.

    The NHS says withdrawal symptoms usually begin within 5 days of stopping and generally last 1 to 2 weeks, but some people have severe symptoms that last for several months or more. The Royal College of Psychiatrists says withdrawal symptoms usually improve quickly, in days or even hours, if the antidepressant is restarted. The NHS says that if symptoms are severe, a doctor may suggest reducing the dose more slowly or going back to a higher dose before trying again. The NHS also says that once a person feels better, it is likely they will continue to take sertraline for several more months.
