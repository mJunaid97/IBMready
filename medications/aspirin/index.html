    Aspirin

    Also known as: acetylsalicylic acid, low-dose aspirin, 75 mg aspirin

    A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes; at higher doses it is a painkiller and anti-inflammatory.

    Open in the 3D explorerHeart anatomy
    Generic name
    Aspirin
    Drug class
    Antiplatelet drugs
    Also known as
    acetylsalicylic acid, low-dose aspirin, 75 mg aspirin
    Forms
    Tablets 75 mg (dispersible or enteric coated), 300 mg
    Onset and duration
    Antiplatelet effect within an hour of a chewed dose; lasts the life of the platelet.
    Monitoring
    No routine blood tests; watch for black stools, vomiting blood and unusual bruising.

    What it is used for

    • Preventing further heart attacks and strokes in people who have had one (secondary prevention)
    • During a suspected heart attack (300 mg chewed) and after stents
    • Pain, fever and inflammation at higher doses (now largely replaced by ibuprofen and paracetamol)
    • Pre-eclampsia prevention in high-risk pregnancy

    How it works in the body

    Irreversibly blocks the enzyme COX-1 in platelets so they cannot make thromboxane, the signal that makes them clump. Because platelets have no nucleus they cannot make new enzyme, so one small daily dose keeps them quiet for their whole 7-10 day life. Higher doses also block COX-2 in tissues, reducing prostaglandins that cause pain, fever and inflammation.

    Common side effects

    • Indigestion, stomach irritation
    • Increased bruising and bleeding time

    Serious: seek help

    • Stomach ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding (risk rises with age, alcohol, NSAIDs and anticoagulants)
    • Bleeding in the brain (rare)
    • Reye syndrome in children with viral illness: not for under-16s except on specialist advice
    • Bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthma

    Cautions and interactions

    • Not routinely used for primary prevention in healthy people: bleeding outweighs benefit
    • Active peptic ulcer, bleeding disorders, severe asthma triggered by aspirin
    • With anticoagulants, other NSAIDs or SSRIs, bleeding risk multiplies
    • Take with food; a proton pump inhibitor is often added in those at ulcer risk
