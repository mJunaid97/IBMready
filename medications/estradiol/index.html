    Estradiol (oestrogen)

    Also known as: oestradiol, oestrogen, estrogen, HRT, hormone replacement therapy, feminising hormones

    The main natural oestrogen, given as tablets, patches, gel, spray or vaginal preparations to replace what the ovaries no longer make after the menopause or in premature ovarian insufficiency, and, with testosterone suppression, as the feminising hormone in gender-affirming care. A progestogen is added to systemic oestrogen whenever the uterus is present; low-dose vaginal oestrogen acts locally and does not need one.

    Forms
    Tablets taken daily, alone or in combined packs with a progestogen; Transdermal patches changed once or twice a week, and a gel or spray applied to the skin daily; transdermal routes are preferred where clot risk is higher; Vaginal cream, pessary, tablet or ring for local genitourinary symptoms; Subcutaneous implants and, in some countries and specialist settings, injections
    Onset and duration
    Hot flushes and night sweats usually ease within a few weeks and settle over about three months; vaginal symptoms improve over weeks to a few months of local treatment. In feminising therapy breast growth begins after about three to six months and reaches its maximum after two to three years.
    Terminology
    ATC G03CA03
    Monitoring
    Review after about three months and then yearly: symptom control, blood pressure, bleeding pattern and breast awareness, with screening according to the organs a person has. Blood levels are not needed to guide HRT in most women; in gender-affirming care oestradiol, testosterone and the bloods set out in the Endocrine Society and UCSF guidelines are checked, including potassium if spironolactone is used.

    What it is used for

    • Menopausal symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats, disturbed sleep, low mood, joint aches) as hormone replacement therapy, combined with a progestogen when the uterus is present so that the endometrium is not overstimulated
    • Vaginal dryness, soreness, urinary frequency and recurrent urinary tract infection after the menopause (the genitourinary syndrome of menopause), as a vaginal cream, pessary, tablet or ring that acts locally
    • Premature ovarian insufficiency, surgical menopause after removal of the ovaries, and hypogonadism from other causes such as Turner syndrome or pituitary failure, including induction of puberty under specialist care; replacement is usually continued at least until the usual age of natural menopause to protect bone and the heart
    • Gender-affirming feminising hormone therapy for trans women and some non-binary people, together with a medicine that suppresses testosterone: an established use outside the product licence, prescribed by a specialist service and supported by the Endocrine Society and WPATH guidelines
    • Prevention of bone loss, for people at increased risk of fracture who cannot take other osteoporosis treatments: bone density and fracture risk improve while it is taken and the benefit falls after stopping

    How it works in the body

    Oestradiol is a steroid that passes into cells and binds the oestrogen receptor, a nuclear receptor that switches genes on or off over hours to days in bone, breast, brain, vessels and the genital tract. In the uterus it makes the endometrium proliferate, which is why a progestogen is added to systemic oestrogen when the uterus is present. Oral oestradiol passes through the liver first and raises clotting factors; patches, gel and spray avoid that first pass, and vaginal preparations act locally.

    Common side effects

    • Breast tenderness or enlargement, nausea, bloating, headache, leg cramps and fluid retention, most marked in the first months
    • Irregular or breakthrough bleeding in the first months of combined HRT; bleeding that begins after the pattern has settled needs assessment
    • Mood changes, and skin irritation under patches

    Serious: seek help

    • Venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism): the risk is raised with oral oestrogen, while NICE advises that transdermal preparations at standard strengths carry no risk above the background population risk.
    • Stroke: a small increase with oral HRT, larger in older women; the baseline risk under 60 is low
    • Breast cancer: combined oestrogen and progestogen HRT carries a small increase in risk that depends on how long it is taken and falls after stopping; oestrogen alone carries little or no increase
    • Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer if oestrogen is taken without a progestogen when the uterus is present
    • Gallbladder disease, raised blood pressure in a few people, worsening of migraine with aura on oral preparations, and a raised prolactin in feminising therapy

    Cautions and interactions

    • Not usually used after breast cancer or other oestrogen-dependent cancers, with undiagnosed vaginal bleeding, untreated endometrial hyperplasia, active or recent venous thromboembolism, or active liver disease
    • A history of clots, obesity, smoking, migraine with aura or raised cardiovascular risk favours a transdermal rather than an oral preparation; the decision is individual
    • Enzyme-inducing medicines (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin, St John's wort) lower oestradiol levels; oestrogens can lower lamotrigine levels and increase the levothyroxine dose needed
    • Bleeding after the menopause, or an unexpected change in bleeding on HRT, needs assessment to exclude endometrial cancer before treatment continues
    • Hormone replacement therapy is not a contraceptive, and most combined hormonal contraceptives use a different, synthetic oestrogen (ethinylestradiol). HRT changes or stops bleeding, so the usual rule of counting from the last natural period cannot be applied while taking it; contraception is continued until a clinician advises it can stop.
    • In gender-affirming care oestradiol is prescribed with a testosterone-suppressing medicine until orchidectomy or vaginoplasty, ethinylestradiol is avoided because of its higher clot risk, treatment may be paused around major surgery on the surgical team's advice, and fertility preservation is discussed first because feminising therapy reduces sperm production
