    Medication · Bone & osteoporosis

    Alendronate

    A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures. Its benefit builds slowly over months and treatment usually continues for some years, and the way the tablet is taken matters as much as the dose, because a tablet that lingers in the gullet can injure its lining.

    Product type

    Bisphosphonates
    Brand names
    UK Fosamax, Binosto · US Fosamax, Binosto
    Routes
    Oral
    Dosage forms
    Tablet, Effervescent tablet, Solution
    Usual preparations
    Tablets 70 mg once weekly (or 10 mg daily), swallowed with a full glass of plain water on an empty stomach, standing or sitting upright for 30 minutes before food, drink or other medicines; effervescent and liquid forms; risedronate weekly; ibandronate monthly; zoledronic acid infusion yearly (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Bone turnover falls within weeks; fracture reduction from about a year. NHS information notes that bone mineral density usually starts to rise in the months after starting and that several years of treatment are needed for the full effect. Usually 5 years, then reviewed (drug 'holiday').
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 46041 · ATC M05BA04 · FDA EPC Bisphosphonate [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Bone density scan every 3-5 years; calcium and vitamin D; kidney function for intravenous forms. Alendronate does not need routine drug-level or frequent blood monitoring; instead the NHS asks people to report the warning symptoms of its rarer harms promptly (new heartburn or swallowing difficulty, jaw or tooth problems, thigh or groin pain, eye pain, cramps or tingling) and reviews whether to continue after some years of treatment.

    What it is used for

    • Osteoporosis: preventing hip, spine and wrist fractures in postmenopausal women and men at high risk
    • Bone protection during long courses of steroid tablets
    • Paget disease of bone, a disorder of over-active bone remodelling, which US patient information lists alongside osteoporosis as a use of oral alendronate
    • Zoledronic acid infusions for osteoporosis (yearly), Paget disease, bone metastases and high calcium in cancer

    How it works in the body

    The drug binds tightly to bone mineral, and when osteoclasts (the cells that dissolve bone) resorb that surface they take it up, which disables their metabolism and triggers their death. Resorption falls while bone-building osteoblasts keep working for a time, so bone density rises by a few per cent and, more importantly, the microarchitecture is preserved; hip fractures fall by about 40% and spine fractures by half. NHS information puts the same idea simply: alendronic acid slows the rate at which bone is broken down. Because bone remodels slowly, the effect is not immediate: bone mineral density begins to rise in the months after starting, and the medicine is usually continued for several years to gain the full effect. US patient information (MedlinePlus) describes the action as preventing bone breakdown and increasing bone density. The tablet is irritant to the lining of the oesophagus, which is why it is swallowed first thing in the morning with plain tap water before any food, drink or other medicine, and why staying upright afterwards protects the gullet; taken incorrectly it can cause ulcers of the food pipe or sores in the mouth.

    Common side effects

    • Indigestion, heartburn, bloating or wind, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea; staying upright for at least 30 minutes after a dose and eating smaller, more frequent meals ease the indigestion
    • Dizziness or a spinning sensation, headaches, feeling sleepy or tired
    • Muscle and joint aches, swollen joints, hands or legs; flu-like illness after the first zoledronic acid infusion
    • Severe or prolonged diarrhoea can stop the combined or progestogen-only contraceptive pill from working properly

    Serious: seek help

    • Oesophageal ulcers and strictures if the dosing instructions are not followed: new or worsening heartburn, or pain or difficulty on swallowing, are the warning signs and the NHS advises stopping the tablets and contacting a doctor or NHS 111
    • Osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare, mostly with high-dose cancer regimens; dental check before starting): a loose tooth, mouth sores, or jaw swelling or pain need review by both doctor and dentist
    • Atypical femoral fractures after many years of use: thigh, hip or groin pain is a warning that can precede a break in the thigh bone
    • Bleeding from the gut, showing as black or red stools
    • Eye inflammation with blurred vision or painful, red eyes
    • Low blood calcium, felt as muscle cramps or tingling around the mouth or in the fingers; kidney impairment with intravenous forms
    • Serious allergic reaction with swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue or difficulty breathing, which needs an emergency (999) call

    Cautions and interactions

    • Correct vitamin D deficiency and ensure adequate calcium before starting
    • Not used with eGFR below 35, swallowing disorders, active oesophageal disease, or inability to sit upright
    • Taken first thing in the morning, before food, drink or other medicines, with plain tap water only: the NHS advises against mineral water, tea, coffee, juice or milk, and against lying down for 30 minutes afterwards, to protect absorption and the oesophagus
    • Dental review and good oral hygiene; tell the dentist
    • Alcohol in moderation is permitted, but heavy drinking weakens bone and raises fracture risk, undoing part of the benefit
    • Review at 5 years (3 for zoledronate): continue in those at highest risk
