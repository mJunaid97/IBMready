    Diclofenac

    Also known as: Voltarol, Voltaren, Diclomax, Dicloflex, Econac, Motifene, diclofenac sodium, diclofenac gel

    Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) that eases pain and swelling in problems affecting joints, muscles and bones. It comes as tablets, capsules, suppositories, gels, plasters and patches. Like other NSAIDs it can irritate the stomach, and long-term or high-amount use carries small risks to the heart, circulation and kidneys, so it is not suitable for everyone.

    What it is used for

    • Aches and pains, and problems with joints, muscles and bones, including arthritis, sprains and back pain
    • Migraines and pain after dental surgery
    • Gel, patches and plasters for joint pain, used on the area of the body that hurts
    • Children may be prescribed diclofenac for joint problems, with the type depending on their age

    How it works in the body

    Diclofenac is an NSAID and works by reducing hormones that cause inflammation and pain in the body. A gel, plaster or patch only works on the area of the body where it is put on.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling sick and stomach ache are common; the NHS advises taking tablets with a meal or snack, or just after eating
    • With gels, skin reactions where the gel is applied are the most common side effect

    Serious: seek help

    • Black poo, or yellowing of the skin or eyes, are warning signs the NHS lists, and prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111 is the route to take
    • Breathlessness with swollen legs can be a sign of heart failure and needs prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111
    • Chest pain or symptoms of a stroke need an emergency call to 999
    • Swelling of the lips, mouth, tongue or throat can be a serious allergic reaction and needs an immediate call to 999
    • Tablets and capsules can cause an ulcer in the stomach or gut after long use or in big amounts
    • There is a small risk of heart failure or kidney failure with the maximum amount taken for a long time

    Cautions and interactions

    • Not suitable for people who have had an allergic reaction to diclofenac, aspirin or other NSAIDs
    • The NHS also lists asthma, a history of stomach ulcers or bleeding, high blood pressure, heart failure, severe liver or kidney disease, Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, lupus and blood clotting disorders as reasons it may not be suitable
    • NSAIDs other than aspirin may carry a higher risk of heart attack or stroke than not using them, and the risk of artery-clotting events may rise with higher amounts and longer use
    • UK product information for diclofenac lists established heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, peripheral arterial disease and cerebrovascular disease as contraindications
    • The NHS says diclofenac is not usually recommended in pregnancy, and a doctor may advise it only if the benefits outweigh the risks
    • The NHS says diclofenac can be used while breastfeeding, because only tiny amounts reach breast milk
    • Other medicines needing care include some antibiotics, anticoagulants such as warfarin, digoxin and blood pressure medicines
    • The NHS says diclofenac can be taken with paracetamol or codeine, but aspirin, ibuprofen or naproxen should not be combined with it without talking to a doctor
    • The NHS says herbal remedies and supplements lack enough information to show they are compatible with diclofenac
    • Drinking too much alcohol may irritate the stomach and raises the risk of a stomach ulcer

    What is diclofenac used for?

    Diclofenac is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, or NSAID, which means it reduces swelling and pain. The NHS describes it as a treatment for aches and pains, and for problems with joints, muscles and bones. These include arthritis, sprains, back pain, migraines and pain after dental surgery. Diclofenac comes as tablets, capsules, suppositories, gels, plasters and patches. The NHS says gels, patches and plasters are used for joint pain in a particular area of the body. Children may be prescribed diclofenac for joint problems, with the type depending on their age.

    How does diclofenac work, and how quickly?

    Diclofenac works by reducing hormones that cause inflammation and pain in the body. Tablets and capsules take about 20 to 30 minutes to start working. Gels, plasters and patches usually take 1 to 2 days. A gel, plaster or patch only works on the area where it is put on, and the NHS describes gels as treatment for pain in one area. For tablets and capsules the NHS advice is the lowest amount that works for the shortest possible time.

    What are the side effects of diclofenac?

    Feeling sick and stomach ache are common, and the NHS advises taking tablets with a meal or snack, or just after eating. With gels, skin reactions where the gel is applied are the most common problem. The NHS describes options for irritated skin that include using the gel less often or leaving it off until the irritation settles, and says to speak to a doctor if it does not go away. Black poo, or yellowing of the skin or eyes, are warning signs, and prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111 is the route to take. Breathlessness with swollen legs can be a sign of heart failure and also needs prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111. Chest pain or symptoms of a stroke need an emergency call to 999. Swelling of the lips, mouth, tongue or throat can be a serious allergic reaction, and this also needs an immediate 999 call.

    Heart, circulation and stomach cautions

    MedlinePlus says NSAIDs other than aspirin may have a higher risk of heart attack or stroke than not using them. UK product information says the risk of artery-clotting events such as heart attack or stroke may rise with higher amounts and longer use. The NHS lists high blood pressure and heart failure among reasons diclofenac may not be suitable. UK product information for diclofenac also lists established heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, peripheral arterial disease and cerebrovascular disease as contraindications. The NHS says tablets and capsules can cause an ulcer in the stomach or gut after long use or in big amounts, and MedlinePlus says NSAIDs may cause ulcers, bleeding or holes in the stomach or intestine. There is also a small risk of heart failure or kidney failure with the maximum amount taken for a long time.

    Pregnancy, other medicines and alcohol

    The NHS says diclofenac is not usually recommended in pregnancy, and a doctor may advise it only if the benefits outweigh the risks. The NHS says it can be used while breastfeeding, because only tiny amounts reach breast milk. The NHS says some antibiotics, anticoagulants such as warfarin, digoxin and blood pressure medicines need caution with diclofenac. It says diclofenac can be taken with paracetamol or codeine, but aspirin, ibuprofen or naproxen should not be combined with it without talking to a doctor. There is not enough information to show that herbal remedies and supplements are compatible with it. The NHS says drinking alcohol within the national guidelines is possible, but too much may irritate the stomach and raises the risk of an ulcer.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does diclofenac gel work as fast as tablets?

    The NHS says tablets and capsules take 20 to 30 minutes to work, while gels, plasters and patches usually take 1 to 2 days. A gel, plaster or patch only works on the area where it is put on.

    Can I drink alcohol while taking diclofenac?

    The NHS says alcohol within the national guidelines is possible, but drinking too much may irritate the stomach. This raises the risk of a stomach ulcer, so a pharmacist or GP can advise if there are doubts.

    Can diclofenac be taken with other painkillers?

    The NHS says it can be taken with paracetamol or codeine. Aspirin, ibuprofen or naproxen should not be combined with it without talking to a doctor first.

    Is long-term diclofenac a concern for the heart?

    The NHS says there is a small risk of heart failure or kidney failure with the maximum amount taken for a long time. Anyone with chest pain or stroke symptoms needs an emergency call to 999.

    Can diclofenac be used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

    The NHS says diclofenac is not usually recommended in pregnancy, and a doctor may advise it only if the benefits outweigh the risks. The NHS says it can be used while breastfeeding, because only tiny amounts reach breast milk.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Headache Pain felt anywhere in the head.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Drug classes

    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
