    Zopiclone

    Also known as: Zimovane

    Zopiclone is a medicine used to treat insomnia, usually prescribed for just 2 to 4 weeks. The NHS says the body gets used to it quickly and that dependence is possible with longer use, and it advises against driving, riding a bike or using machinery for 12 hours after taking it. It is a controlled drug in the UK (Schedule 4 Part 1, Class C).

    What it is used for

    • Short-term treatment of insomnia in adults.
    • NICE says sleeping medicines such as zopiclone should be considered only for severe insomnia that interferes with daily life, and only for short periods.

    How it works in the body

    The NHS says zopiclone boosts the effect of GABA, a calming chemical in the brain, which helps with sleep. The NHS says caffeine has the opposite effect to zopiclone in the body and stops it working.

    Common side effects

    • The NHS lists a metallic or bitter taste, a dry mouth and daytime sleepiness as common side effects.
    • Some people have sleepwalked or made phone calls while asleep without remembering it afterwards, the NHS says.
    • The NHS advises against driving, riding a bike or using machinery for 12 hours after taking zopiclone.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS says a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is a rare possible side effect of zopiclone.
    • The summary of product characteristics says treatment should stop immediately if a complex sleep behaviour such as sleepwalking occurs.
    • In an emergency the route to care is 999 or A&E; for an urgent concern that is not an emergency it is NHS 111 or a same-day GP appointment, and for routine questions a GP appointment or a pharmacist.

    Cautions and interactions

    • The NHS says alcohol with zopiclone can cause very deep sleep, so that breathing is not normal and waking is very difficult.
    • The NHS says combining it with opioid painkillers or other sedating medicines can cause problems, and that cannabis, heroin or methadone can intensify its sedative effects.
    • The NHS says most adults over 18 can take zopiclone, and lists a past allergic reaction, liver or kidney problems, myasthenia gravis, breathing problems or sleep apnoea, a history of mental health problems and past alcohol or drug misuse as reasons for care.
    • A UK leaflet and the summary of product characteristics say it should not be used by anyone who has previously had complex sleep behaviours with it.
    • The NHS says taking it in pregnancy may cause drowsiness or withdrawal symptoms such as agitation or shaking in the newborn.
    • The NHS says zopiclone can be taken while breastfeeding if a doctor or health visitor confirms the baby is healthy, and that it is important not to share a bed with the baby while taking it.
    • The NHS says stopping suddenly can bring back insomnia, with anxiety and restlessness, although withdrawal symptoms are unlikely after less than a month of use.

    What is zopiclone used for?

    The NHS describes zopiclone as a medicine used to treat insomnia. The summary of product characteristics gives short-term treatment of insomnia in adults as its licensed use. The NHS says it is usually prescribed for only 2 to 4 weeks, because the body gets used to it quickly.

    Dependence and stopping

    The NHS says someone taking zopiclone for up to 4 weeks is unlikely to become addicted, and may become dependent if it is taken for longer. The NHS says it should not be stopped suddenly without speaking to a doctor. Withdrawal can bring back insomnia, with anxiety and restlessness, but symptoms are unlikely in people who have taken it for less than a month. A GP appointment is the route for questions about stopping, and NHS 111 or 999 if symptoms are severe.

    Driving, alcohol and sleep behaviours

    The NHS advises against driving, riding a bike or using machinery for 12 hours after taking zopiclone. Alcohol with zopiclone can cause very deep sleep, with breathing that is not normal and great difficulty waking. Some people have sleepwalked or made phone calls while asleep, and the summary of product characteristics says treatment should stop immediately if a complex sleep behaviour happens. Caffeine has the opposite effect to zopiclone and can stop it working, the NHS says.

    Side effects and who needs extra care

    Common side effects listed by the NHS are a metallic taste, a dry mouth and daytime sleepiness. The NHS says a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is a rare possible side effect of zopiclone. Liver or kidney problems, myasthenia gravis, breathing problems or sleep apnoea, a history of mental health problems and past alcohol or drug misuse are listed by the NHS as reasons for care. Zopiclone is a Class C drug in Schedule 4 Part 1 of the Misuse of Drugs Regulations.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is zopiclone addictive?

    The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to zopiclone, and that people taking it for up to 4 weeks are unlikely to become addicted.

    Is zopiclone a controlled drug?

    Yes. It is Class C and in Schedule 4 Part 1 of the Misuse of Drugs Regulations, from June 2014.

    Can zopiclone be taken with alcohol?

    The NHS says alcohol with zopiclone can cause very deep sleep with breathing problems and difficulty waking.
