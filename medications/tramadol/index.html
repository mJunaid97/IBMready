    Tramadol

    An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling, a controlled drug with a particular risk of seizures and serotonin syndrome when combined with antidepressants.

    What it is used for

    • Moderate to severe pain, including after surgery or injury, when non-opioid painkillers are not enough
    • Short-term use is preferred; long-term use for chronic non-cancer pain is discouraged

    How it works in the body

    Tramadol and, more strongly, the active metabolite the liver makes from it through CYP2D6, act on mu-opioid receptors in the brain and spinal cord to dampen pain signalling. Unlike other opioids it also blocks the reuptake of serotonin and noradrenaline, which adds to pain relief but is why it can cause serotonin syndrome with antidepressants and lowers the seizure threshold. People who lack CYP2D6 activity get less pain relief; ultra-rapid metabolisers get more opioid effect.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling sick or being sick, constipation, dry mouth
    • Dizziness, drowsiness, headache, tiredness
    • Sweating, feeling agitated

    Serious: seek help

    • Slow or shallow breathing, extreme drowsiness, especially with alcohol, benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids
    • Seizures, more likely at high doses or with antidepressants and other seizure-threshold-lowering medicines
    • Serotonin syndrome with antidepressants, triptans or St John's wort
    • Dependence, withdrawal on stopping, and misuse
    • Signs of an allergic reaction

    Cautions and interactions

    • A controlled drug: prescribed for the shortest time and reviewed regularly
    • Not combined with MAOI antidepressants; caution with SSRIs, SNRIs, tricyclics and other serotonergic medicines
    • Alcohol, benzodiazepines and other sedatives increase respiratory depression
    • Epilepsy or a low seizure threshold
    • Reduced doses or avoidance in severe kidney or liver impairment; carbamazepine reduces its effect
    • Driving: it is an offence in the UK to drive with the ability impaired by tramadol

    What is tramadol used for?

    Tramadol belongs to a group of medicines called opioids. The NHS describes it as a strong painkiller used to treat moderate to severe pain. A doctor may also prescribe it for severe pain when weaker painkillers do not help.

    The NHS says standard forms usually start to ease pain within about 30 to 60 minutes, and the relief wears off after 4 to 6 hours. Slow-release forms take longer to start working but last longer. Tramadol also raises serotonin levels in the brain, which is why it can interact with certain antidepressants and other medicines.

    What happens if too much is taken?

    The NHS lists warning signs of taking too much: feeling very sleepy, sick or dizzy, difficulty breathing, and becoming unconscious. It says the amount that can lead to an overdose is different for everyone, and that help should be sought immediately if too much may have been taken. The NHS also says slow-release tablets are swallowed whole, because breaking, crushing, chewing or sucking them can cause an overdose.

    What are the side effects of tramadol?

    The NHS says common side effects, which affect more than 1 in 100 people, include headaches, feeling or being sick, constipation, dry mouth, sweating, and feeling sleepy or dizzy. Not everyone gets them.

    The NHS says to call 999 for a seizure (fit), breathing difficulties, or signs of a severe allergic reaction such as facial swelling or trouble breathing. It says to call 111 or a doctor for dizziness or weakness (which can be a sign of low blood pressure), hallucinations, confusion, or difficulty passing urine.

    A Royal Devon NHS leaflet says that anyone with symptoms of serotonin toxicity should not take the next dose and should seek urgent medical help. The NHS warns that using MDMA (ecstasy), cocaine, amphetamines or LSD with tramadol, or taking it with certain antidepressants, raises the risk of serotonin syndrome.

    Can you become addicted to tramadol, and what is withdrawal like?

    The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to tramadol. Signs include finding it hard to stop or feeling the need to take it more often than necessary. Long-term use can also lead to tolerance, meaning higher doses are gradually needed to control pain. Stopping can bring on withdrawal symptoms.

    A Royal Devon NHS leaflet advises against stopping tramadol suddenly and says anyone who wants to stop should discuss it with a doctor or pharmacist. The NHS says that people who have taken tramadol for more than a few weeks should speak to a doctor before stopping, and that a treatment plan may set out how and when to stop.

    Can tramadol be taken with other medicines, alcohol or when driving?

    The NHS asks people to tell their doctor about any other medicines they take, including ones for depression or other mental health problems, pain, sleep, anxiety, allergy, blood clots or infection. Prescribing guidance from an NHS trust says tramadol should not be given to people taking monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs), a type of antidepressant. The NHS says carbamazepine, buprenorphine, ondansetron and rifampicin can weaken or shorten the effect of tramadol, and that products containing codeine, including some over-the-counter combinations such as co-codamol, are best avoided. MedlinePlus, a US source, says St John's wort and tryptophan may interact with tramadol. The NHS says there is very little information on taking herbal remedies or supplements with it.

    The NHS says it is best not to drink alcohol with tramadol because side effects such as sleepiness are more likely. It also says not to drive, cycle or use tools or machinery if tramadol makes a person sleepy, dizzy or clumsy, or if they have blurred vision or cannot concentrate.

    Who may not be able to take tramadol, and what about pregnancy or breastfeeding?

    The NHS says most adults and children aged 12 and over can take tramadol. It lists groups who may not be able to take it, including people who have had an allergic reaction to tramadol or other medicines, and people with a seizure or fit condition, a head injury, breathing difficulties, kidney or liver problems, or an addiction to alcohol, strong painkillers or recreational drugs.

    The NHS says tramadol is not known to cause problems with a baby's development, but more studies are needed to confirm this. If it is taken near the end of pregnancy, the baby might have withdrawal symptoms after birth. For breastfeeding, the NHS says tramadol passes into breast milk in very small amounts and is unlikely to cause side effects in the baby. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service says it can be used short term with caution and monitoring of the infant, at the lowest effective dose for the shortest time, and that babies under 2 months are most at risk. Some other NHS leaflets advise against it while breastfeeding, so the decision rests with a doctor or pharmacist.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is tramadol addictive?

    The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to tramadol. Signs include finding it hard to stop or feeling the need to take it more often than necessary. Stopping can bring withdrawal symptoms. The NHS says people who have taken it for more than a few weeks should speak to a doctor before stopping.

    When should someone call 999 about tramadol?

    The NHS says to call 999 for a seizure, breathing difficulties, or signs of a severe allergic reaction such as facial swelling or trouble breathing. For dizziness or weakness, hallucinations, confusion or difficulty passing urine, it says to call 111 or a doctor.

    Why does tramadol interact with antidepressants?

    Tramadol raises serotonin levels in the brain, so combining it with certain antidepressants raises the risk of serotonin syndrome, the NHS says. NHS trust prescribing guidance says it should not be given to people taking MAOI antidepressants.

    Can you drink alcohol or drive while taking tramadol?

    The NHS says it is best not to drink alcohol with tramadol because side effects such as sleepiness are more likely. It says not to drive, cycle or use tools or machinery if the medicine makes a person sleepy, dizzy or clumsy.
