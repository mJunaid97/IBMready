    Metformin

    The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin, without causing low blood sugar on its own.

    Metformin lowers blood glucose mainly by telling the liver to release less sugar between meals, and by helping muscle respond to insulin. It does not make the pancreas release more insulin, so on its own it does not cause low blood sugar.

    How it works

    Reduces the amount of glucose the liver releases into blood (by inhibiting gluconeogenesis via AMP-activated protein kinase and the mitochondrial redox state), increases the sensitivity of muscle and fat to insulin, and modestly reduces glucose absorption from the gut. It does not stimulate insulin release, so it does not cause hypoglycaemia on its own or weight gain; HbA1c falls by about 11 mmol/mol (1%).

    Mechanism of action

    Metformin accumulates in hepatocytes via the OCT1 transporter and reduces hepatic gluconeogenesis: it lowers the mitochondrial energy state (partial inhibition of complex I and of mitochondrial glycerophosphate dehydrogenase, shifting the cytosolic redox state) and activates AMP-activated protein kinase, which suppresses gluconeogenic gene expression and lipogenesis. Peripheral insulin sensitivity improves modestly, intestinal glucose uptake and GLP-1 secretion increase, and the gut microbiome changes. Because insulin secretion is not stimulated, hypoglycaemia does not occur with monotherapy and weight is neutral or slightly reduced. Metformin is not metabolised and is cleared by the kidney (tubular secretion); accumulation in renal failure, hypoxia or severe illness impairs lactate clearance and can precipitate lactic acidosis. It reduces ileal vitamin B12 absorption.

    Side effects

    Common

    • Nausea, diarrhoea, bloating, metallic taste (a third at the start, most settle; take with food, build up slowly, or use modified release)
    • Vitamin B12 deficiency with long-term use

    Serious: seek help

    • Lactic acidosis: very rare, but dangerous when the drug accumulates in kidney failure, or in severe illness, dehydration, sepsis, heart failure or heavy alcohol use
    • Hypoglycaemia only when combined with insulin or sulfonylureas

    Source: NHS: Metformin. Frequencies follow the source's categories; no percentages are invented.

    Serious safety information

    • Boxed warning (US). US labelling carries a boxed warning for lactic acidosis, a rare but potentially fatal complication that is more likely with kidney impairment, liver disease, heart failure, sepsis, dehydration, excess alcohol, older age and iodinated contrast; the drug is stopped immediately if it is suspected. US
    • Special warning. Lactic acidosis: UK information advises stopping metformin and seeking urgent help for fast or deep breathing, muscle cramps, stomach pain, unusual sleepiness or feeling very cold, particularly during acute illness with dehydration. UK

    Warnings and precautions

    • Kidney. Contraindicated when eGFR is below 30 mL/min/1.73 m²; the dose is reviewed at eGFR 30-45 and kidney function is checked before starting and at least annually (more often when function is declining). UK
    • Precaution. Iodinated contrast media: metformin is withheld on the day of a contrast procedure and for 48 hours afterwards in people with reduced kidney function, and restarted once kidney function is confirmed stable. UK
    • Precaution. Sick-day guidance: during vomiting, diarrhoea, fever or any illness that stops eating and drinking, UK information advises pausing metformin and restarting 24-48 hours after recovery. UK
    • Monitoring. Vitamin B12 deficiency: long-term metformin lowers B12 levels; UK regulators recommend checking B12 when anaemia, neuropathy or other symptoms of deficiency appear, and periodically in people at risk. UK

    Alcohol

    Heavy or binge drinking while taking metformin increases the risk of lactic acidosis and of low blood sugar, particularly when meals are missed. (Alcohol impairs hepatic lactate clearance and gluconeogenesis)

    UK information advises keeping within recommended limits and avoiding binge drinking; occasional moderate alcohol with food is acceptable for most people. UK

    Special populations

    Pregnancy
    UK information states metformin can be used in pregnancy, alone or with insulin, when a doctor advises it, and NICE recommends it in gestational diabetes and pre-existing type 2 diabetes; it crosses the placenta but has not been linked with birth defects. Use in pregnancy is outside the UK product licence. UK
    Breastfeeding
    Small amounts pass into milk; UK information states metformin can be taken while breastfeeding and NICE supports its use after birth. UK
    Children
    Licensed for type 2 diabetes in children from 10 years, started by a specialist with the same kidney precautions as adults. UK
    Older adults
    Kidney function declines with age, so eGFR is checked more often and doses are reduced accordingly; frailty and intercurrent illness raise lactic acidosis risk, and HbA1c targets are relaxed. UK
    Kidney impairment
    The central safety issue: full dose when eGFR is above 45, dose review and a maximum of 1 g daily between 30 and 45, and stop below 30. Kidney function is rechecked during any acute illness. UK
    Liver impairment
    Avoided in significant hepatic impairment because reduced lactate clearance increases the risk of lactic acidosis; alcohol-related liver disease is a particular concern. UK

    Effects on tests and results

    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin): HbA1c falls by about 11 mmol/mol (1%) at full dose; the intended effect, measured every 3-6 months. UK
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV): Vitamin B12 levels fall with long-term use, which can cause macrocytic anaemia and neuropathy; B12 is checked when symptoms or a raised MCV appear. UK
    • Blood lactate rises in lactic acidosis; metformin is stopped while any acute illness that lowers kidney function or oxygen delivery is treated. UK
    • eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate): Kidney function determines the dose rather than being changed by the drug; eGFR is the monitoring test. UK

    Pharmacokinetics

    Absorption
    About 50-60% absorbed from the small intestine; absorption is slowed by food and saturable at high doses
    Peak
    2-3 hours (immediate release); 7 hours (modified release)
    Half-life
    About 6.5 hours in plasma; longer in red cells
    Metabolism
    Not metabolised
    Elimination
    Excreted unchanged by the kidney through filtration and tubular secretion (OCT2/MATE); clearance falls in proportion to eGFR; removed by haemodialysis

    What is metformin used for and how does it work?

    Metformin is a medicine that is used to treat type 2 diabetes. The NHS says it can also be used for gestational diabetes and for polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS), the name the NHS now uses for what was previously called polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). It is also used to help prevent type 2 diabetes in people at high risk of developing it. Gestational diabetes is diabetes that first appears during pregnancy.

    The NHS says metformin lowers blood sugar levels by improving the way the body handles insulin. Other descriptions add that it reduces how much glucose the liver releases. For PMOS, the NHS says it works by lowering insulin and blood sugar levels, and that it can improve ovulation and encourage regular periods, even in people without diabetes. The NHS says this use is not officially approved. It comes as standard tablets, which release metformin quickly, and as slow-release tablets, which work gradually.

    What are the side effects of metformin?

    The NHS lists feeling or being sick, diarrhoea, tummy pain and loss of appetite among the common side effects. A change in taste is also listed. The NHS says most of these ease after a couple of weeks. The NHS advises taking metformin with a meal to reduce side effects. Vitamin B12 deficiency is also listed as a side effect and is more likely with long-term use. Doctors may check vitamin B12 levels with a blood test in some people who take metformin for a long time.

    Lactic acidosis, a build-up of acid in the blood, is a very rare but serious side effect. Symptoms the NHS lists include being sick, stomach pain, muscle cramps, breathing problems and severe tiredness. The NHS says to call 999 if lactic acidosis or a severe allergic reaction is suspected. For other serious side effects, such as inflammation of the liver, the NHS says to call NHS 111. A UK patient leaflet says lactic acidosis can lead to serious illness that needs hospital care.

    Taking metformin with alcohol, other illnesses or before scans

    The NHS advises limiting how much alcohol is drunk while taking metformin, because it can increase the risk of serious side effects. UK leaflets say drinking too much alcohol may increase the risk of lactic acidosis.

    A UK patient leaflet says metformin is stopped before or at the time of an injection of contrast dye, and the doctor says when to restart. A review of manufacturers' labels found that all of them advise stopping metformin before scans that use contrast dye. UK patient leaflets say metformin is stopped for a short time with a condition that may cause dehydration, such as severe vomiting or diarrhoea. The NHS says that anyone who has taken more than the prescribed amount should call NHS 111.

    Who needs extra care with metformin?

    The NHS says most adults and children aged 10 years and over can take metformin, but some people should tell the doctor first. These include people with uncontrolled diabetes, liver or kidney problems, a severe infection, heart failure or a high alcohol intake. A past allergic reaction to metformin is also on the NHS list.

    The risk of lactic acidosis is higher when the kidneys do not work well, UK patient leaflets say. The same leaflets say the doctor checks how well the kidneys work regularly during treatment. The NHS says that if blood sugar levels are stable, metformin should not affect the ability to drive, cycle or use machinery and tools. The NHS says the risk comes from other diabetes medicines that can lower blood sugar.

    Metformin in pregnancy and breastfeeding

    The NHS says metformin can be taken in pregnancy, but advises checking with a doctor first. The NHS says that in gestational diabetes, medicines taken to control blood sugar are usually stopped after the baby is born.

    The NHS says that if the doctor or midwife says the baby is healthy, metformin can be taken while breastfeeding. Only tiny amounts pass into breast milk, the NHS says. A review of manufacturers' labels found that they advise against metformin during breastfeeding, which differs from the NHS advice. The NHS says a health professional should be contacted if a breastfed baby feeds poorly or seems unusually drowsy or restless. The NHS also says that if metformin causes vomiting or severe diarrhoea for more than 24 hours, contraceptive pills may not protect against pregnancy.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can metformin cause lactic acidosis?

    Lactic acidosis is a very rare but serious side effect. The NHS lists being sick, stomach pain, muscle cramps, breathing problems and severe tiredness as symptoms. It says to call 999 if lactic acidosis is suspected. UK patient leaflets say the risk is higher when the kidneys do not work well, and that heavy drinking may also raise it.

    How can the stomach side effects of metformin be eased?

    The NHS says the most common side effects are feeling or being sick, diarrhoea, tummy pain and loss of appetite. It advises taking metformin with a meal to reduce them, and says most side effects ease after a couple of weeks. Standard tablets release metformin quickly, while slow-release tablets work gradually.

    Can alcohol be drunk while taking metformin?

    The NHS advises limiting how much alcohol is drunk while taking metformin, because it can increase the risk of serious side effects. UK leaflets link drinking too much alcohol to a higher risk of lactic acidosis. The NHS asks people who drink heavily to tell the doctor before starting the medicine.

    Can metformin cause vitamin B12 deficiency?

    The NHS lists vitamin B12 deficiency among the side effects of metformin, and it is more likely with long-term use. Because of this, doctors may check vitamin B12 levels with a blood test in some people who take metformin for a long time. The common stomach side effects are separate, and most of them ease after a couple of weeks, the NHS says.
