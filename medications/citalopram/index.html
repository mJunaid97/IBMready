    Citalopram

    A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily, with a maximum dose set by its effect on the heart's QT interval.

    Active ingredient
    Citalopram (variants: citalopram hydrobromide)
    Product type
    Small-molecule medicine

    Brand names

    Routes

    Tablet, Drops
    Forms
    Tablets 10, 20 and 40 mg once daily; oral drops (a different strength equivalence from tablets)
    Onset and duration
    Benefit usually within 4 to 6 weeks; side effects appear first. Reduced gradually at the end of treatment to avoid discontinuation symptoms.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 2556 · ATC N06AB04 · FDA EPC Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitor [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Mood and suicidal thoughts in the first weeks of treatment and after dose changes; an ECG before or during treatment in people with heart disease or on other QT-prolonging medicines; sodium in older people if drowsy or confused.

    What it is used for

    • Depression
    • Panic disorder
    • Also used for some anxiety disorders under specialist advice

    How it works in the body

    Citalopram blocks the serotonin transporter that carries serotonin back into the nerve ending after it has been released, so more serotonin stays in the synapse and signalling through serotonin receptors increases. Mood and anxiety improve gradually over several weeks as receptor sensitivity and downstream signalling adapt; the immediate rise in serotonin explains the early nausea and restlessness.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling sick, dry mouth, sweating, tiredness
    • Difficulty sleeping, feeling restless or shaky
    • Reduced appetite, weight change, sexual problems

    Serious: seek help

    • Thoughts of self-harm, especially in the first weeks and under 25 years of age
    • Palpitations or fainting from a prolonged QT interval, more likely at higher doses or with other QT-prolonging medicines
    • Bleeding, particularly with NSAIDs, aspirin or anticoagulants
    • Serotonin syndrome with other serotonergic medicines: agitation, fever, muscle twitching
    • Low sodium, especially in older people

    Cautions and interactions

    • Maximum daily dose is lower in people over 65 and in liver impairment because of the QT effect
    • Not combined with other QT-prolonging medicines or with MAOI antidepressants
    • Bleeding risk rises with NSAIDs, aspirin and anticoagulants
    • Closer monitoring for suicidal thoughts when starting, especially in young people
    • Serotonin syndrome risk with tramadol, triptans, St John's wort and other serotonergic medicines

    Other medicines

    Citalopram + Azithromycin

    ModerateMonitoring or dose adjustment advised

    Azithromycin with citalopram, which also prolongs the QT interval, increases the risk of dangerous heart rhythms.

    Why it can occur
    QT prolongation
    What official information says
    US information advises telling a doctor about medicines for irregular heartbeat and other listed drugs, whose doses may need changing or monitoring; the BNF advises caution or avoidance depending on the drug.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official information: an alternative antibiotic where possible; otherwise ECG and electrolyte monitoring.
    What may be monitored
    ECG (QT interval) · Potassium and magnesium · Potassium and magnesium
    Context
    Higher risk with heart disease, bradycardia, low potassium or magnesium, older age
    Basis of the status
    MedlinePlus: Azithromycin precautions (doses may need changing or monitoring with these medicines)
