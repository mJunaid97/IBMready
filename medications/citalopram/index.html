    Citalopram

    A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor antidepressant used for depression and panic disorder, taken once daily, with a maximum dose set by its effect on the heart's QT interval.

    What it is used for

    • Depression
    • Panic disorder
    • Also used for some anxiety disorders under specialist advice

    How it works in the body

    Citalopram blocks the serotonin transporter that carries serotonin back into the nerve ending after it has been released, so more serotonin stays in the synapse and signalling through serotonin receptors increases. Mood and anxiety improve gradually over several weeks as receptor sensitivity and downstream signalling adapt; the immediate rise in serotonin explains the early nausea and restlessness.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling sick, dry mouth, sweating, tiredness
    • Difficulty sleeping, feeling restless or shaky
    • Reduced appetite, weight change, sexual problems

    Serious: seek help

    • Thoughts of self-harm, especially in the first weeks and under 25 years of age
    • Palpitations or fainting from a prolonged QT interval, more likely at higher doses or with other QT-prolonging medicines
    • Bleeding, particularly with NSAIDs, aspirin or anticoagulants
    • Serotonin syndrome with other serotonergic medicines: agitation, fever, muscle twitching
    • Low sodium, especially in older people

    Cautions and interactions

    • Maximum daily dose is lower in people over 65 and in liver impairment because of the QT effect
    • Not combined with other QT-prolonging medicines or with MAOI antidepressants
    • Bleeding risk rises with NSAIDs, aspirin and anticoagulants
    • Closer monitoring for suicidal thoughts when starting, especially in young people
    • Serotonin syndrome risk with tramadol, triptans, St John's wort and other serotonergic medicines

    Citalopram + Azithromycin

    ModerateMonitoring or dose adjustment advised

    Azithromycin with citalopram, which also prolongs the QT interval, increases the risk of dangerous heart rhythms.

    Why it can occur
    QT prolongation
    What official information says
    US information advises telling a doctor about medicines for irregular heartbeat and other listed drugs, whose doses may need changing or monitoring; the BNF advises caution or avoidance depending on the drug.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official information: an alternative antibiotic where possible; otherwise ECG and electrolyte monitoring.
    What may be monitored
    ECG (QT interval) · Potassium and magnesium · Potassium and magnesium
    Context
    Higher risk with heart disease, bradycardia, low potassium or magnesium, older age

    What is citalopram used for?

    The NHS describes citalopram as an antidepressant that is often used to treat low mood and depression. It is also sometimes used for panic attacks. It belongs to a group of antidepressants called selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, or SSRIs.

    For citalopram, the NHS says people usually need to take it for at least 6 months, and to keep taking it for as long as the doctor recommends. The NHS does not usually recommend citalopram for children and young people under 18.

    How long does it take to work, and what about stopping?

    The NHS says it may take one or two weeks before citalopram starts to work on symptoms, and it usually takes between four and six weeks before the full benefit is felt. The NHS advises giving it at least six weeks, and talking to the doctor if there is no improvement after six weeks. If side effects continue, the NHS says a different type of antidepressant may be needed.

    The NHS says citalopram should not be stopped suddenly or without talking to the doctor first, because this may cause withdrawal symptoms. If the person and the doctor decide to stop, the doctor may recommend reducing the amount gradually, which the NHS describes as usually over several weeks, or longer after a long course. The NHS lists feeling dizzy, feeling sick, trouble sleeping, feeling agitated or anxious, and headaches among possible withdrawal symptoms.

    What are the side effects of citalopram?

    The NHS describes common side effects as ones that affect more than 1 in 100 people. Examples it gives include headaches, sweating, dry mouth and sleep problems. It says these are usually mild and most ease after a couple of weeks as the body gets used to the medicine.

    The NHS also lists sexual side effects, such as a lower sex drive and erection or orgasm problems. It says these can sometimes be long lasting and may not get better even after the medicine is stopped.

    The NHS calls serious side effects rare, affecting fewer than 1 in 1,000 people. They include a severe allergic reaction, and serotonin syndrome, a rare but serious condition that causes a fast heartbeat, sweating, shaking, twitching muscles, and feeling confused or agitated. They also include a rare, life-threatening heart rhythm problem that causes a fast, irregular heartbeat, light-headedness and fainting. The NHS says bleeding from the gums, unexplained bruising, or blood in the urine or stools is a reason to speak to a doctor or call NHS 111.

    Where to get urgent help

    The NHS says to call NHS 111 if someone taking citalopram thinks they may be having serious side effects. It says to call 999 or go to A&E for chest pain or pressure, a fit or seizure for the first time, or feeling very dizzy or passing out. Sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue also needs a 999 call, according to the NHS.

    For thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, the NHS says to call 999 or go straight to A&E if you think you may be about to harm yourself, or if you already have. The NHS also suggests calling NHS 111 or using its online service to find support. Samaritans can be reached free, at any time and from any phone, on 116 123. The charity Mind says that if you have suicidal feelings while taking medication, you can contact your GP as soon as possible, or speak to a pharmacist or NHS 111.

    Can you take citalopram with other medicines or alcohol?

    The NHS advises not using St John's wort, a herbal remedy, with citalopram because it increases the risk of side effects. It says other herbal remedies and supplements are not tested in the same way as pharmacy and prescription medicines, and are generally not tested for their effect on other medicines. The NHS advises telling the doctor or pharmacist about any other medicines, herbal remedies, vitamins or supplements.

    The NHS says using citalopram with other medicines can increase the chance of side effects. Its list includes sumatriptan, strong painkillers such as tramadol, anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs), blood thinners, medicines that affect heart rhythm and other antidepressants.

    On alcohol, the NHS says it may make a person feel sleepy, and that it might be best to stop drinking alcohol until they see how the medicine makes them feel.

    Who needs extra care, and what about pregnancy and breastfeeding?

    The NHS lists a heart condition, such as a heart rhythm problem, among the reasons citalopram may not suit some people.

    According to the NHS, citalopram can be used in pregnancy if needed. The doctor will talk through the risks and benefits and prescribe the lowest effective amount. The NHS says some studies have suggested citalopram might occasionally affect a baby's heart development, but that if there is any risk it is small, and most babies born to women taking it have a normal heart. Taking it in the last month of pregnancy may slightly increase the risk of bleeding after delivery.

    If the doctor or health visitor says the baby is healthy, the NHS says citalopram can be taken while breastfeeding. It passes into breast milk in fairly small amounts, and the NHS says it is important to continue taking it to keep the mother well.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long does citalopram take to work?

    The NHS says citalopram may start to work on symptoms after one or two weeks. The full benefit usually takes four to six weeks. If there is no improvement after about six weeks, the NHS advises talking to the doctor.

    Can I drink alcohol on citalopram?

    The NHS says alcohol may make a person feel sleepy while taking citalopram. It says it might be best to stop drinking alcohol until they see how the medicine makes them feel.

    Can citalopram be stopped suddenly?

    The NHS says it should not be stopped suddenly, because this may cause withdrawal symptoms. If the person and the doctor decide to stop, the doctor may recommend reducing the amount gradually. The NHS lists feeling dizzy, feeling sick, trouble sleeping, feeling agitated or anxious, and headaches among possible withdrawal symptoms.

    Is citalopram used in pregnancy and breastfeeding?

    According to the NHS, citalopram can be used in pregnancy if needed, at the lowest effective amount. Taking it in the last month of pregnancy may slightly increase the risk of bleeding after delivery. It can be taken while breastfeeding if the doctor or health visitor says the baby is healthy.

    What should happen if someone has thoughts of harming themselves?

    The NHS says to call 999 or go straight to A&E if you think you may be about to harm yourself, or if you already have. Samaritans can be reached free on 116 123 at any time. Mind says that if you have suicidal feelings while taking medication, you can contact your GP as soon as possible, or speak to a pharmacist or NHS 111. For other serious side effects, the NHS says to call NHS 111.
