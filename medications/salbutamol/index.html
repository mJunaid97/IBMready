    Salbutamol

    Also known as: albuterol, reliever inhaler, blue inhaler

    The blue reliever inhaler: a short-acting beta-2 agonist that opens the airways within minutes to relieve asthma and COPD symptoms during an attack or before exercise. The NHS describes it as very effective when used properly, with few side effects beyond a brief tremor and a faster heartbeat, but it relieves the narrowing without treating the inflammation beneath it, so needing it often is itself a warning that the asthma is not controlled.

    What it is used for

    • Relief of wheeze and breathlessness in asthma and COPD ('reliever')
    • Before exercise in exercise-induced asthma
    • Nebulised or intravenous in severe asthma attacks and COPD flares
    • Lowering high potassium in emergencies (nebulised)
    • Known as albuterol in the United States, where it is supplied as pressurised aerosol inhalers, a dry powder inhaler and a nebuliser solution, with combination inhalers pairing it with ipratropium (for COPD) or with the steroid budesonide

    How it works in the body

    Stimulates beta-2 adrenergic receptors on the smooth muscle wrapped around the bronchi. This raises cyclic AMP inside the muscle cells, relaxing them within minutes so the airways widen and air moves more freely. It relieves the narrowing but does nothing for the underlying inflammation, which is why needing it more than twice a week signals poorly controlled asthma and the need for a preventer. The NHS describes it as a bronchodilator that loosens the muscles lining the airways so that breathing becomes easier, starting almost immediately after a puff and continuing to work for some hours; US information puts it as opening the air passages to the lungs. The same beta-2 receptors exist outside the airways, on skeletal muscle, blood vessels and the heart, and on the cells that move potassium into cells, which explains the side effects: a fine tremor of the hands, a faster heartbeat, cramps and, at high doses, a fall in blood potassium. The effect wears off after a few hours, so it is used when symptoms occur rather than on a schedule, and a reliever that is needed several times a day, or that does not bring an attack under control, is a sign that the airways are inflamed and swollen in a way a bronchodilator alone cannot fix; the NHS advises that a person whose breathing does not improve after using the inhaler during an attack needs emergency help. Used for years it causes no lasting damage to the lungs; the risk lies in relying on it instead of treating the inflammation.

    Common side effects

    • Fine tremor, fast heartbeat, palpitations, headache: the NHS notes that shakiness of the hands usually wears off after a short time, and that a regularly faster heartbeat is a reason to have the treatment reviewed
    • Feeling shaky, nervous or restless; muscle cramps; these often improve and disappear after a few days or weeks of use
    • Dizziness, during which driving, cycling and using machinery are avoided
    • Using too much causes a racing heart and shakiness

    Serious: seek help

    • Low potassium with high doses (with steroids, diuretics, theophylline): muscle pain or weakness, cramps, or an irregular or more noticeable heartbeat can be the signs
    • Arrhythmias in people with heart disease at high doses: a fast, irregular heartbeat that does not settle, or very bad dizziness, needs medical help
    • Paradoxical bronchospasm (rare); over-reliance masking worsening asthma, linked to asthma deaths: needing the inhaler several times in a day can mean the condition is getting worse and different treatment is needed, and breathing that does not improve after using it is an emergency (999)
    • Serious allergic reaction (rare): sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue or struggling to breathe needs a 999 call

    Cautions and interactions

    • Using more than three canisters a year, or needing it daily, means the asthma plan needs review
    • Anti-inflammatory reliever (budesonide-formoterol) is now preferred as the sole reliever in many guidelines
    • Inhaler technique matters: slow deep breath, hold for 10 seconds; spacers for children and during attacks
    • Beta blockers antagonise the effect
    • Not stopped unless a doctor advises, because breathing problems can worsen; there is no evidence of lasting harm from years of use, and alcohol and food do not interact
    • Pressurised canisters are labelled with the number of doses they contain and are discarded once that count is reached even if propellant remains, and an aerosol inhaler device is used only with the medicine it was designed for

    What is salbutamol used for?

    Salbutamol is a reliever inhaler, the kind many people know as the blue inhaler. The NHS describes it as a medicine that makes breathing easier when asthma or other breathing conditions cause wheezing and breathlessness. It is also used for symptoms of long-term lung conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

    According to the NHS, a reliever works quickly to ease symptoms, and people with asthma are also given a preventer inhaler that is used every day to help stop symptoms. The NHS adds that keeping a diary of how often the reliever is used helps when talking about asthma or COPD control with a doctor or nurse.

    How does a salbutamol inhaler work?

    Salbutamol is a bronchodilator, which means it relaxes the muscles around the airways so they widen. The NHS says it works almost straight away after a puff and keeps working for a few hours.

    Pressurised (metered-dose) inhalers, sometimes called puffers, contain hydrofluorocarbon propellants, while dry powder inhalers do not. The NHS says a person may be asked whether switching to a more environmentally friendly inhaler would suit them.

    What are the side effects of salbutamol?

    The NHS describes salbutamol as very effective with few side effects when it is used properly. Common side effects, which the NHS says affect more than 1 in 100 people, are feeling shaky, a faster heartbeat and headaches. Salbutamol can also cause dizziness or shakiness, and the NHS advises not to drive, cycle or use tools or machinery until feeling better.

    Using too much can make the heart beat faster than normal and cause shakiness. The NHS says to contact NHS 111 about a fast or noticeably pounding heartbeat after using too much, and to call 999 if there is chest pain. Serious side effects are rare. The NHS lists muscle pain or weakness, muscle cramps, a fast irregular heartbeat that does not settle, and very bad dizziness as warning signs, and it says new or worsening breathlessness or chest pain needs a 999 call or a trip to A&E. A serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is also possible. Sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue is one sign, and it needs an emergency 999 call.

    Can I drink alcohol or take salbutamol with other medicines?

    The NHS says alcohol is fine and normal eating and drinking can continue while using a salbutamol inhaler. It does not affect contraception, including the combined pill and emergency contraception.

    Salbutamol can generally be used with most medicines, but some combinations need care. The NHS says water tablets (diuretics), steroid tablets such as prednisolone, and theophylline or aminophylline can raise the risk of low potassium, and it names beta blockers as another combination that can cause problems. The NHS also says there is not enough information on how herbal remedies and supplements interact with salbutamol, and it advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor before starting any new medicine, herbal remedy or supplement.

    Who needs extra care with salbutamol, and is it used in pregnancy?

    The NHS says most adults and children can use salbutamol inhalers. Extra advice is suggested for people who have had an allergy to salbutamol or other medicines, or who have heart problems, an overactive thyroid, low potassium in the blood or diabetes.

    The NHS says salbutamol can be used in pregnancy, and stresses the importance of keeping asthma under control. For breastfeeding, the NHS says the inhaler can be used as normal. It says the amount reaching breast milk is not known but is likely to be very low, and that it has been used this way for many years without side effects in babies. The NHS also says there is no evidence that salbutamol affects fertility in men or women.

    What if I need my inhaler more often or have an asthma attack?

    The NHS says that needing salbutamol more often than usual can mean a condition is getting worse, and it advises speaking to a doctor, pharmacist or nurse.

    For an asthma attack, the NHS says to call 999 if breathing does not improve after using the reliever inhaler. Asthma and Lung UK says to call 999 if the reliever is not helping or the person feels worse at any point, and that calling an ambulance means treatment can start on the way to hospital. After an attack that was managed with the reliever alone, Asthma and Lung UK advises asking for an urgent same-day appointment with a GP or nurse.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does using a salbutamol inhaler for many years cause harm?

    The NHS says there is no evidence that salbutamol does any lasting damage to the body, even if it is used for many years. Needing it more often than usual can be a sign that a condition is getting worse, and the NHS advises speaking to a doctor, pharmacist or nurse about that.

    Can I drive after using my blue inhaler?

    The NHS says salbutamol can cause dizziness or shakiness in some people. If that happens, it advises not to drive, cycle or use tools or machinery until feeling better, and says that anyone in doubt should not drive.

    What happens if I use too much salbutamol?

    Using too much can make the heart beat faster than normal and cause shakiness. The NHS says to contact NHS 111 about a fast or noticeably pounding heartbeat after using too much, and to call 999 if there is chest pain.

    Does salbutamol affect contraception?

    According to the NHS, salbutamol does not affect any type of contraception. This includes the combined pill and emergency contraception. The NHS also says alcohol is fine and normal eating and drinking can continue while using the inhaler.

    Is salbutamol an emergency treatment?

    The reliever inhaler is what the NHS and Asthma and Lung UK describe using during an asthma attack. The NHS says that if breathing does not improve after using it, a 999 call is needed. Needing it more often than usual is a reason to speak to a doctor, pharmacist or nurse.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.

    Medical tests

    • Spirometry A forced breath out into a tube measures how much air the lungs can move and how fast.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.

    Procedures

    • Bronchoscopy A thin flexible scope is passed through the nose or mouth, between the vocal cords and down the trachea into the bronchi of each lung.

    Medications

    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Montelukast Montelukast is a prescription medicine that helps prevent asthma symptoms.

    Drug classes

    • Bronchodilators Inhaled medicines that relax the muscle around the airways within minutes (salbutamol, terbutaline) or for about 12 hours (salmeterol.

    Guides and news

    • Asthma and COPD: how they differ Asthma is a long-term airway condition whose symptoms come and go; COPD is mostly smoking-related and tends to get worse over time.
    • Asthma in children: signs and NHS advice Asthma in children brings wheeze, cough and a tight chest; the NHS says to call 999 if the reliever is not helping during an attack.
    • Reliever and preventer inhalers explained A reliever inhaler eases symptoms when they happen, while a preventer is taken every day to reduce airway inflammation.
