    Naproxen

    Also known as: Naprosyn

    Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, used for conditions such as period pain, back pain, gout and arthritis. It can cause ulcers and bleeding in the stomach or gut, so the lowest amount that works is used for the shortest time. Vomiting blood or passing black, tar-like poo needs a call to a GP or NHS 111 straight away.

    What it is used for

    • Period pain
    • Joint and muscle pain and swelling, including osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis and gout
    • Back pain, sprains and strains
    • In the US, prescription naproxen is also used for juvenile arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis

    How it works in the body

    Naproxen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). It works by reducing hormones that cause inflammation and pain in the body. This eases swelling and pain in joints and muscles.

    Common side effects

    • Constipation or diarrhoea, bloating and stomach pain, and feeling or being sick
    • Dizziness, feeling sleepy or tired, and headaches
    • A rash and ringing in the ears

    Serious: seek help

    • Ulcer in the stomach or gut, more likely after long use or in big doses; vomiting blood or dark particles like coffee grounds, blood in the poo or black tar-like poo are signs of bleeding, and the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 now
    • Serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis): sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, or very fast or difficult breathing, needs a 999 call
    • Chest pain, which could be a heart attack, needs a 999 call
    • Rarely, aseptic meningitis (fever, sickness, confusion, headache, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light), for which the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E

    Cautions and interactions

    • People who have had an allergic reaction to, or a serious side effect from, naproxen, ibuprofen, aspirin or another NSAID are advised not to take it
    • A past stomach ulcer or bleeding: a doctor may advise against naproxen
    • The NHS lists conditions such as high blood pressure, severe liver or kidney failure, severe heart failure and other heart problems, Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, lupus and blood-clotting disorders among those that may rule naproxen out or call for a doctor's check
    • UK patient leaflets for naproxen say to talk to a doctor or pharmacist first about asthma or allergies such as hay fever
    • Not taken together with other NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or aspirin, because this raises the risk of serious side effects such as stomach ulcers
    • Interacting medicines named by the NHS include anticoagulants such as warfarin, steroids, antidepressants and medicines to lower blood pressure
    • Alcohol can increase the risk of side effects such as stomach ulcers or bleeding, so the NHS advises limiting it
    • The NHS says naproxen is not usually recommended in pregnancy or while breastfeeding

    What is naproxen used for?

    Naproxen is a painkiller that also treats inflammation, and it belongs to the group of medicines called NSAIDs. The NHS says paracetamol is usually the best treatment for most types of pain, but naproxen is better for some types, such as period pain or back pain. It can also help with joint and muscle pain, gout, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, sprains and strains.

    Naproxen comes as tablets or a liquid that is swallowed. Some types can be bought from pharmacies and others are prescribed. Adults and teenagers aged 15 and over can buy it from a pharmacy for period pain. MedlinePlus, a US source, says prescription naproxen treats osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, juvenile arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis. It says non-prescription naproxen is used for fever and mild pain such as headaches, muscle aches, menstrual pain, colds, toothaches and backaches.

    How does naproxen work and how quickly?

    Naproxen is an NSAID, and it works by reducing hormones that cause inflammation and pain in the body. The NHS says this reduces swelling and pain in joints and muscles. People usually start to feel better about 1 hour after taking it. With regular use it can take up to 3 days to work properly.

    The NHS advises that the lowest amount that controls symptoms is used for the shortest possible time. A UK patient leaflet says naproxen should preferably be taken with or after food.

    What are the side effects of naproxen?

    The NHS lists constipation or diarrhoea, dizziness, feeling sleepy or tired, bloating and stomach pain, headaches, feeling or being sick, a rash and ringing in the ears as common side effects.

    Naproxen can cause an ulcer in the stomach or gut, especially after long use or in big doses. Vomiting blood or dark particles that look like coffee grounds, blood in the poo or black poo that looks like tar can be signs of bleeding in the stomach or gut. The NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 now if these happen.

    A serious allergic reaction is rare, but sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, or very fast or difficult breathing, needs a 999 call. Chest pain, which could be a heart attack, also needs a 999 call. The NHS also says to call 999 or go to A&E for possible aseptic meningitis, with fever, sickness, confusion, headache, neck stiffness and sensitivity to light.

    Who needs extra care with naproxen?

    The NHS says naproxen is not suitable for people who have had an allergic reaction to, or a serious side effect from, naproxen, ibuprofen, aspirin or another NSAID. A doctor may advise against it for someone who has or has had a stomach ulcer. The NHS lists conditions such as high blood pressure, severe liver or kidney failure, severe heart failure and other heart problems, Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, lupus and blood-clotting disorders among those that may rule naproxen out or call for a doctor's check.

    MedlinePlus says older adults usually take lower doses for short periods.

    Naproxen with other medicines, alcohol and in pregnancy

    The NHS says naproxen should not be taken at the same time as other NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or aspirin, because this raises the risk of serious side effects such as stomach ulcers. It can also interact with anticoagulants such as warfarin, steroid medicines, antidepressants and medicines to lower blood pressure.

    The NHS says people can drink alcohol while taking naproxen, but limiting it is advised because alcohol can increase the risk of stomach ulcers or bleeding. In pregnancy, naproxen is not usually recommended because it may affect the baby, in particular their circulation and the level of amniotic fluid. The NHS advises avoiding naproxen in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist advises it. Naproxen passes into breast milk in small amounts, and the NHS says it is not usually recommended while breastfeeding. Large amounts or long use of anti-inflammatories like naproxen can affect ovulation, which may make it harder to get pregnant.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is naproxen the same as ibuprofen?

    Both are NSAIDs, a group of painkillers that also reduce inflammation. The NHS advises against taking naproxen at the same time as another NSAID such as ibuprofen or aspirin. This is because it raises the risk of serious side effects such as stomach ulcers.

    Can I drink alcohol while taking naproxen?

    The NHS says people can drink alcohol while taking naproxen. It advises limiting how much, because alcohol can increase the risk of side effects such as stomach ulcers or bleeding.

    Does naproxen affect driving?

    The NHS says naproxen will not usually affect the ability to drive, ride a bike or use machinery. If someone feels dizzy or tired or has problems with their vision after taking it, the NHS advises waiting until these effects have gone before driving.

    Can I take naproxen in pregnancy?

    The NHS says naproxen is not usually recommended in pregnancy because it may affect the baby, in particular their circulation and amniotic fluid levels. The NHS says paracetamol is usually the best painkiller in pregnancy.

    When should I get urgent help with naproxen side effects?

    Call 999 for sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, trouble breathing or chest pain. For vomiting blood, blood in the poo or black tar-like poo, the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 now.

    Conditions

    • Osteoarthritis The commonest joint disease: progressive loss of articular cartilage with changes in the underlying bone, causing pain and stiffness.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.

    Symptoms

    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.

    Drug classes

    • NSAIDs Painkillers that also reduce inflammation and fever: ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac and the COX-2 selective coxibs.
