    Ramipril

    An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure, after heart attacks and to protect the kidneys in diabetes. It begins to lower blood pressure within hours but takes weeks to show its full effect, and because people often feel no different while taking it, the benefit is judged by blood pressure readings and the blood tests of kidney function and potassium that accompany every dose change; its signature side effect is a dry cough, and its dangerous ones are angio-oedema, kidney injury when dehydrated and harm to an unborn baby.

    What it is used for

    • High blood pressure (first choice with type 2 diabetes at any age, and under 55 if not of Black African or African-Caribbean family origin)
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (improves survival); US information describes improved survival in heart failure after a heart attack
    • After a heart attack, and more broadly to reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke or death in people at high cardiovascular risk
    • Protecting the kidneys in diabetes and chronic kidney disease with protein in the urine
    • Angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan, candesartan) do the same job for people who cough on ACE inhibitors

    How it works in the body

    Blocks the enzyme that converts angiotensin I to angiotensin II, the hormone that constricts arteries, drives the adrenal glands to release aldosterone (salt and water retention) and thickens heart muscle and vessel walls. Less angiotensin II means relaxed vessels, lower pressure, less strain on a failing heart and lower pressure inside the kidney's filters. ACE also breaks down bradykinin, which accumulates and causes the characteristic dry cough. The NHS describes the result simply: ramipril relaxes and widens the blood vessels, which lowers blood pressure and makes it easier for the heart to pump blood around the body. It starts to lower blood pressure within a few hours, but the full effect takes a few weeks, and in heart failure it can be weeks or months before the person feels better; feeling no different does not mean it is not working, since high blood pressure itself usually causes no symptoms. The same hormone blockade explains the hazards. Angiotensin II normally holds up the pressure inside the kidney's filtering units when blood volume falls, so when a person on ramipril becomes dehydrated through vomiting, diarrhoea or fever, or takes an anti-inflammatory painkiller, the kidneys can be starved of filtration pressure; this is why the medicine is paused on sick days. Less aldosterone means the kidneys hold on to potassium, which is why potassium supplements and potassium-based salt substitutes are avoided. Ramipril controls blood pressure and heart failure without curing them, so it is continued long term, and stopping it lets the blood pressure rise again.

    Common side effects

    • Dry tickly cough (10-15%, from bradykinin): the NHS notes it sometimes settles with continued treatment, but a persistent cough that disturbs sleep is a reason to consider a different medicine
    • Dizziness or light-headedness with the first doses or after a dose increase, especially on standing up quickly; getting up slowly helps, and driving, cycling and using machinery are avoided until it passes. Tiredness, weakness and headache
    • A small rise in creatinine and potassium
    • Diarrhoea, vomiting or an upset stomach, and a mild skin rash; a stomach bug with vomiting or diarrhoea may mean pausing the medicine on medical advice, and severe or prolonged diarrhoea can stop contraceptive pills working

    Serious: seek help

    • Angio-oedema: swelling of the lips, tongue, face and throat, can block the airway; stop immediately and call 999
    • Acute kidney injury when dehydrated, on NSAIDs, or with narrowed renal arteries: swollen ankles, blood in the urine or passing very little urine are the warning signs
    • High potassium, especially with spironolactone, kidney disease or potassium supplements
    • Harm to the fetus: stop before or as soon as pregnancy is known; US labelling carries a warning that the medicine is not taken in pregnancy
    • Liver problems (yellowing of the skin or eyes), inflamed pancreas (severe stomach pain), and blood or bone marrow disorders (paleness, tiredness, unusual bleeding or bruising, sore throat, high temperature or repeated infections), all rare
    • Fainting, a fast heartbeat or chest pain, and the signs of a stroke, which are emergencies; serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is rare

    Cautions and interactions

    • Pause on 'sick days' with vomiting, diarrhoea or fever to protect the kidneys
    • Avoid with NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen and naproxen) and potassium supplements; caution with diuretics (first-dose hypotension); not combined with sacubitril/valsartan
    • Bilateral renal artery stenosis, previous angio-oedema (from any cause), pregnancy: do not use
    • Potassium-based salt substitutes such as Lo-Salt are avoided because, combined with ramipril, they can raise blood potassium too high
    • Alcohol increases the blood-pressure-lowering effect and can cause dizziness, so it is avoided in the first days and after dose increases
    • The anaesthetist is told before a general anaesthetic or major operation; the medicine may be paused shortly before surgery on medical advice
    • Heart, liver or kidney disease, diabetes, lupus and scleroderma are mentioned to the prescriber; breastfeeding is discussed. Treatment is long term and not stopped without advice because blood pressure rises again

    What is ramipril used for?

    The NHS describes ramipril as a medicine widely used to treat high blood pressure and heart failure. It is also prescribed after a heart attack, and the NHS says it helps prevent future strokes, heart attacks and kidney problems. The NHS says a person taking ramipril for high blood pressure may not feel any different, but this does not mean the medicine is not working. According to the NHS, ramipril starts to lower high blood pressure within a few hours, but it may take a few weeks to work fully. The NHS says ramipril is generally used long term, and that blood pressure could rise again if it is stopped. Ramipril comes in several forms, including capsules, tablets and an oral solution, which is a liquid. UK patient leaflets say the safety and effectiveness of ramipril capsules in children has not been established. The NHS has separate pages on ramipril side effects, on pregnancy, breastfeeding and fertility, and on taking it with other medicines, which can be read alongside this article. MedlinePlus, a US health site, also has a drug information page on ramipril.

    How does ramipril work?

    Ramipril belongs to a group of medicines called ACE inhibitors, the NHS says. The NHS says ramipril relaxes and widens the blood vessels, which lowers blood pressure and makes it easier for the heart to pump blood around the body. StatPearls, a medical reference on the NCBI Bookshelf, says ramipril and its active form, ramiprilat, block an enzyme called ACE. ACE also breaks down a substance called bradykinin, so blocking it raises bradykinin levels, which StatPearls links to the dry cough. StatPearls says the cough usually starts somewhere between a week and six months after an ACE inhibitor is started. UK labelling says high potassium in the blood can occur in people with impaired kidney function or those taking potassium supplements, including salt substitutes, potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim or co-trimoxazole. The same labelling notes that blood potassium usually stays within normal limits, although high potassium may occur in some people.

    What are the side effects of ramipril?

    The NHS describes a dry cough as the most common side effect of ramipril. The NHS says cough medicines do not usually help a cough caused by ramipril. Sometimes the cough gets better on its own if the person keeps taking ramipril, the NHS says. If the cough is bothersome, the NHS says a doctor might suggest changing to a different medicine. StatPearls says angiotensin receptor blockers cause much less cough than ACE inhibitors, which is why they are often used as an alternative. The NHS lists feeling dizzy and headaches among the common side effects, and says the very first dose may make a person feel dizzy. The NHS says to call 999 now if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or if someone is breathing very fast, struggling to breathe, or has a tight throat or difficulty swallowing. The NHS says swelling of the lips, tongue and face may signal angioedema, and that people with Black skin have a higher chance of getting it. A UK patient leaflet says swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat can happen at any time during treatment.

    Taking ramipril with other medicines, alcohol or salt substitutes

    A UK patient leaflet says that medicines used to lower blood pressure and alcohol can have additive effects. The NHS says alcohol can make dizziness worse, and advises not drinking alcohol until a person sees how ramipril affects them. UK leaflets say anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen can make ramipril work less well. The NHS says daily low-dose aspirin can be taken with ramipril. UK leaflets list spironolactone among the medicines that can increase the amount of potassium in the blood. MedlinePlus advises talking to a doctor before using salt substitutes that contain potassium. UK labelling says ramipril can raise lithium levels in the blood, and that it may lower blood sugar, so people using insulin or diabetes tablets need close monitoring. UK leaflets list sacubitril/valsartan as a medicine to avoid combining with ramipril.

    Pregnancy, breastfeeding and who needs extra care

    The NHS does not recommend ramipril in pregnancy, and says anyone trying to conceive should talk to a doctor first. The NHS also advises planning any pregnancy with a GP or specialist. The NHS says there is no clear evidence that ramipril reduces fertility in either men or women. According to the NHS, ramipril does not affect any type of contraception, but vomiting or prolonged diarrhoea can make contraceptive pills less reliable. The NHS says some hormonal contraception, such as the combined pill and the contraceptive patch, is not usually recommended for people with high blood pressure. On breastfeeding, the NHS says ramipril can be taken, but a person should talk to a doctor or pharmacist first, and it says the medicine is best avoided if the baby was born prematurely. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service says ramipril can be used with caution during breastfeeding, but enalapril is preferred, and that this applies to healthy full-term babies only. UK labelling says ramipril should not be used by people with a history of angioedema linked to ACE inhibitors, or with abnormally low or unstable blood pressure. A UK patient leaflet says dialysis or other types of blood filtering may make ramipril unsuitable, depending on the machine used.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I drink alcohol while taking ramipril?

    A UK patient leaflet says that blood-pressure medicines and alcohol can have additive effects. The NHS says alcohol can make dizziness worse. The NHS advises not drinking alcohol until a person sees how ramipril affects them. It also says the very first dose may make a person feel dizzy.

    Is swelling of the face an emergency?

    Sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue can be a sign of angioedema. The NHS says to call 999 now if this happens. The NHS gives the same advice if someone is breathing very fast, struggling to breathe, or has a tight throat or difficulty swallowing. Swelling in the throat can make breathing difficult. A UK patient leaflet says swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat can happen at any time during treatment.

    What if ramipril gives me a cough?

    A dry cough is the most common side effect of ramipril, the NHS says. Sometimes it gets better on its own if the person keeps taking ramipril. The NHS says cough medicines do not usually help, and that if the cough is bothersome a doctor might suggest a different medicine. StatPearls says that after an ACE inhibitor is stopped, the cough usually goes within one to four days, although it can last up to a month.

    Can I take ibuprofen or use a salt substitute?

    UK leaflets say anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen can make ramipril work less well. The NHS says daily low-dose aspirin can be taken with ramipril. MedlinePlus advises talking to a doctor before using salt substitutes that contain potassium. UK leaflets also list spironolactone among the medicines that can increase the amount of potassium in the blood.

    Can ramipril be taken in pregnancy?

    The NHS does not recommend ramipril in pregnancy. The NHS says anyone trying to conceive should talk to a doctor first, and advises planning any pregnancy with a GP or specialist. It also says there is no clear evidence that ramipril reduces fertility in either men or women.

    Connected pages in detail

    Conditions

    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.

    Symptoms

    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Urine protein Healthy kidney filters hold back almost all protein.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.

    Procedures

    • Coronary angioplasty and stenting A balloon and stent passed through a catheter from the wrist or groin reopen a narrowed or blocked coronary artery.
    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.

    Medications

    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Spironolactone Spironolactone is a diuretic, a medicine that helps reduce fluid build-up in the body.

    Drug classes

    • ACE inhibitors Medicines ending in -pril that block the enzyme which makes angiotensin II, lowering blood pressure.

    Guides and news

    • ACE inhibitors, ARBs and amlodipine compared ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.
    • High blood pressure: what happens next High blood pressure usually causes no symptoms and is found by measuring it; treatment starts with lifestyle changes and may include medicine.
