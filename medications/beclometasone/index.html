    Beclometasone (inhaled)

    Also known as: beclomethasone, beclometasone dipropionate inhaler

    An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks; the preventer that reliever inhalers cannot replace.

    Forms
    Metered-dose inhaler (best with a spacer) and dry-powder inhaler, usually twice daily; combination inhalers with formoterol; different brands are not interchangeable dose for dose
    Onset and duration
    Improvement over 1-2 weeks; full effect after several weeks of regular use. Not a reliever: it does nothing for an attack in progress.

    Monitoring
    Asthma control (symptoms, reliever use, peak flow) at each review; inhaler technique; growth in children; the lowest dose that keeps control is used.

    What it is used for

    • Preventing asthma symptoms and attacks, used every day even when well
    • COPD only in combination inhalers for people with frequent exacerbations
    • Nasal spray and cream forms treat rhinitis and skin inflammation (separate products)

    How it works in the body

    Inhaled into the airways, beclometasone enters the cells lining them and binds glucocorticoid receptors, which switch off the genes driving inflammation and switch on anti-inflammatory ones. Over days to weeks the swelling, mucus and twitchiness of asthmatic airways subside, so attacks become less likely. Because it acts locally at small doses, the whole-body effects of steroid tablets are largely avoided, though the mouth and throat are exposed with each dose.

    Common side effects

    • Oral thrush and a hoarse voice, reduced by rinsing the mouth and using a spacer
    • Sore throat, cough

    Serious: seek help

    • Rarely, at high doses: adrenal suppression, slowed growth in children, bone thinning, cataracts, glaucoma or bruising
    • Worsening breathing immediately after a dose (paradoxical bronchospasm)
    • Signs of an allergic reaction

    Cautions and interactions

    • Taken every day as prescribed; stopping it is a common cause of asthma attacks
    • Brands and devices differ in dose equivalence and are prescribed by brand
    • High doses: a steroid card, and monitoring of growth in children
    • Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ritonavir, some antifungals) can raise steroid exposure
    • Tuberculosis or untreated respiratory infections

    Severity not gradedInteraction documented

    Systemic corticosteroids raise blood glucose and can reduce diabetes control on metformin.

    Why it can occur
    pharmacodynamic antagonism
    What official information says
    UK information lists steroid tablets among medicines that affect blood sugar.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Documented; glucose monitoring during and after a steroid course.
