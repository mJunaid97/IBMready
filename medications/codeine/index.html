    Codeine and co-codamol

    Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury, and co-codamol is codeine combined with paracetamol for aches and pains. The NHS lists constipation, drowsiness and sickness as common effects, and says it is possible to become addicted to codeine. Co-codamol contains paracetamol, so taking other paracetamol-containing medicines as well can lead to too much paracetamol, which can harm the liver.

    What it is used for

    • Codeine is used to treat pain, for example after an operation or injury.
    • Co-codamol is used for aches and pains such as period pain, muscle pain and toothache.
    • Co-codamol is often used when other painkillers, such as paracetamol on its own, have not worked.

    How it works in the body

    Codeine is an opioid that acts on the central nervous system and the brain to block pain signals to the rest of the body. It also slows digestion, which is why it can cause constipation. Co-codamol adds paracetamol, another painkiller, to the codeine.

    Common side effects

    • Constipation, feeling sick or being sick, and feeling sleepy or tired.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS describes a serious allergic reaction, slow or shallow breathing, an irregular heartbeat and low blood pressure as rare serious side effects of codeine, and says to get emergency help (999) for breathing difficulty, a fit or allergic symptoms such as throat swelling.
    • The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to codeine, and a prescriber or pharmacist can be asked about any concern.
    • The NHS advises against stopping codeine suddenly after regular use, and says the doctor reduces the dose gradually over weeks or months.

    Cautions and interactions

    • Codeine is not suitable for everyone: the NHS lists a lung problem, a head injury, adrenal gland problems, a condition that causes fits, an addiction to alcohol, an underactive thyroid, and kidney or liver problems among the reasons, as well as a previous reaction to codeine.
    • The NHS says not to give codeine to children under 12, and that people aged 12 to 18 are given it only if everyday painkillers have not worked.
    • The NHS says codeine should not be given to anyone under 18 who has had their tonsils or adenoids removed because of obstructive sleep apnoea.
    • The NHS says codeine can be used during pregnancy, but long-term use is not advised, and use at the end of pregnancy can leave the baby with withdrawal symptoms and needing extra time in hospital for observation.
    • The NHS says not to take codeine while breastfeeding, because small amounts pass into breast milk and can cause breathing problems and drowsiness in the baby.
    • The NHS says drinking alcohol with codeine may make a person sleepier and can raise the risk of serious side effects.
    • The NHS says recreational drugs such as cannabis, cocaine or heroin raise the risk of serious side effects of codeine, including breathing difficulties.
    • Co-codamol leaflets say not to drive or use machinery if it causes dizziness or drowsiness.
    • The NHS advises against taking co-codamol with other paracetamol-containing medicines unless a doctor says so, and the leaflets include cold and flu remedies among them.

    What are codeine and co-codamol used for?

    Codeine is an opioid painkiller that the NHS describes as a medicine for pain, for example after an operation or injury.

    Co-codamol is a combination of two painkillers, paracetamol and codeine. It is used for aches and pains, including period pain, muscle pain and toothache. The NHS says it is used when everyday painkillers such as ibuprofen, aspirin or paracetamol on their own have not worked.

    The NHS says codeine is not suitable for everyone. It should not be given to children under 12, and young people aged 12 to 18 are given it only if everyday painkillers have not worked.

    Product leaflets show that co-codamol comes in different strengths. The NHS says the lowest strength can be bought from a pharmacy, while higher strengths need a prescription.

    How do codeine and co-codamol work?

    Codeine is an opioid. The NHS says it acts on pain receptors in the central nervous system and the brain to block pain signals to the rest of the body. It also slows digestion, which is why it can cause constipation.

    In co-codamol the codeine is combined with paracetamol. The NHS says co-codamol can take up to an hour to start working.

    Side effects, constipation and dependence

    The NHS lists constipation, feeling or being sick, and feeling sleepy as common side effects of codeine and co-codamol.

    Because codeine slows digestion, the NHS suggests eating more fibre and drinking plenty of fluids for constipation. If that does not help, a laxative can be used after checking with a pharmacist or doctor, and the NHS says lactulose is usually the best choice.

    The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to codeine, and advises talking to a doctor if there is a worry. It advises against stopping suddenly after regular use, and says the doctor reduces the dose gradually over weeks or months.

    The NHS describes a serious allergic reaction, slow or shallow breathing, an irregular heartbeat and low blood pressure as rare serious side effects. It says to call 999 or go to A&E for breathing difficulty, a fit or allergic symptoms such as swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue.

    Paracetamol in co-codamol and other medicines

    Co-codamol contains paracetamol. The NHS advises against combining it with other paracetamol-containing medicines unless a doctor has said to, because of the risk of too much paracetamol. The product leaflets say cold and flu remedies are among the products to check.

    The NHS says taking too much co-codamol can be harmful, and the leaflets say too much paracetamol can cause delayed, serious liver damage.

    The leaflets say to get medical advice if too much has been taken, even when feeling well. A North Tees and Hartlepool NHS leaflet gives NHS 111 as the number to call, and says to call 999 or go to A&E in an emergency.

    Who needs extra care, and what about pregnancy, driving and alcohol?

    The NHS lists a lung problem, a head injury, adrenal gland problems, a condition that causes fits, an addiction to alcohol, an underactive thyroid, and kidney or liver problems among the reasons codeine may not be suitable. It also says codeine should not be given to anyone under 18 who has had their tonsils or adenoids removed because of obstructive sleep apnoea. A pharmacist or doctor can say whether it suits a particular person.

    The NHS says codeine can be used during pregnancy, but long-term use is not advised. Codeine used near the end of pregnancy can cause withdrawal symptoms in the baby, who may need extra time in hospital for observation.

    The NHS says not to take codeine while breastfeeding, because small amounts reach breast milk and can cause breathing problems and drowsiness in the baby. The Specialist Pharmacy Service says the MHRA gives the same advice. A doctor or pharmacist can recommend a more suitable painkiller.

    Co-codamol leaflets say it can cause dizziness or drowsiness, and not to drive or use machinery if affected. The NHS says alcohol may make a person sleepier and can raise the risk of serious side effects, and that recreational drugs such as cannabis, cocaine or heroin also raise the risk of serious side effects, including breathing difficulties.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can you become addicted to codeine?

    The NHS says it is possible to become addicted to codeine and to talk to a doctor about any worry. The NHS advises against stopping suddenly after regular use, and says the doctor reduces the dose gradually.

    Why does codeine cause constipation?

    The NHS says codeine slows down digestion, which is why it can cause constipation. The NHS suggests more fibre and fluids, and a laxative after checking with a pharmacist or doctor.

    Can co-codamol be taken with other paracetamol medicines?

    Co-codamol already contains paracetamol, and the NHS and product leaflets advise against taking other paracetamol-containing medicines with it. Cold and flu remedies are among those to check, because too much paracetamol can cause liver damage.

    Is codeine used while breastfeeding?

    The NHS advises against it, because small amounts of codeine pass into breast milk and can cause breathing problems and drowsiness in the baby. A doctor or pharmacist can recommend a more suitable painkiller.

    Can you drink alcohol with co-codamol?

    The NHS says drinking alcohol with codeine may make a person sleepier and can raise the risk of serious side effects. Co-codamol leaflets say not to drive or use machinery if drowsy or dizzy.
