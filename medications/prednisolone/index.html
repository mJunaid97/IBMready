    Prednisolone and steroids

    Also known as: steroid tablets

    A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease and many autoimmune conditions. Short courses are usually well tolerated, but the longer and higher the course the more the body shows the effects of excess steroid: weight gain, raised blood sugar and pressure, thinning bones, infections and mood change, and a shut-down of the body's own cortisol production that makes sudden stopping dangerous.

    Anti-inflammatory effect within hours; symptom relief in asthma and arthritis within a day or two. Weight gain becomes likely once a course lasts more than a few weeks, and the long-term harms (bone thinning, diabetes, eye problems, high blood pressure) build with months and years of use.

    Monitoring
    Blood pressure, glucose, weight; bone density and eye checks on long courses. The NHS lists the warning signs people are asked to watch for between reviews (signs of infection, of high blood sugar, of adrenal or Cushing's problems, mood change and eyesight change), and on long courses clinicians look for the slow harms of thinner bones, poorly controlled diabetes, high blood pressure and, in children, slowed growth.

    What it is used for

    • Asthma attacks and COPD flares (5-day courses)
    • Flares of rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, gout, polymyalgia rheumatica, temporal arteritis
    • Autoimmune disease, severe allergic reactions, some cancers and transplant rejection; US information also lists multiple sclerosis relapses and the symptoms of some cancers
    • Replacing cortisol in adrenal insufficiency (hydrocortisone); dexamethasone in brain swelling and severe COVID-19
    • Prednisolone eye drops for inflammation of the eye from allergy, infection, chemical or physical injury and after eye surgery; enemas and suppositories for inflammation of the rectum and lower bowel

    How it works in the body

    A synthetic version of cortisol, the adrenal stress hormone, at doses far above what the body makes. It enters cells and binds glucocorticoid receptors that switch off genes for inflammatory cytokines and switch on anti-inflammatory ones, shrinking the whole inflammatory response, whatever its trigger: it does not treat the cause. The same receptors in liver, muscle, bone, skin and brain produce the side effects, and prolonged dosing suppresses the pituitary-adrenal axis so the body's own cortisol production shuts down. The NHS explains it to the reader as reducing swelling (inflammation) and calming down the immune system, which is why it helps autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, where the immune system attacks the body's own tissues; US information puts it as reducing swelling and redness and changing the way the immune system works. Because the dose is higher than the body's own cortisol output, every tissue that responds to cortisol responds to the medicine: appetite and fluid retention rise, blood sugar and blood pressure drift up, bone is lost, skin thins and mood and sleep change. The suppression of the body's own production is the reason a course of more than a few weeks is tapered rather than stopped: as US information puts it, if the medicine is stopped suddenly the body may not have enough naturally produced steroid to function normally, and the NHS describes withdrawal with tiredness, weakness, body aches and joint pain as well as a flare of the original condition. Taking the dose once in the morning copies the body's own rhythm and reduces sleeplessness.

    Common side effects

    • Increased appetite and weight gain, fluid retention, mood change, insomnia: the NHS notes weight gain is likely with courses of more than a few weeks because the medicine increases hunger and water retention, and that a morning dose reduces sleep problems and restlessness
    • Raised blood glucose (new or worsened diabetes), raised blood pressure
    • Indigestion, eased by taking the dose with food and avoiding rich or spicy meals, with a stomach-protecting medicine considered if it persists; thin skin, bruising, acne
    • Sweating; mood changes including irritability, anxiety or feeling low or high, which a minority of people experience and which are reported to a doctor

    Serious: seek help

    • Adrenal suppression: after more than 3 weeks the dose must be tapered, and extra cover is needed for illness and surgery; abrupt stopping can cause an adrenal crisis. Severe sickness, dizziness or extreme tiredness can signal adrenal problems, and a rounded 'moon' face with weight gain on the upper body signals Cushing's syndrome from prolonged excess
    • Osteoporosis and fractures (bone protection with bisphosphonates for long courses), avascular necrosis of the hip
    • Infections (masked and more severe: chickenpox, shingles, measles, TB reactivation), peptic ulcer with NSAIDs, cataract and glaucoma: a high temperature, a very sore throat, cough, pain on passing urine or a wound that will not heal can be signs of infection, and eyesight changes are reported
    • Psychosis, severe mood disturbance including depression, feeling high or thoughts of self-harm, which the NHS advises reporting to a doctor or NHS 111; growth suppression in children; muscle wasting
    • High blood sugar (excessive thirst, passing urine often, fruity-smelling breath), low potassium (muscle pain, cramps, changes in heart rate), inflamed pancreas (severe stomach or back pain), breathlessness or swelling of the arms or legs, unusual bruising or bleeding, red or black stools
    • Serious allergic reaction with swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue or difficulty breathing, which needs an emergency (999) call

    Cautions and interactions

    • Carry a steroid emergency card on courses longer than 3-4 weeks; the NHS issues a blue steroid card for courses of more than 3 weeks or at high doses
    • Never stop long-term steroids suddenly; sick-day doubling rules. Withdrawal causes tiredness, weakness, body aches and joint pain and the original condition can flare
    • Check for TB, hepatitis, diabetes and blood pressure before long courses; consider PPI and bone protection; people with diabetes may need their diabetes treatment adjusted by their clinician while on steroids
    • Live vaccines avoided on high doses; report exposure to chickenpox, shingles or measles if not immune
    • Real liquorice is avoided because it is thought to increase the amount of prednisolone in the body and the risk of low potassium
    • Taken once in the morning with food; the lowest dose that controls the condition is used, and long courses are reviewed for the lowest effective dose

    What is prednisolone used for?

    Prednisolone is a steroid, or corticosteroid, medicine. The NHS describes it as treating swelling (inflammation) and calming down the immune system. It is available only on prescription, as tablets or as a liquid that is swallowed.

    The NHS lists conditions that may affect whether prednisolone is suitable, including recent infections, liver problems, heart failure, high blood pressure, diabetes, epilepsy and glaucoma. Steroids can also slow growth in children and teenagers.

    How is prednisolone taken?

    The NHS says most people take prednisolone in the morning, because this stops it keeping them awake. Most people take it after breakfast. The NHS advises swallowing tablets whole with water and measuring the liquid with the cup or spoon supplied.

    What are the side effects of prednisolone?

    The NHS lists problems sleeping, weight gain and indigestion as the most common side effects. Headaches, feeling sick and mood changes such as irritability are also listed. Long-term use can lead to thinner bones (osteoporosis), poorly controlled diabetes, eyesight problems and slower growth in children and teenagers. Prednisolone can also make infections more likely.

    Serious side effects are uncommon and are more likely with a high dose or longer use. They include a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), mood changes including suicidal thoughts or mania, Cushing's syndrome, adrenal insufficiency. The NHS says to call 999 for possible anaphylaxis.

    For signs of infection (such as a high temperature, a very sore throat, a cough or pain when peeing), high blood sugar, Cushing's syndrome, adrenal problems or low potassium, the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away. It says to talk to a doctor or contact NHS 111 about mood changes such as feeling depressed, feeling high or having strange and frightening thoughts, and to contact NHS 111 about thoughts of harming oneself. According to the NHS, black or dark brown vomit, or vomiting blood, means going to A&E.

    Steroid treatment cards and stopping prednisolone suddenly

    The NHS says people who take prednisolone for a longer period, or at a high dose, are given a blue steroid treatment card. A red steroid emergency card may also be recommended, depending on the risk to the adrenal glands, which a doctor or pharmacist assesses.

    The NHS says prednisolone should not be stopped suddenly after long use or at high doses, because this can cause withdrawal side effects. Adrenal insufficiency is also among the serious side effects the NHS lists. For signs of adrenal problems, such as severe vomiting, dizziness, muscle weakness or weight loss, the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away. Sick day guidance from one NHS trust for people on long-term steroids says that vomiting with tablets that cannot be kept down needs a 999 call immediately.

    Chickenpox, shingles and measles while taking prednisolone

    The NHS says to tell a doctor promptly after contact with someone who has an infectious illness such as measles, chickenpox or shingles while taking prednisolone. The NHS pages say to contact the doctor, or to see a pharmacist or call NHS 111, after such contact. The doctor may be able to prescribe a medicine to protect the person.

    Other medicines, alcohol, liquorice and pregnancy

    The NHS prednisolone page says alcohol can be drunk, though some NHS hospital leaflets note that it may irritate the stomach. The NHS says not to eat real liquorice while taking prednisolone.

    The NHS pregnancy page says prednisolone can be taken during pregnancy. The NHS says the benefits usually outweigh any risks, and the baby's growth is checked regularly. For breastfeeding, the NHS says prednisolone can be taken if the doctor or health visitor confirms the baby is healthy. It passes into breast milk in small amounts and has been used for many years in breastfeeding women without side effects in their babies.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can prednisolone be stopped suddenly?

    The NHS says prednisolone should not be stopped suddenly after long use or at high doses, because this can cause withdrawal side effects. Adrenal insufficiency is among the serious side effects the NHS lists. For signs of adrenal problems, the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away.

    Why do some people carry a steroid card?

    People who take prednisolone for a longer period or at a high dose are given a blue steroid treatment card. A red steroid emergency card may be recommended, depending on the risk to the adrenal glands, which a doctor or pharmacist assesses.

    What should happen after contact with chickenpox, shingles or measles?

    The NHS says to tell a doctor promptly after contact with measles, chickenpox or shingles while taking prednisolone, and to contact the doctor, or see a pharmacist or call NHS 111.

    Can a person drink alcohol or eat liquorice on prednisolone?

    The NHS prednisolone page says alcohol can be drunk, though some NHS hospital leaflets note that it may irritate the stomach. The NHS says not to eat real liquorice.

    When do prednisolone side effects need urgent help?

    The NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away for signs of infection, high blood sugar, Cushing's syndrome, adrenal problems or low potassium. Possible anaphylaxis needs a 999 call, and vomiting blood or black vomit means going to A&E. For thoughts of harming oneself, the NHS says to contact NHS 111.

    Conditions

    • Asthma A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways causing reversible narrowing, with episodes of wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness and cough.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Symptoms

    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.

    Imaging

    • DXA scan (bone density) A quick, very low-dose X-ray measurement of bone mineral density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.
    • Cataract surgery The eye's clouded natural lens is broken up with ultrasound through a tiny corneal incision and replaced with a folded artificial lens.

    Medications

    • Inhaled corticosteroids The preventer inhalers of asthma: low doses of steroid delivered straight into the airways damp down inflammation so attacks become rarer and milder.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Alendronate A weekly bisphosphonate that slows bone breakdown, strengthening bone in osteoporosis and cutting the risk of hip and spine fractures.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Beclometasone (inhaled) An inhaled corticosteroid taken every day to calm the airway inflammation of asthma and prevent attacks.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Chlorphenamine A first-generation antihistamine, sold over the counter, that relieves hay fever.
    • Hydroxychloroquine Hydroxychloroquine is a medicine used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, lupus and some skin conditions.

    Drug classes

    • Corticosteroids Synthetic versions of cortisol that switch off inflammation of any cause: inhaled for asthma, as tablets or injections for flares of many diseases.
