    Simvastatin

    Also known as: Zocor

    Simvastatin is a statin, a medicine that lowers cholesterol by reducing how much the liver makes, and it is used to treat high cholesterol and to help prevent heart disease, including heart attacks and strokes. The NHS describes serious side effects as rare, but advises seeking help from a doctor for unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness, and a GP or NHS 111 is the route for prompt advice. Grapefruit juice, some antibiotics such as clarithromycin and several other medicines can raise simvastatin levels and the risk of muscle damage.

    What it is used for

    • Lowering cholesterol in people who have been diagnosed with high blood cholesterol
    • Helping to prevent heart disease, including heart attacks and strokes
    • A doctor may prescribe it when there is a family history of heart disease or a long-term condition such as rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes

    How it works in the body

    Simvastatin stops the liver from making cholesterol, which lowers the level of cholesterol in the blood. It belongs to the group of medicines called statins, also known as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors.

    Common side effects

    • Headache, dizziness, feeling sick, digestive problems, muscle aches and sleep problems are listed by the NHS as common side effects.
    • The NHS says unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness should be reported to a doctor.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS lists muscle damage, inflammation of the liver and inflammation of the pancreas among the serious side effects, which it describes as rare; unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, weakness or cramps need prompt advice from a GP or NHS 111.
    • Yellowing of the skin or eyes can point to liver problems, and a GP or NHS 111 should be contacted promptly.
    • A serious allergic reaction, with breathing or swallowing problems or a swollen face or throat, is an emergency, and the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E.

    Cautions and interactions

    • Grapefruit juice raises the level of simvastatin in the blood and makes side effects more likely, and the NHS advises avoiding it.
    • NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance lists clarithromycin as contraindicated with simvastatin and says that, if the antibiotic is essential, simvastatin is stopped for the course.
    • The NHS lists other medicines that raise the risk of muscle damage, including the antibiotics clarithromycin, rifampicin and fusidic acid, the antifungals fluconazole and ketoconazole, some HIV medicines, hepatitis C treatments, warfarin, ciclosporin and danazol.
    • If one of those medicines is needed, the NHS says the doctor may lower the simvastatin dose, switch to a different statin or pause simvastatin for a while.
    • The NHS says drinking a lot of alcohol may increase the chance of muscle and liver side effects.
    • The NHS lists liver or kidney problems, lung disease, an underactive thyroid, a muscle disorder and myasthenia gravis as reasons to check with a doctor before taking simvastatin.
    • The NHS says simvastatin is not recommended in pregnancy because it can affect how the baby develops, and it gives separate advice for people planning a pregnancy.
    • On breastfeeding, the NHS says simvastatin might be suitable but a person may be told to stop until they are no longer breastfeeding, and NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance prefers rosuvastatin, pravastatin or atorvastatin.
    • The NHS advises talking to a GP or pharmacist before stopping simvastatin, because stopping can make cholesterol rise again and the risk of heart attacks and strokes may go up.

    Other medicines

    Simvastatin + Amlodipine

    ModerateMonitoring or dose adjustment advised

    Amlodipine raises simvastatin blood levels, increasing the risk of muscle damage (myopathy and rhabdomyolysis).

    Why it can occur
    CYP inhibition · Amlodipine is a weak CYP3A4 inhibitor; simvastatin exposure roughly doubles
    What official information says
    UK information states the doctor may prescribe a lower dose of simvastatin (usually no more than 20 mg a day) for someone taking amlodipine.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official UK information: simvastatin dose limited to 20 mg daily with amlodipine; report unexplained muscle pain.
    What may be monitored
    Muscle symptoms · Creatine kinase if symptoms

    What is simvastatin used for?

    Simvastatin is a type of medicine called a statin. The NHS says it is used to lower cholesterol in people who have been diagnosed with high blood cholesterol. It is also used to help prevent heart disease, including heart attacks and strokes. A doctor may prescribe it when there is a family history of heart disease or a long-term condition such as rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes.

    Most adults can take simvastatin, and the NHS says it is sometimes prescribed to children aged 10 and over. It comes as tablets and as a liquid that is swallowed. In the UK, many simvastatin products are sold under the generic name, and the brand name Zocor also appears in the electronic Medicines Compendium. The NHS says it is available on prescription, and describes treatment as usually long term.

    How does simvastatin work?

    Simvastatin stops the liver from making cholesterol, which lowers the level of cholesterol in the blood. It belongs to the group of medicines also known as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors. MedlinePlus says it is used to reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke in people at high risk, and to decrease cholesterol.

    The NHS says it takes some weeks for cholesterol levels to fall.

    What are the side effects of simvastatin?

    The NHS lists headache, dizziness, feeling sick, digestive problems, muscle aches and sleep problems as common side effects. It describes the serious side effects as rare.

    The NHS lists muscle damage, inflammation of the liver and inflammation of the pancreas among the serious side effects. The NHS advises seeking help from a doctor for unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, weakness or cramps. A GP or NHS 111 is the route for prompt advice.

    Yellowing of the skin or eyes can point to liver problems, and a GP or NHS 111 should be contacted promptly. For breathing or swallowing problems, or a swollen face or throat, the NHS says to call 999 or go to A&E.

    Taking simvastatin with other medicines, grapefruit or alcohol

    The NHS says grapefruit juice increases the level of simvastatin in the blood and makes side effects more likely, so it advises avoiding it. Some medicines also raise the risk of serious side effects such as muscle damage. The NHS lists the antibiotics clarithromycin, rifampicin and fusidic acid, the antifungals fluconazole and ketoconazole, some HIV medicines, hepatitis C treatments, warfarin, ciclosporin and danazol.

    NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance lists clarithromycin as contraindicated with simvastatin. Where such a medicine is needed, the NHS says the doctor may lower the simvastatin dose, switch to a different statin or pause simvastatin for a while.

    The NHS says drinking a lot of alcohol may increase the chance of muscle and liver side effects. A pharmacist or doctor can check any new medicine against simvastatin.

    Who needs extra care, and what about pregnancy?

    The NHS lists liver or kidney problems, lung disease, an underactive thyroid, a muscle disorder and myasthenia gravis as reasons to check with a doctor before taking simvastatin.

    The NHS says simvastatin is not recommended in pregnancy because it can affect how the baby develops, and it gives separate advice for people planning a pregnancy. On breastfeeding, the NHS says simvastatin might be suitable but a person may be told to stop until they are no longer breastfeeding. NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance prefers rosuvastatin, pravastatin or atorvastatin.

    The NHS says that during the first 12 months of treatment a couple of routine tests are offered to check that the liver is working properly. It advises talking to a GP or pharmacist before stopping simvastatin, because stopping can make cholesterol rise again and the risk of heart attacks and strokes may go up.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I drink grapefruit juice while taking simvastatin?

    The NHS says grapefruit juice increases the level of simvastatin in the blood, which makes side effects more likely. It therefore advises avoiding grapefruit juice with simvastatin. A pharmacist or doctor can say how this applies to an individual.

    Can I take clarithromycin with simvastatin?

    NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service guidance lists clarithromycin as contraindicated with simvastatin. If the antibiotic is essential, the guidance is that simvastatin is stopped for the course. The prescriber decides what happens in each case, so the doctor or pharmacist should be told about any antibiotic.

    What should I do about muscle pain on simvastatin?

    The NHS advises seeking help from a doctor for unexplained muscle pain, tenderness, weakness or cramps. A GP appointment or NHS 111 is the route. The NHS advises talking to a GP or pharmacist before stopping simvastatin.

    Can simvastatin be taken in pregnancy?

    The NHS says simvastatin is not recommended in pregnancy because it can affect how the baby develops. It gives separate advice for people planning a pregnancy. On breastfeeding, the NHS says simvastatin might be suitable but a person may be told to stop, and a doctor may suggest a different statin.

    Are blood tests needed with simvastatin?

    The NHS says that during the first 12 months of treatment a couple of routine tests are offered to check that the liver is working properly. Yellowing of the skin or eyes can point to liver problems and should be reported to a GP or NHS 111 promptly.

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Stroke Sudden loss of brain function because blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, by a blocked artery (ischaemic.
    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.

    Symptoms

    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Lipid profile Measures the fats carried in blood: total cholesterol, LDL ('bad', deposited in artery walls), HDL ('good'.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.

    Medications

    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.

    Drug classes

    • Statins The main cholesterol-lowering medicines.
