    Metoprolol and beta blockers

    Also known as: beta blocker, bisoprolol, atenolol, propranolol, carvedilol

    A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks, in heart failure and to control fast heart rhythms.

    Generic name
    Metoprolol and beta blockers
    Drug class
    Beta blockers
    Brand names
    UK Lopresor · US Lopressor, Toprol-XL
    Also known as
    beta blocker, bisoprolol, atenolol, propranolol, carvedilol
    Forms
    Tablets (metoprolol 25-100 mg twice daily or modified release once daily; bisoprolol 1.25-10 mg once daily); intravenous for rapid rate control
    Onset and duration
    Heart rate falls within an hour; heart failure benefit builds over months.
    Monitoring
    Pulse and blood pressure; ECG if the rate falls below 50 or symptoms occur.

    What it is used for

    • Angina and after a heart attack (reduce the heart's oxygen demand and the risk of dangerous rhythms)
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (bisoprolol, carvedilol: improve survival when started low and slow)
    • Controlling the rate in atrial fibrillation and other fast rhythms
    • High blood pressure (no longer first line unless another indication)
    • Propranolol for migraine prevention, tremor, anxiety symptoms and thyroid storm

    How it works in the body

    Occupies beta-1 receptors on heart muscle and the conducting system so adrenaline and noradrenaline cannot stimulate them. The heart beats more slowly and less forcefully, uses less oxygen, and the AV node lets fewer atrial impulses through. Non-selective agents (propranolol) also block beta-2 receptors in airways and blood vessels, which is why they can trigger asthma.

    Common side effects

    • Tiredness, cold hands and feet, slow pulse
    • Dizziness on standing, vivid dreams, sleep disturbance
    • Erectile dysfunction
    • Masking the warning signs of low blood sugar in diabetes

    Serious: seek help

    • Severe bradycardia or heart block
    • Worsening of heart failure if started at too high a dose
    • Bronchospasm in asthma (especially non-selective agents)
    • Rebound angina, fast rhythms or blood pressure surge if stopped abruptly

    Cautions and interactions

    • Never stop suddenly: taper over one to two weeks
    • Asthma (relative contraindication), severe peripheral arterial disease, uncontrolled heart failure, second- or third-degree heart block
    • Caution with verapamil and diltiazem (additive slowing), and in diabetes
