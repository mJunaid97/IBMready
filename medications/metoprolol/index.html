    Metoprolol and beta blockers

    A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks, in heart failure and to control fast heart rhythms. It acts mainly on the heart rather than the airways and blood vessels, so it causes fewer of the side effects seen with older non-selective beta blockers; its main effects are tiredness, dizziness and cold hands, and the one rule every user learns is that it is never stopped suddenly.

    Onset and duration
    Heart rate falls within an hour; heart failure benefit builds over months. The NHS notes it starts working on the day it is taken but can take up to a week for the full effect on blood pressure.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 6918 · ATC C07AB02 · FDA EPC beta-Adrenergic Blocker [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Pulse and blood pressure; ECG if the rate falls below 50 or symptoms occur. Monitoring is otherwise clinical: in heart failure, weight, breathlessness and ankle swelling are watched as the dose is built up, in diabetes glucose readings replace the blunted warning symptoms, and people are told to report fainting, a very slow or irregular heartbeat or worsening breathlessness between reviews rather than wait for them.

    What it is used for

    • Angina and after a heart attack (reduce the heart's oxygen demand and the risk of dangerous rhythms); US information states it can improve survival after a heart attack
    • Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (bisoprolol, carvedilol: improve survival when started low and slow); the modified-release metoprolol succinate form also has proven survival benefit in heart failure
    • Controlling the rate in atrial fibrillation and other fast rhythms
    • High blood pressure (no longer first line unless another indication): it lowers blood pressure as well as other beta blockers
    • Propranolol for migraine prevention, tremor, anxiety symptoms and thyroid storm; metoprolol is also used for migraine prevention

    How it works in the body

    Occupies beta-1 receptors on heart muscle and the conducting system so adrenaline and noradrenaline cannot stimulate them. The heart beats more slowly and less forcefully, uses less oxygen, and the AV node lets fewer atrial impulses through. Non-selective agents (propranolol) also block beta-2 receptors in airways and blood vessels, which is why they can trigger asthma. The NHS describes the effect for the reader: beta blockers change how the body responds to some nerve impulses, especially in the heart, blocking the messages the heart and blood vessels would otherwise receive, so the heart beats more slowly and with less force and finds it easier to pump blood around the body. Because metoprolol works mainly on the heart, it is less likely than non-selective beta blockers to cause some side effects, although it still carries a risk of bronchospasm in people with asthma. The slowing of the heart begins the same day, but the full effect on blood pressure builds over about a week, and not feeling any different does not mean it is not working. The same receptor blockade blunts the body's adrenaline response to low blood sugar, which is why people with diabetes may notice fewer warning signs of a hypo, and it is why stopping abruptly after regular use lets the heart over-react to its own adrenaline, with the risk of rebound angina, fast rhythms or a surge in blood pressure; the dose is reduced gradually instead.

    Common side effects

    • Tiredness, cold hands and feet, slow pulse
    • Dizziness on standing, light-headedness, headaches and feeling sick, most likely when first starting or after a dose increase; the NHS says these are usually mild, improve as the body gets used to the medicine, and driving, cycling and using machinery are avoided while they last
    • Vivid dreams, sleep disturbance; a feeling of breathlessness
    • Erectile dysfunction and reduced sex drive are reported, although the NHS notes there is not enough evidence to say metoprolol definitely causes them
    • Masking the warning signs of low blood sugar in diabetes; US information adds diarrhoea, dry mouth and rash or itching
    • Vomiting or severe diarrhoea while taking it can stop the contraceptive pill working properly

    Serious: seek help

    • Severe bradycardia or heart block: a slow, irregular or pounding heartbeat, or fainting, needs urgent assessment
    • Worsening of heart failure if started at too high a dose: shortness of breath with a cough that gets worse on exertion, swollen ankles or legs, or sudden weight gain are the warning signs the NHS and US information list
    • Bronchospasm in asthma (especially non-selective agents)
    • Rebound angina, fast rhythms or blood pressure surge if stopped abruptly; US information warns that sudden stopping can cause serious heart problems
    • Overdose: nausea, vomiting, reduced consciousness, a very slow or irregular heartbeat, chest pain and difficulty breathing

    Cautions and interactions

    • Never stop suddenly: taper over one to two weeks
    • Asthma (relative contraindication), severe peripheral arterial disease, uncontrolled heart failure, second- or third-degree heart block; StatPearls lists severe bradycardia, heart block and decompensated heart failure as contraindications
    • Caution with verapamil and diltiazem (additive slowing), and in diabetes, where it can both raise the risk of low blood sugar and hide its warning signs
    • Interacting medicines include clonidine (stopping clonidine abruptly while on a beta blocker can cause rebound high blood pressure), digoxin (additive slowing, with digoxin levels checked) and amiodarone, which raises metoprolol levels
    • The anaesthetist is told before any operation under general anaesthetic, because metoprolol can lower blood pressure too far with some anaesthetics
    • Modified-release tablets and capsules have their own swallowing instructions and are not crushed or split without checking the label; US labelling for the extended-release capsule advises avoiding alcohol
    • Thyroid disease, liver disease, pregnancy and breastfeeding are mentioned to the prescriber; long-term use is regarded as safe and the medicine works best when taken for a long time

    Conditions

    • Coronary artery disease Narrowing of the coronary arteries by fatty plaque (atherosclerosis), limiting blood flow to the heart muscle.
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Migraine A recurrent headache disorder with attacks of moderate to severe throbbing pain, typically one-sided, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medications

    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Glyceryl trinitrate A fast-acting nitrate spray or tablet under the tongue that widens veins and coronary arteries to relieve an angina attack within minutes.
    • Apixaban and DOACs A direct oral anticoagulant that blocks clotting factor Xa, preventing strokes in atrial fibrillation and treating deep vein thrombosis and…
    • Bisoprolol A once-daily beta blocker that selectively slows the heart and lowers its workload, used for heart failure, angina.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Levothyroxine Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Losartan An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes.
    • Propranolol Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines.
