    Allopurinol

    A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood, taken every day to prevent gout attacks and kidney stones rather than to treat an acute attack. It takes weeks to months to show its benefit, attacks can briefly become more frequent after starting, and once established it is considered safe to take for many years.

    Usual preparations
    Tablets 100 and 300 mg once daily after food, starting at 100 mg (50 mg in kidney disease) and titrating up to 900 mg (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Urate falls within days, but attacks can increase for the first 3-6 months as crystals dissolve, so colchicine or an NSAID is given as cover. The NHS notes that it may take several weeks for the uric acid level to fall far enough, and US information that the full benefit can take months; the medicine is continued even when the person feels well.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 519 · ATC M04AA01 · FDA EPC Xanthine Oxidase Inhibitor [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Serum urate every 4 weeks during titration, then yearly; kidney and liver function. The NHS describes regular blood tests to check the uric acid level and closer monitoring for people with kidney or liver disease; new rashes are reported immediately rather than watched. Long-term treatment is regarded as very safe, so monitoring is about confirming the urate target and kidney function rather than screening for cumulative toxicity.

    What it is used for

    • Preventing gout attacks by lowering blood uric acid (started after the first attack settles, especially with repeated attacks, tophi, kidney stones or kidney disease)
    • Preventing uric acid kidney stones, including recurrent stones in people whose urine carries a lot of uric acid
    • Preventing tumour lysis syndrome during chemotherapy: some cancer treatments release large amounts of uric acid as cells break down, so allopurinol is started before the treatment begins; an intravenous form exists for people who cannot swallow tablets

    How it works in the body

    Blocks xanthine oxidase, the enzyme that converts hypoxanthine and xanthine into uric acid at the end of purine breakdown. Blood urate falls below its saturation point, so no new crystals form and existing deposits in joints and tophi slowly dissolve over months. The dose is increased every few weeks until the urate target (below 360, or 300 micromol/L with tophi) is reached. The NHS explains the consequence for the reader: uric acid that cannot stay dissolved forms crystals, in joints (causing the swollen, painful attacks of gout) and in the kidneys (causing damage and stones), and allopurinol works by reducing the amount of uric acid the body's cells make, so that these crystals stop building up. Because existing crystals take time to dissolve, it may take several weeks for the uric acid level to fall and months for the full benefit to appear, and during that time attacks can paradoxically become more frequent; this is why it is started at a low dose, increased gradually, and usually covered with another medicine in the early months. It prevents attacks but does nothing to relieve an attack that has already started.

    Common side effects

    • Rash or other skin reaction: the NHS advises stopping allopurinol and contacting a doctor straight away, because a rash can be the first sign of a serious skin reaction
    • Feeling sick, being sick, diarrhoea
    • Drowsiness, dizziness, sleepiness or problems with coordination, so driving is avoided until they pass
    • Gout flares in the first months of treatment

    Serious: seek help

    • Allopurinol hypersensitivity syndrome: severe skin reactions (Stevens-Johnson, toxic epidermal necrolysis) with liver and kidney involvement, rare but life-threatening, more likely in those with the HLA-B*58:01 gene (common in Han Chinese, Thai and Korean populations, who are screened), kidney disease and high starting doses; blistering or peeling skin is the warning sign
    • Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) with swelling of the throat or tongue or difficulty breathing
    • Bone marrow suppression (a serious fall in white blood cells, which may show as unusual bruising with a high temperature or repeated infections), liver injury (rare)

    Cautions and interactions

    • Do not start during an acute attack; do not stop during an attack once established. Stopping allopurinol lets uric acid rise again and gout attacks may return, so it is not stopped without talking to the prescriber; a rash or other signs of a serious reaction need urgent medical advice
    • Interactions: azathioprine and mercaptopurine (levels rise dangerously), warfarin, ACE inhibitors and thiazides (hypersensitivity risk)
    • Drink plenty of fluid; reduce dose in kidney impairment. People with kidney or liver disease are started on a lower dose and monitored more closely
    • Started at a low dose and increased gradually, because raising the dose too quickly increases the risk of serious skin reactions
    • Febuxostat is the alternative if allopurinol is not tolerated, with cardiovascular caution

    Conditions

    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).

    Symptoms

    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Knee pain Pain in or around the knee, the body's largest joint, where the femur, tibia and patella meet.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.

    Medical tests

    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Liver function tests A panel of enzymes and proteins that together show whether liver cells are injured, whether bile is flowing.
    • Genetic testing Reading part or all of a person's DNA (from blood or saliva) or a tumour's DNA (from a biopsy) to find variants that cause disease, raise risk.

    Procedures

    • Joint aspiration and injection A needle drawn into a swollen joint removes fluid for analysis and relief of pressure, and may be followed by a corticosteroid injection.

    Medications

    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Furosemide A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure.
    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.

    Drug classes

    • Xanthine oxidase inhibitors Medicines that lower uric acid by blocking the enzyme that makes it, dissolving the crystals that cause gout attacks and tophi when taken every day.
