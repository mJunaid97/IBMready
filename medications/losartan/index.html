    Losartan

    An angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) that relaxes blood vessels to lower blood pressure and protect the kidneys in diabetes, an alternative for people who cough on ACE inhibitors.

    How it works

    Blocks the AT1 receptor that angiotensin II uses to constrict arteries, release aldosterone from the adrenal glands and thicken heart and vessel walls. With the receptor occupied, arteries relax, pressure falls, and the heart and the kidney's filters are unloaded. Because the converting enzyme is untouched, bradykinin does not build up, so the dry cough of ACE inhibitors is rare.

    Mechanism of action

    Losartan and its more potent active metabolite (EXP3174) are selective, competitive antagonists at the angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor. Blockade prevents angiotensin II-mediated vasoconstriction, aldosterone secretion, sodium reabsorption, sympathetic activation and the trophic effects on cardiac and vascular tissue. Efferent arteriolar dilatation lowers intraglomerular pressure, which underlies the reduction in proteinuria and slowing of diabetic nephropathy but also the rise in creatinine when renal perfusion depends on angiotensin II (renal artery stenosis, dehydration, NSAIDs). Because ACE is not inhibited, bradykinin is not accumulated and cough and angioedema are much less frequent than with ACE inhibitors. Losartan also has a mild uricosuric effect not shared by other ARBs.

    Common

    Serious safety information

    • Boxed warning (US). US labelling carries a boxed warning on fetal toxicity: drugs that act on the renin-angiotensin system can cause injury and death to the developing fetus, and losartan should be discontinued as soon as pregnancy is detected. US
    • Special warning. Angioedema (swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat) is a rare but serious reaction that needs emergency treatment; it is less common than with ACE inhibitors but still possible. UK

    Warnings and precautions

    • Pregnancy. UK information states losartan is not recommended in pregnancy or when trying to conceive, because it can harm the baby, particularly in the second and third trimesters; a different blood-pressure medicine is used. UK
    • Kidney. Losartan can reduce kidney function and raise potassium, particularly with dehydration, NSAIDs, potassium supplements, potassium-sparing diuretics or narrowed renal arteries; kidney function and potassium are checked before treatment and after dose changes. UK
    • Precaution. Sick-day guidance: when vomiting, diarrhoea or fever cause dehydration, UK information advises that losartan may need to be paused temporarily to protect the kidneys, restarting once eating and drinking normally. UK
    • Driving. Dizziness on standing is common at the start; do not drive or use machinery if affected. UK

    Special populations

    Pregnancy
    Not recommended at any stage; contraindicated in the second and third trimesters. Women planning pregnancy are switched to a safer antihypertensive, and losartan is stopped as soon as pregnancy is confirmed. UK
    Breastfeeding
    UK information advises against losartan while breastfeeding, particularly for premature or newborn babies, because little is known about the amounts in milk; other medicines with more safety data are preferred. UK
    Children
    Licensed in the UK for hypertension in children aged 6 years and over at weight-based doses under specialist supervision. UK
    Older adults
    A lower starting dose (25 mg) is used over 75 years and with intravascular depletion; older adults are more prone to hypotension and to potassium and creatinine changes. UK
    Kidney impairment
    No dose reduction is needed for mild to moderate impairment, but kidney function and potassium must be monitored more closely; in renal artery stenosis and advanced disease losartan can precipitate acute kidney injury. UK

    A lower dose (25 mg once daily) is used in mild to moderate hepatic impairment; avoid in severe impairment. UK

    Effects on tests and results

    • Creatinine: A small rise in serum creatinine and fall in eGFR is expected when losartan is started; a rise of more than about 30% prompts investigation. UK
    • Potassium: Serum potassium rises because aldosterone release is reduced; hyperkalaemia is more likely with kidney disease, potassium supplements, spironolactone or NSAIDs. UK
    • Losartan modestly lowers serum urate (a uricosuric effect not shared by other ARBs). UK
    • Urine protein: Urine albumin excretion falls in diabetic kidney disease; this is the intended effect and is followed with the albumin-to-creatinine ratio. UK

    What is losartan used for?

    Losartan is a medicine for high blood pressure, and it belongs to the group called angiotensin II receptor blockers. The NHS says it can also be used to treat heart failure and for kidney problems in people with diabetes, where it helps to protect the kidneys. It is available on prescription only.

    How does losartan work and how is it taken?

    Losartan blocks the effect of a hormone called angiotensin II, which narrows blood vessels. The NHS says that, like other medicines in this group, losartan relaxes and widens the blood vessels, which lowers blood pressure and makes it easier for the heart to pump blood around the body.

    The NHS says losartan can be taken with or without food, and that it should not be stopped unless a doctor says so, even when a person feels well.

    What are the side effects of losartan?

    The NHS lists dizziness, tiredness, headache, tummy pain, feeling or being sick, and swelling of the legs, ankles or feet as common side effects. It says losartan can make people feel dizzy, especially when they first start taking it or after a bigger dose, and that anyone who feels dizzy should not drive, ride a bike, or use tools or machinery until they feel better.

    Taking losartan for a long time can sometimes mean the kidneys do not work as well as they should, so the doctor checks kidney function with regular blood tests. The NHS says yellow skin or yellow whites of the eyes may be a sign of liver problems, and that this can be harder to see on brown or black skin. For this, the NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away. It also lists feeling weak, an irregular heartbeat, pins and needles and muscle cramps as possible signs of changes in blood potassium, with the same advice to call a doctor or NHS 111 straight away.

    What are the signs of a serious allergic reaction or swelling?

    A serious allergic reaction to losartan is rare. The NHS says to call 999 if the lips, mouth, throat or tongue suddenly swell, or if breathing becomes difficult, and not to drive to A&E. A raised, itchy rash can also be part of an allergic reaction. Sudden swelling of parts of the body is called angioedema, and the NHS lists a past episode of it as a reason losartan may not be suitable.

    Can losartan be taken with other medicines, alcohol or potassium salt substitutes?

    The NHS lists painkillers such as ibuprofen, naproxen and diclofenac among the medicines that do not mix well with losartan. It also lists other blood pressure medicines, diuretics, lithium and heparin. A pharmacist or doctor can be asked about any combination.

    The NHS advises against potassium-rich salt substitutes such as LoSalt, because they may push the level of potassium in the blood too high when mixed with losartan. Alcohol can add to the blood pressure lowering effect of losartan and may bring on dizziness or light-headedness. The NHS advises limiting alcohol while taking it, because alcohol can increase the risk of side effects.

    Who needs extra care with losartan, including in pregnancy and during illness?

    The NHS says losartan is not recommended in pregnancy, because it can reduce the fluid around the baby, particularly in the second and third trimesters. It advises women who could become pregnant to use contraception while taking it and to plan any pregnancy with their doctor. For breastfeeding, the NHS says to talk to a doctor or pharmacist first.

    The NHS says losartan may not be suitable for people with a history of angioedema or with liver problems. It also says vomiting or diarrhoea can cause dehydration, and that a doctor or pharmacist may recommend stopping losartan until the person is better.

    Frequently asked questions

    What are the common side effects of losartan?

    The common side effects the NHS lists for losartan are dizziness, tiredness, headache, tummy pain, feeling or being sick, and swelling of the legs, ankles or feet.

    What should happen if the lips or tongue swell while taking losartan?

    Sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue is treated as an emergency. The NHS says to call 999, and not to drive to A&E oneself. Past angioedema, meaning earlier sudden swelling of this kind, is also a reason losartan may not be suitable for a person.

    Can painkillers like ibuprofen be taken with losartan?

    The NHS lists painkillers such as ibuprofen, naproxen and diclofenac among the medicines that do not mix well with losartan. It also lists other blood pressure medicines among those that can interact.

    Does alcohol affect losartan?

    Alcohol can add to the blood pressure lowering effect of losartan. That may lead to dizziness or light-headedness. During the first few days of taking it, or after a bigger dose, the NHS says it is best to avoid alcohol until it is clear how the medicine affects the person.

    Biomarkers

    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.

    Medications

    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Spironolactone Spironolactone is a diuretic, a medicine that helps reduce fluid build-up in the body.

    Drug classes

    • Angiotensin receptor blockers Medicines ending in -sartan that block the receptor angiotensin II acts on.

    Guides and news

    • ACE inhibitors, ARBs and amlodipine compared ARBs work much like ACE inhibitors but do not typically cause a dry cough; amlodipine relaxes artery walls and can cause ankle swelling.
    • NIH-funded study: losartan and brown fat An NIH-funded study reports that losartan, a blood pressure medicine, raised brown fat activity in mice and cells.
