    Amoxicillin

    A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus, dental and some urinary infections and as part of Helicobacter pylori treatment; without effect on viruses and not used in people with a history of true penicillin allergy.

    How it works

    Binds the penicillin-binding proteins that bacteria use to cross-link the peptidoglycan of their cell wall. Growing bacteria with a weakened wall burst under their own osmotic pressure. Human cells have no cell wall, which is why penicillins are so selective. Bacteria fight back with beta-lactamase enzymes that destroy the drug; clavulanic acid (in co-amoxiclav) blocks that enzyme. Antibiotics do nothing against viruses, the cause of most sore throats, colds and coughs.

    Mechanism of action

    Amoxicillin is an aminopenicillin, a beta-lactam that binds and inactivates penicillin-binding proteins (transpeptidases) in the bacterial cytoplasmic membrane, preventing cross-linking of peptidoglycan during cell-wall synthesis; autolytic enzymes then lyse the growing cell, so the effect is bactericidal and time-dependent (efficacy depends on the time the concentration stays above the MIC, hence three-times-daily dosing). Its spectrum covers streptococci including most pneumococci, many enterococci, Listeria, Haemophilus (non-beta-lactamase producers), Helicobacter pylori and some Enterobacterales, but it is destroyed by beta-lactamases; clavulanic acid in co-amoxiclav inhibits many of them. Amoxicillin is better absorbed orally than ampicillin and is excreted largely unchanged by the kidney, so doses are reduced in severe renal impairment and levels are raised by probenecid.

    Pathway

    1. Amoxicillin
    2. Penicillin (beta-lactam antibiotic)
    3. Penicillin-binding proteins
    4. Bacterial cell wall (peptidoglycan)
    5. Cross-linking blocked; the wall weakens
    6. Growing bacteria lyse
    7. Infection cleared in the lungs, ears, sinuses, teeth, urinary tract
    8. Pneumonia, otitis media, sinusitis, H. pylori

    Side effects

    Common

    • Nausea, diarrhoea, thrush
    • Non-allergic rash (very common in glandular fever, where amoxicillin should be avoided)

    Serious: seek help

    • Anaphylaxis in true penicillin allergy (about 1 in 10,000 courses); most reported 'penicillin allergies' are not true allergy and can be safely delabelled
    • Clostridioides difficile colitis, especially with broad-spectrum agents in hospital
    • Liver injury with co-amoxiclav (cholestatic hepatitis), seizures at very high doses in kidney failure
    • Antimicrobial resistance across the population with every unnecessary course

    Source: NHS: Amoxicillin. Frequencies follow the source's categories; no percentages are invented.

    Serious safety information

    • Special warning. Allergy: anaphylaxis occurs in a small minority of people with true penicillin allergy; UK information advises stopping and seeking emergency help for swelling of the face, lips or throat, wheeze, tight chest or a widespread itchy rash. A previous serious reaction to any penicillin is a contraindication. UK
    • Special warning. Rash in glandular fever: a widespread non-allergic rash is very common when amoxicillin is given during infectious mononucleosis (and with some leukaemias), so it is avoided when glandular fever is suspected. UK
    • Special warning. Antibiotic-associated colitis: severe or persistent diarrhoea during or after treatment may be Clostridioides difficile infection, particularly in hospital and in older people. UK

    Warnings and precautions

    • Kidney. Doses are reduced in severe renal impairment (eGFR below 30 for high doses, below 10 for standard doses) because amoxicillin is excreted by the kidney; crystalluria can occur with high doses and poor hydration. UK
    • Other. Antimicrobial resistance: every unnecessary course selects resistant bacteria; the drug does not treat viral sore throats, colds or most coughs, and the course is completed as prescribed. GLOBAL

    Effects on tests and results

    • C-reactive protein (CRP): CRP and the white-cell count fall as a bacterial infection responds; used to judge treatment. UK
    • Urinalysis: Amoxicillin can cause a false-positive urine glucose result with copper-reduction tests (not with enzyme-based dipsticks). US
    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase): Co-amoxiclav (amoxicillin with clavulanic acid) can raise liver enzymes and bilirubin (cholestatic hepatitis), sometimes after the course has finished. UK
    • Warfarin: amoxicillin can raise the INR in some people by altering gut flora and vitamin K; extra INR checks are advised. UK

    What is amoxicillin used for?

    Amoxicillin is a penicillin antibiotic, a medicine that treats infections caused by bacteria. The NHS says it is used for chest infections, including pneumonia, and for dental abscesses. It is often prescribed for children with ear infections. The NHS also says it can be used with other medicines for stomach ulcers.

    In the UK it needs a prescription. It comes as capsules or a liquid, and the NHS says injections are usually given only in hospital. The NHS says most adults and children can take amoxicillin. For most infections, the NHS says people should feel better within a few days.

    Antibiotics only work on infections caused by bacteria. They have no effect on viral illnesses such as colds and flu.

    How does amoxicillin work?

    Amoxicillin belongs to the penicillin group of antibiotics. Penicillin antibiotics kill susceptible bacteria by blocking an enzyme that completes the building of the bacterial cell wall.

    The NHS says to tell a doctor if there is no improvement after a few days, or if symptoms get worse at any point. NHS Shropshire, Telford and Wrekin advises taking antibiotics exactly as prescribed, including the full course, and never saving them for later or sharing them with others.

    Unnecessary use of antibiotics can cause some bacteria to become resistant to them, which is called antimicrobial resistance. A practice page adds that routine procedures such as surgery, cancer treatment and organ transplants become riskier as antibiotics lose their effect.

    What are the side effects of amoxicillin?

    The NHS lists feeling sick and diarrhoea as the most common side effects. It also says thrush can occur after antibiotics.

    The NHS says to call a doctor or NHS 111 now for diarrhoea that is severe or that contains blood or mucus. It lists signs of liver problems among the serious effects, such as yellowing of the skin or eyes, and pale poo with dark urine. Some of these serious effects can appear up to two months after the course has finished.

    Delayed allergic reactions can happen several days after taking amoxicillin capsules. The NHS says these are usually a mild skin rash, though a high temperature, joint pain and swollen glands can also occur.

    Allergy and who needs extra care with amoxicillin

    In rare cases, amoxicillin can cause a serious allergic reaction called anaphylaxis. The NHS says signs include swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat and difficulty breathing, and that anaphylaxis needs emergency treatment. In the UK, 999 is the number to call in an emergency.

    The NHS lists a past allergic reaction to amoxicillin, penicillin or another medicine among the things to tell a doctor about before taking amoxicillin. The UK product information says a doctor should ask carefully about earlier allergic reactions to penicillins, cephalosporins or other related antibiotics before treatment starts.

    The NHS says to check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking amoxicillin. It advises telling a doctor first about liver or kidney problems, upcoming blood or urine tests, and recent or upcoming vaccinations. Glandular fever is also on that list, because amoxicillin can cause a rash in people who have it.

    The NHS says amoxicillin can be taken during pregnancy, while advising a check with a pharmacist or doctor first. It also says amoxicillin can be taken while breastfeeding, because only tiny amounts get into breast milk.

    Taking amoxicillin with other medicines or alcohol

    The NHS says alcohol can be drunk while taking amoxicillin. UK product information lists some medicines that can interact with amoxicillin. Allopurinol may make allergic skin reactions more likely. Probenecid reduces the kidneys' removal of amoxicillin, which can raise and prolong its levels in the blood, and the product information says using the two together is not recommended. Blood thinners such as warfarin may call for extra blood tests. Methotrexate may increase side effects, and other antibiotics such as tetracycline may make amoxicillin work less well.

    The NHS says amoxicillin does not stop any type of contraception working, including the combined pill, the progestogen-only pill and emergency contraception. However, being sick or having severe or long-lasting diarrhoea may stop contraceptive pills protecting against pregnancy. The NHS says amoxicillin should not affect the ability to drive or cycle.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can alcohol be drunk while taking amoxicillin?

    The NHS says alcohol can be drunk while taking amoxicillin. It also says amoxicillin should not affect the ability to drive or cycle. The most common side effects the NHS lists are feeling sick and diarrhoea, and the NHS says thrush can occur after antibiotics. These answers come from the NHS page about amoxicillin itself.

    Does amoxicillin work for colds and flu?

    No, antibiotics only work on infections caused by bacteria. Colds and flu are viral illnesses, and antibiotics have no effect on them. The NHS says GPs do not recommend antibiotics for colds and flu because they will not relieve symptoms or speed up recovery. Unnecessary use of antibiotics can cause some bacteria to become resistant to them, which is called antimicrobial resistance.

    What if amoxicillin does not seem to help?

    The NHS says to tell a doctor if there is no improvement after a few days, or if symptoms get worse at any point. For most infections, the NHS says people should feel better within a few days. NHS Shropshire, Telford and Wrekin advises taking antibiotics exactly as prescribed, including the full course.

    Does amoxicillin affect the contraceptive pill?

    The NHS says amoxicillin does not stop any type of contraception working, including the combined pill, the progestogen-only pill and emergency contraception. The exception is when it makes someone sick or causes severe or long-lasting diarrhoea, because contraceptive pills may then not protect against pregnancy.

    What can cause a rash during amoxicillin treatment?

    The NHS says amoxicillin can cause a rash in people with glandular fever. Delayed allergic reactions can happen several days after taking amoxicillin capsules and are usually a mild skin rash, though a high temperature, joint pain and swollen glands can also occur. Swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, or difficulty breathing, may be anaphylaxis, which needs emergency treatment: call 999.

    Conditions

    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.
    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Peptic ulcer A break in the lining of the stomach or duodenum that extends through the mucosa, caused by acid acting on a weakened defence.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Cough A reflex blast of air that clears the airways of mucus, irritants and foreign material.
    • Rash Any change in the colour or texture of the skin.

    Medical tests

    • Microbiology tests Tests that find the organism causing an infection: growing it on culture, detecting its genes by PCR, its proteins by antigen tests.
    • Urine culture A urine sample is spread on culture plates and incubated for 24-48 hours to grow any bacteria, identify them and test which antibiotics kill them.
    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Urinalysis A plastic strip with chemical pads dipped into a fresh urine sample.

    Biomarkers

    • C-reactive protein (CRP) A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.
    • Creatinine A waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a steady rate and cleared almost entirely by the kidneys.
    • ALP (alkaline phosphatase) An enzyme made mainly by the cells lining the bile ducts and by bone-forming cells.
    • ALT (alanine aminotransferase) An enzyme concentrated in liver cells that leaks into blood when they are injured.

    Procedures

    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.
    • Cholecystectomy Keyhole removal of the gallbladder is the usual treatment for gallstones that cause pain or complications; the liver keeps making bile.
    • Urinary catheterisation A soft tube passed along the urethra (or through the lower abdomen) into the bladder drains urine when the bladder cannot empty or output must be…
    • Gastroscopy A flexible camera passed through the mouth examines the oesophagus, stomach and duodenum, allowing biopsies and treatment.

    Medications

    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Cetirizine and antihistamines A second-generation (less sedating) antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.
    • Clarithromycin A macrolide antibiotic for chest, skin, ear and throat infections and part of the combination that eradicates Helicobacter pylori.
    • Co-amoxiclav (Augmentin) A penicillin antibiotic that combines amoxicillin with clavulanic acid, used for bacterial infections such as those of the ears, lungs, sinuses.
    • Flucloxacillin A penicillin-type antibiotic, available on prescription, used for infections of the skin and wounds, bone infections and ear infections.
    • Nitrofurantoin Nitrofurantoin is an antibiotic that the NHS describes as a medicine for lower urinary tract infections such as cystitis.
    • Trimethoprim Trimethoprim is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections such as cystitis, and occasionally other infections.

    Drug classes

    • Penicillins The oldest family of antibiotics and still the first choice for many chest, ear, throat, skin and dental infections: amoxicillin, co-amoxiclav.
