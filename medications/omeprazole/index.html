    Omeprazole

    A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach, healing ulcers and reflux oesophagitis and protecting the stomach from anti-inflammatory drugs.

    How it works
    Omeprazole switches off the acid pumps in the cells of your stomach lining, so far less acid is made. With less acid, ulcers can heal, the oesophagus is no longer burned by reflux, and the stomach is protected from anti-inflammatory painkillers.

    How it works

    Irreversibly blocks the hydrogen-potassium ATPase, the 'proton pump' in the parietal cells of the stomach lining that secretes acid as the final step of every stimulus. Because the pump is disabled rather than the signals, acid falls by over 90%, and new pumps must be made before secretion recovers, so the effect outlasts the drug's short half-life. Given 30-60 minutes before breakfast, when pumps are most active.

    Mechanism of action

    Omeprazole is a substituted benzimidazole prodrug. Absorbed from gastro-resistant granules, it concentrates in the acidic secretory canaliculi of gastric parietal cells, where it is protonated and converted to a sulfenamide that forms covalent disulphide bonds with cysteines on the H+/K+ ATPase, inhibiting the pump irreversibly. Because the pump is the final step of acid secretion, basal and stimulated acid output fall by more than 90%; secretion recovers only as new pumps are synthesised (half-life of pump turnover about 50 hours), so once-daily dosing before breakfast gives a sustained effect despite a plasma half-life of about an hour. Omeprazole is metabolised by CYP2C19 and CYP3A4 and inhibits CYP2C19, reducing activation of clopidogrel and raising levels of some other drugs. Long-term acid suppression raises gastrin, alters absorption of magnesium, calcium, iron and vitamin B12, and changes the gut microbiome.

    Side effects

    Common

    • Headache, nausea, diarrhoea, constipation, abdominal pain
    • Wind, dizziness

    Serious: seek help

    • Low magnesium with long-term use (cramps, arrhythmia)
    • Clostridioides difficile and other gut infections, and pneumonia (acid normally kills swallowed microbes)
    • Reduced absorption of B12, iron and calcium; a modest increase in fractures with prolonged high-dose use
    • Acute interstitial nephritis and, rarely, subacute cutaneous lupus

    Serious safety information

    • Special warning. Masking of gastric cancer: PPIs relieve symptoms of stomach cancer without treating it; alarm features (weight loss, difficulty swallowing, vomiting, bleeding, anaemia, a new symptom over 55) need endoscopy before or soon after starting. UK
    • Special warning. Fracture risk: high doses for over a year modestly increase the risk of hip, wrist and spine fractures, mainly in older people and those with other risk factors. UK
    • Special warning. Infections: reduced stomach acid increases the risk of gut infections including Clostridioides difficile and, possibly, pneumonia; persistent diarrhoea on a PPI needs assessment. UK

    Warnings and precautions

    • Monitoring. Low magnesium: long-term use (usually over a year) can cause hypomagnesaemia with cramps, palpitations, dizziness or seizures; magnesium is checked before prolonged treatment and periodically, particularly with diuretics or digoxin. UK
    • Other. Subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus: a rare skin reaction to PPIs, presenting as a rash in sun-exposed skin with joint pain; the drug is stopped. UK
    • Precaution. Review of long-term use: rebound acid hypersecretion for a few weeks after stopping can be mistaken for relapse; the dose is stepped down or used on demand where possible. UK

    Effects on tests and results

    • Electrolytes: Magnesium can fall with long-term use; checked before and during prolonged treatment. UK
    • Ferritin: Vitamin B12 absorption falls (acid is needed to release B12 from food), and iron absorption is reduced; ferritin and haemoglobin may fall over years of use. UK
    • Serum gastrin rises (a normal response to acid suppression) and can confound investigations for gastrinoma; chromogranin A also rises. UK
    • Microbiology tests: Urea breath tests and stool antigen tests for H. pylori give false negatives on a PPI; it is stopped for at least 2 weeks before testing. UK
    • Sodium: Sodium can fall (hyponatraemia) rarely, mainly in older people. UK

    Pharmacokinetics

    Absorption
    Absorbed from the small intestine after the gastro-resistant coating dissolves; bioavailability about 35% after a single dose, rising to about 60% with repeated dosing
    Peak
    1-3 hours (plasma); maximal acid suppression after 3-5 days
    Half-life
    About 1 hour in plasma, but the effect lasts over 24 hours because pump inhibition is irreversible
    Metabolism
    Liver, CYP2C19 (poor metabolisers have higher levels) and CYP3A4; inhibits CYP2C19
    Elimination
    About 80% as metabolites in urine, the rest in faeces

    What is omeprazole used for?

    Omeprazole is a proton pump inhibitor, a medicine that lowers the amount of acid the stomach makes. The NHS says it is used to treat or prevent stomach ulcers, heartburn and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, often called GORD. It is also used, together with antibiotics, to treat a stomach infection called Helicobacter pylori.

    Omeprazole comes as capsules, tablets and a liquid, and the NHS says tablets and capsules are swallowed whole with water or squash. The NHS says capsules are prescription only, while some tablets can be bought from pharmacies and supermarkets for adults. Tablets bought this way are meant for short-term use, and the NHS says to tell a doctor if symptoms persist. The NHS describes omeprazole as a medicine to treat heartburn and indigestion.

    How does omeprazole work, and how quickly?

    Omeprazole blocks the last step in the making of stomach acid. It acts on an enzyme called the proton pump, which sits on acid-making cells in the stomach wall. A review of its pharmacology says omeprazole is a prodrug, changed to its active form only in the acid-making cells, where it binds irreversibly to the proton pump. This gives an effective and long-lasting reduction in stomach acid.

    The NHS says omeprazole starts to work within 2 to 3 days, but it may take up to 4 weeks to work fully. It is a medicine that lowers stomach acid, and the NHS lists ulcers, heartburn and GORD among the conditions it treats or prevents.

    What are the side effects of omeprazole?

    The NHS lists headache, stomach pain, feeling or being sick, constipation and diarrhoea as common side effects. The NHS says omeprazole can also cause dizziness, sleepiness or blurred vision, and advises against driving, cycling or using machinery or tools until feeling better.

    Serious side effects are rarer. The NHS says omeprazole may affect how the body absorbs vitamin B12, particularly with long use, that taking it for more than a year may raise the chance of bone fractures, and that a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is possible in rare cases. The NHS says the level of magnesium in the blood may also fall with longer use. Anaphylaxis is a medical emergency, and the route for that in the UK is 999.

    The NHS says to speak to a doctor about trouble swallowing, feeling or being sick, feeling full very quickly when eating, or losing weight without trying, because these can be signs of stomach cancer.

    Taking omeprazole with other medicines or alcohol

    The NHS says omeprazole can interact with other medicines, so a pharmacist or doctor can be asked about anything new. UK drug safety guidance discourages using omeprazole or esomeprazole with clopidogrel, an antiplatelet medicine. The Specialist Pharmacy Service, part of the NHS, explains that clopidogrel is activated by an enzyme called CYP2C19, and that omeprazole and esomeprazole inhibit this enzyme, which can lower the level of activated clopidogrel. The Specialist Pharmacy Service says lansoprazole, pantoprazole or rabeprazole are preferred to omeprazole for people taking clopidogrel.

    The NHS says alcohol does not stop omeprazole working. But alcohol makes the stomach produce more acid than usual, which can irritate the stomach lining and make symptoms worse. For that reason the NHS says it is best to avoid alcohol if possible while taking omeprazole.

    Who needs extra care with omeprazole?

    The NHS lists kidney conditions, liver conditions, osteoporosis, and a past allergic reaction or serious side effect from omeprazole or another proton pump inhibitor as reasons to check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking it. The NHS says that after more than a year of omeprazole, a doctor will regularly check health to see whether to carry on.

    The NHS says omeprazole can usually be taken in pregnancy, but a pharmacist or doctor should be asked first. It suggests trying to ease indigestion without medicine first. On breastfeeding, the NHS says omeprazole passes into breast milk in small amounts, and that it is OK to take while breastfeeding if a doctor or health visitor says the baby is healthy. The NHS says there is no clear evidence that omeprazole lowers fertility in men or women.

    Frequently asked questions

    Can I buy omeprazole without a prescription?

    The NHS says some omeprazole tablets can be bought from pharmacies and supermarkets for adults, while capsules are prescription only. Tablets bought this way are meant for short-term use. The NHS says to tell a doctor if symptoms persist, and that a doctor may order tests or suggest a different medicine.

    How long does omeprazole take to work?

    The NHS says omeprazole starts to work within 2 to 3 days, but it may take up to 4 weeks to work fully. Tablets bought from a pharmacy are meant for short-term use, and the NHS says to tell a doctor if symptoms persist. The NHS also says it is not known whether omeprazole works less well the longer it is taken.

    Can I drink alcohol while taking omeprazole?

    The NHS says alcohol does not interfere with how omeprazole works. However, alcohol makes the stomach produce more acid than usual. That extra acid can irritate the stomach lining and make symptoms worse. The NHS therefore says it is best to avoid alcohol if possible while taking omeprazole.

    What does the NHS say about long-term use of omeprazole?

    The NHS says taking omeprazole for more than a year may raise the chance of bone fractures and gut infections. It may also affect how the body absorbs vitamin B12. The NHS says a doctor will regularly check health after a year to see whether to carry on. The NHS also says magnesium levels in the blood may fall with longer use.

    Which symptoms does the NHS say to mention to a doctor while taking omeprazole?

    The NHS says to speak to a doctor about trouble swallowing, feeling or being sick, feeling full very quickly when eating, or losing weight without trying, because these can be signs of stomach cancer. The NHS also says a doctor will regularly check health after a year of omeprazole, to see whether to carry on.

    • Heartburn, acid reflux and GORD Heartburn is a burning feeling in the chest from acid reflux; when it keeps happening it is called gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
