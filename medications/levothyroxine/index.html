    Levothyroxine

    Also known as: thyroxine, T4

    Synthetic thyroid hormone (T4) that replaces what an underactive thyroid cannot make, restoring energy, weight and metabolism; the dose is set by regular TSH blood tests. Because it stands in for a hormone the body is missing rather than adding a foreign drug action, side effects are uncommon at the right dose and most of those that occur are the signs of too much thyroid hormone; treatment is usually lifelong and the tablet is taken on an empty stomach, apart from the foods, supplements and medicines that stop it being absorbed.

    Monitoring
    TSH 6-8 weeks after starting or changing dose, then yearly once stable. The NHS describes regular blood tests as the way the dose is confirmed: a follow-up test some weeks after every change, then a yearly check once the result is steady. Between tests the review is clinical, asking about symptoms of under- or over-replacement (tiredness and weight gain on one side; palpitations, sweating, anxiety, poor sleep and weight loss on the other) and checking for new medicines or supplements that interfere with absorption.

    What it is used for

    • Hypothyroidism from any cause (autoimmune, after surgery or radioiodine, congenital); it controls the condition rather than curing it, so it is continued even when the person feels well
    • Suppressing TSH after thyroid cancer, alongside surgery and radioactive iodine treatment
    • Subclinical hypothyroidism when TSH is high, symptomatic, or in pregnancy
    • Not used for weight loss in people whose thyroid works normally: US patient information warns that large doses, especially with amphetamines, can cause serious or life-threatening harm

    How it works in the body

    A synthetic copy of thyroxine (T4), the hormone the thyroid normally releases. Tissues convert it to the active T3, which enters cell nuclei and switches on genes controlling metabolic rate, heat production, heart rate, gut motility, growth and brain development. Replacement restores the pituitary's TSH to normal, which is how the dose is tuned. The NHS puts the same idea simply: levothyroxine is a synthetic version of T4, the main hormone the thyroid makes, and the body converts it into the more active T3, so it stands in for what an underactive gland is not producing. Because it replaces a hormone the body is missing, it usually has few effects of its own; what the person notices depends on whether the dose matches their need. Too little leaves the symptoms of hypothyroidism (tiredness, weight gain, feeling cold) in place, and too much produces the opposite picture of an overactive thyroid. The response is slow: some people feel better within days, others take several months, and the TSH result used to judge the dose takes weeks to settle after each change. Absorption from the gut is the weak point. The tablet is swallowed with water on an empty stomach and food is delayed afterwards, because food, coffee, calcium and iron supplements, antacids and foods such as soya, walnuts and high-fibre meals all reduce how much is taken up, which is why a missed dose or a dose taken too close to food can push the TSH up at the next test.

    Common side effects

    • Few at the right dose; at too high a dose: palpitations, weight loss, sweating, anxiety, insomnia, diarrhoea (symptoms of hyperthyroidism). The NHS lists difficulty sleeping, headaches, feeling anxious, sweating and hair loss as the common effects and notes that they usually ease once the dose is adjusted
    • Temporary hair loss in the first months
    • US information also lists weight change, headache, vomiting, diarrhoea, changes in appetite and fever as less serious effects to mention to a doctor if they persist

    Serious: seek help

    • Angina or atrial fibrillation if started too fast in the elderly or those with heart disease (start at 25 micrograms); a racing heartbeat or chest pain after a dose is a reason to contact a doctor
    • Bone loss and atrial fibrillation with long-term over-replacement
    • Adrenal crisis if given before untreated adrenal insufficiency is corrected; people with adrenal gland conditions, heart disease, diabetes or an overactive thyroid are assessed before starting
    • Thyroid crisis from a sudden excess of hormone (rare): fever, a fast or irregular heartbeat, dizziness or fainting, or yellowing of the skin or eyes, which the NHS advises reporting urgently (NHS 111)
    • Serious allergic reaction (rare): swelling of the throat or tongue, an itchy raised rash or difficulty breathing needs emergency help

    Cautions and interactions

    • Absorption falls with calcium, iron, antacids, proton pump inhibitors, soya and coffee: separate by 4 hours. US information adds walnuts and high-fibre foods to the list of foods that can change how well the medicine works
    • Dose often needs to rise in pregnancy (increase by 25-50% and check TSH promptly), and with oestrogen, enzyme inducers
    • Stay on the same brand where possible: US information notes that different brands contain slightly different amounts of hormone, so a switch is not made without the prescriber or pharmacist knowing
    • Do not use for weight loss in people with normal thyroid function
    • Taken once a day on an empty stomach with a full glass of water, waiting before breakfast and caffeinated drinks; the tablet can stick in the throat if swallowed dry
    • Blood tests are timed in relation to the morning dose, and a missed dose or one taken with food can raise the TSH at the next test; a single accidental extra dose is unlikely to cause harm but more than that, or a racing heart or chest pain, is reported to a doctor
    • Breastfeeding is usually compatible, confirmed with a pharmacist or doctor; the dose is not stopped or changed without advice, since the body has no other source of the hormone

    What is levothyroxine used for?

    Levothyroxine is a medicine for an underactive thyroid, which is called hypothyroidism. The NHS says it suits most adults and children, though it is not right for everyone, so a clinician checks first.

    MedlinePlus, a US source, says it is also used alongside surgery and radioactive iodine treatment for thyroid cancer. It warns that levothyroxine should not be used to lose weight, and that large amounts, especially with amphetamines, can cause serious or life-threatening harm.

    How does levothyroxine work, and how long is it taken for?

    Levothyroxine is a man-made version of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid makes, so it stands in for what an underactive gland is not producing. The NHS says treatment usually carries on for the rest of a person's life. MedlinePlus says people are told to keep taking it even when they feel well, and not to stop without talking to their doctor.

    At first there are regular blood tests until the right amount is reached. Once the result is steady, the NHS says a blood test about once a year is usual.

    What are the side effects of levothyroxine?

    The NHS lists difficulty sleeping, headaches, feeling anxious, sweating and hair loss as the common side effects. It says most side effects happen when the dose is too high, and NHS leaflets describe signs of too much thyroid hormone such as sweating, headaches, diarrhoea and being sick.

    Serious side effects are rare. The NHS lists a sudden rise in thyroid hormone, called thyroid crisis, which can cause a high temperature, a fast or irregular heartbeat, dizziness or faintness, and yellowing of the skin or eyes. It also lists a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), with swelling of the throat or tongue, a raised itchy rash or difficulty breathing. For a suspected anaphylactic reaction the NHS says to call 999.

    Can levothyroxine be taken with food, drinks or other medicines?

    The NHS says levothyroxine is usually taken on an empty stomach, before the first meal of the day, and before any drink with caffeine, such as tea or coffee, or any other medicine. An NHS trust leaflet explains that caffeinated drinks, calcium, iron and antacids can disturb how well the medicine is absorbed.

    The NHS page also lists calcium and iron supplements and antidepressants such as sertraline among medicines that can interact with levothyroxine.

    Is levothyroxine used in pregnancy and breastfeeding?

    The NHS says levothyroxine can be taken in pregnancy if needed. Extra blood tests may be needed to check thyroid hormone levels and that the amount suits the person. A UK hospital leaflet notes that the amount often has to be raised early in pregnancy, and that most women return to their earlier amount after the birth.

    The NHS says levothyroxine can usually be taken while breastfeeding, but advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor first.

    Frequently asked questions

    Will I need levothyroxine for life?

    The NHS says treatment for an underactive thyroid usually carries on for the rest of a person's life. Blood tests are used at first to find the right amount, and once the result is steady a yearly check is usual. MedlinePlus says people are told to keep taking it when they feel well and not to stop without speaking to their doctor.

    Is levothyroxine used for weight loss?

    MedlinePlus, a US source, warns against using levothyroxine to lose weight. It says large amounts, especially taken with amphetamines, can cause serious or life-threatening harm. The medicine is prescribed to replace a missing hormone, and the dose is checked with blood tests.

    What happens if a dose is missed or too much is taken?

    The NHS says a missed dose is taken once remembered, unless the next one is nearly due, in which case the missed one is skipped. It advises against taking two doses at the same time to make up for a missed one. A Sheffield Children's leaflet says an extra dose given to a child by mistake is unlikely to cause harm. For taking more than prescribed, the NHS says to speak to a pharmacist as soon as possible, or call NHS 111 if none can be reached or the extra was taken on purpose.

    Do other medicines or supplements affect levothyroxine?

    Some can. An NHS trust leaflet says calcium, iron and antacids can disturb absorption, and the NHS page lists calcium and iron supplements and antidepressants such as sertraline among those to be aware of. A pharmacist or doctor can check any new medicine, supplement or herbal product against levothyroxine.

    What tests are used while taking levothyroxine?

    Blood tests are used to check that the amount suits the person, and the results guide any change. Once the result is stable the NHS says a yearly test is usual.

    Conditions

    • Underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism) Insufficient thyroid hormone, most often because the immune system destroys the thyroid gland, slowing the body's metabolism.
    • Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism) Excess thyroid hormone, most often from Graves' disease, in which antibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce.
    • Atrial fibrillation A common heart rhythm disorder in which the atria quiver chaotically instead of contracting, producing an irregular and often fast pulse.
    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Thyroid cancer Thyroid cancer is a cancer that starts in the thyroid gland, a gland in the neck that makes hormones.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.
    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.

    Medical tests

    • Thyroid function tests TSH from the pituitary and the thyroid hormones T4 and T3 measured together.
    • Hormone tests Blood levels of hormones and related chemicals, interpreted against the time of day.

    Biomarkers

    • Free T4 (thyroxine) The unbound fraction of thyroxine, the main hormone the thyroid gland secretes.
    • TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) The pituitary hormone that drives the thyroid gland.
