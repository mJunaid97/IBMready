    Loratadine

    Also known as: Clarityn, Claritin

    A non-drowsy antihistamine that blocks the effects of histamine to relieve allergy symptoms, including those of allergic rhinitis and long-lasting hives. The NHS says it can be bought from pharmacies and shops, drowsiness is its most common side effect, and a serious allergic reaction to it is rare but needs a 999 call.

    What it is used for

    • Symptomatic treatment of allergic rhinitis, the nose and eye symptoms of allergies such as hay fever
    • Symptomatic treatment of chronic idiopathic urticaria (long-lasting hives with no identified cause)
    • Relief of allergy symptoms in general, as the NHS describes it

    How it works in the body

    The NHS says loratadine blocks the effects of histamine, a chemical the body releases during an allergic reaction. It is described as non-drowsy because the NHS says it is much less likely to cause sleepiness than some other antihistamines, although some people still feel slightly sleepy. It eases the symptoms of an allergy rather than removing the allergy itself.

    Common side effects

    • Drowsiness is the most common side effect, happening in more than 1 in 100 people; the NHS says trying a different non-drowsy antihistamine may help.
    • The NHS says children may get headaches, and the UK patient leaflet lists headache, increased appetite and difficulty sleeping as common effects in adults and children over 12.
    • The NHS advises against driving, riding a bike or using tools or machinery until fully alert and awake if drowsy.

    Serious: seek help

    • Rarely, a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to loratadine can occur; the NHS says sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, very fast or struggling breathing, a tight throat or trouble swallowing, skin, lips or tongue turning blue, grey or pale, sudden confusion or drowsiness, or fainting needs a 999 call.
    • MedlinePlus, a US source, lists rash, hives, itching, trouble breathing or swallowing, or swelling of the face, lips or throat as reasons to call a doctor right away; in the UK, NHS 111 or the GP the same day is the route for urgent advice, and 999 for the emergency signs above.
    • One UK patient leaflet describes dizziness and nausea as very rare effects and advises contacting a doctor if fits occur.

    Cautions and interactions

    • Pregnancy: the NHS says loratadine can be taken in pregnancy and advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor, and its antihistamines page says loratadine is usually recommended if an antihistamine is needed. The UK SmPC reports more than 1000 exposed outcomes showing no malformation or foetal toxicity, yet says it is preferable as a precaution to avoid loratadine in pregnancy.
    • Breastfeeding: the NHS says only very small amounts of loratadine get into breast milk, while the UK SmPC says loratadine is excreted in breast milk and does not recommend its use in breastfeeding women.
    • The UK SmPC says all known inhibitors of the liver enzymes CYP3A4 or CYP2D6 could raise loratadine levels. The NHS says to tell a pharmacist or doctor about other medicines, including amiodarone, cimetidine, erythromycin and any medicine that causes drowsiness, dry mouth or difficulty peeing.
    • The UK patient leaflet advises that people with rare hereditary galactose intolerance, total lactase deficiency or glucose-galactose malabsorption should not take the tablets.
    • The UK leaflet says the tablets have not been shown to add to the effects of an alcoholic drink, but the NHS says it is best not to drink alcohol because it can make a person sleepy.
    • The NHS says loratadine is unlikely to do harm if taken for a long time, but it is best taken only for as long as needed.
    • The NHS says loratadine does not affect any type of contraception, including the combined pill and emergency contraception.

    What is loratadine used for?

    Loratadine is an antihistamine that the NHS describes as non-drowsy and as relieving allergy symptoms. In the UK its licensed uses, according to the summary of product characteristics (SmPC), are the symptomatic treatment of allergic rhinitis and of chronic idiopathic urticaria. Allergic rhinitis is the medical name for hay fever and similar allergies that affect the nose, and urticaria is the medical name for hives. Chronic idiopathic urticaria means hives that last a long time without an identified cause. The NHS says loratadine can be bought from pharmacies and shops and can also be prescribed. It comes as tablets and as a liquid medicine, and the NHS gives Clarityn as the brand name. The UK medicines database (emc) holds product information for the tablets and for the syrup, both under the Clarityn Allergy name. MedlinePlus, a US source, lists loratadine (Claritin) among antihistamines that cause less sleepiness than others.

    How does loratadine work?

    When someone meets something they are allergic to, the body releases histamine, a chemical involved in the allergic reaction. The NHS says loratadine works by blocking the effects of histamine, which reduces allergy symptoms. It eases the symptoms of an allergy rather than removing the allergy itself. The NHS says loratadine is much less likely to make people sleepy than some other antihistamines, although some people still feel slightly sleepy. Even so, the NHS lists drowsiness as the most common side effect. The NHS says people may begin to feel better within 1 to 3 hours of taking it.

    What are the side effects of loratadine?

    Drowsiness is the most common side effect, and the NHS says trying a different non-drowsy antihistamine may help if it happens. The NHS says drowsiness happens in more than 1 in 100 people. If drowsy, the NHS advises not driving, riding a bike or using tools or machinery until fully alert and awake. The NHS says children may also get headaches. The UK patient leaflet lists headache, increased appetite and difficulty sleeping as common effects in adults and children over 12. Rarely, a person can have a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to loratadine itself. The NHS says to call 999 for sudden swelling of the lips, mouth, throat or tongue, very fast or struggling breathing, a tight throat or trouble swallowing, skin, lips or tongue turning blue, grey or pale, sudden confusion or drowsiness, or fainting. It also says not to drive yourself to A&E in that situation. MedlinePlus, a US source, lists rash, hives, itching, trouble breathing or swallowing, or swelling of the face, lips or throat as reasons to call a doctor right away. In the UK, NHS 111 or the GP the same day is the route for urgent advice that is not an emergency. One UK patient leaflet describes dizziness and nausea as very rare effects and advises contacting a doctor if fits occur.

    Pregnancy, breastfeeding, other medicines and alcohol

    The NHS says loratadine can be taken in pregnancy and advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor. Its antihistamines page says loratadine is usually recommended if an antihistamine is needed in pregnancy. The UK SmPC reports more than 1000 exposed outcomes showing no malformation or foetal toxicity, yet says it is preferable as a precaution to avoid loratadine in pregnancy. For breastfeeding, the NHS says only very small amounts of loratadine get into breast milk. The UK SmPC takes a more cautious line, saying loratadine is excreted in breast milk and that its use in breastfeeding women is not recommended. The NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service reports that occasional cases of reduced milk supply have been described with loratadine. According to the NHS, loratadine does not affect any type of contraception, including the combined pill and emergency contraception. The SmPC notes that inhibitors of the liver enzymes CYP3A4 or CYP2D6 could raise loratadine levels. The NHS says to tell a pharmacist or doctor about other medicines, including ones bought without a prescription, and names amiodarone, cimetidine, erythromycin and any medicine that causes drowsiness, dry mouth or difficulty peeing. The leaflet advises that people with rare hereditary galactose intolerance, total lactase deficiency or glucose-galactose malabsorption should not take the tablets. The leaflet says the tablets have not been shown to add to the effects of an alcoholic drink, but the NHS says it is best not to drink alcohol because it can make a person sleepy. On long-term use, the NHS says loratadine is unlikely to do harm but is best taken only for as long as needed.

    Frequently asked questions

    Does loratadine make you drowsy?

    It is described as non-drowsy, because the NHS says it is much less likely to cause sleepiness than some other antihistamines. Drowsiness is still the most common side effect. The NHS advises not driving, riding a bike or using tools or machinery until fully alert if it happens, and says a different non-drowsy antihistamine may help.

    Can loratadine be used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding?

    The NHS says loratadine can be taken in pregnancy and advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor. The UK SmPC prefers avoiding it in pregnancy as a precaution, despite reassuring data. The NHS says only very small amounts reach breast milk, while the SmPC does not recommend its use in breastfeeding women.

    Does loratadine affect the contraceptive pill?

    According to the NHS, loratadine does not affect any type of contraception, including the combined pill and emergency contraception. A pharmacist can answer questions about other medicines.

    Is it harmful to take loratadine for a long time?

    The NHS says it is unlikely to do harm if taken for a long time. It also says it is best taken only for as long as needed.

    Can I drink alcohol with loratadine?

    The leaflet says loratadine has not been shown to add to the effects of an alcoholic drink. The NHS still says it is best not to drink alcohol, because it can make a person sleepy.
