    Morphine and opioids

    The reference strong opioid for severe pain, such as after major surgery, injury or in advanced cancer; highly effective but sedating, constipating and able to slow breathing. It works in the brain and spinal cord to interrupt pain messages and ease the distress pain causes, and its harms follow from the same action: the NHS describes slowed breathing as the main danger of taking more than prescribed, and with continued use the body adapts, so the dose is reviewed regularly and never stopped suddenly.

    Dosage forms
    Solution, Tablet, Extended-release (modified-release) tablet, Capsule, Injection
    Usual preparations
    Immediate-release oral liquid and tablets, 12-hourly modified-release tablets, injections into vein, muscle or under the skin, patient-controlled pumps, patches (fentanyl, buprenorphine) (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Intravenous within minutes; oral 30-60 minutes; modified-release over hours. The NHS notes that the injection gives the quickest relief, working almost straight away, while tablets and liquid wear off after some hours, so immediate-release forms are repeated and modified-release forms are used for continuous pain.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 7052 · ATC N02AA01 · FDA EPC Opioid Agonist [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Sedation score and respiratory rate in hospital; regular review of benefit, dose and side effects at home. The NHS describes the dose being reviewed to make sure a person is taking only the amount needed to control pain, with attention to tolerance, dependence and increasing sensitivity to pain; bowel function, drowsiness, confusion and falls are checked at each review, and kidney function is relevant because morphine's active breakdown products accumulate when the kidneys are impaired.

    What it is used for

    • Severe acute pain: heart attack, fractures, after major surgery, sickle cell crises
    • Cancer pain and pain at the end of life
    • Breathlessness in advanced heart and lung disease and in palliative care
    • Weaker relatives (codeine, tramadol) for moderate pain; long-term use for chronic non-cancer pain is now discouraged because harms outweigh benefits
    • It is a prescription-only controlled drug; US patient information describes it as an opiate (narcotic) analgesic for pain severe enough to need an opioid and not relieved by other treatments

    How it works in the body

    Binds mu-opioid receptors on nerve cells in the spinal cord, brainstem and brain, the same receptors used by the body's own endorphins. This closes the gate on pain signals entering the spinal cord, dampens the emotional response to pain in the brain and, unwantedly, slows the breathing centre in the brainstem, slows the gut and triggers nausea and euphoria. The NHS describes it for the reader as working in the central nervous system and the brain to block pain signals to the rest of the body, while also reducing the anxiety and stress that pain causes; US information says it changes the way the brain and nervous system respond to pain. The route sets the speed: an injection into a vein works almost straight away, whereas tablets and liquid take under an hour to act and wear off after some hours, and modified-release forms release the drug slowly over many hours so that pain is covered around the clock. The gut effect explains the most reliable side effect: morphine slows digestion, so constipation is expected rather than exceptional and is usually prevented from the start. With regular use the body becomes used to the drug (tolerance), so higher doses may be needed over time for the same relief, and some people become more sensitive to pain rather than less, which clinicians treat by lowering the dose gradually rather than raising it. Stopping suddenly after regular use produces withdrawal symptoms, and physical dependence and addiction are possible, which is why the dose and the reason for continuing are reviewed at each prescription.

    Common side effects

    • Constipation (almost universal, needs a laxative from the start): the NHS explains it happens because morphine slows digestion
    • Nausea or vomiting, drowsiness, dizziness and confusion, most marked in the first days; the NHS says sleepiness and sickness often fade within days to a couple of weeks as the body adjusts, taking the dose with food eases sickness, and driving, cycling and using machinery are avoided while dizzy, sleepy or confused
    • Headaches, which usually settle within the first week
    • Itching, dry mouth, small pupils, itchy skin or a rash
    • Side effects are more likely at higher doses, and alcohol adds to the sleepiness

    Serious: seek help

    • Respiratory depression and death in overdose, or when combined with alcohol, benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids; reversed by naloxone. The NHS lists slowed or difficult breathing, trouble breathing, a seizure or a swollen face, throat or tongue as reasons to call 999, and warns that recreational drugs such as cannabis, cocaine or heroin raise the risk of breathing problems
    • Tolerance, physical dependence and addiction with continued use; stopping suddenly causes withdrawal symptoms such as agitation, anxiety, shaking and sweating, so the dose is reduced gradually under supervision
    • Falls and confusion in the elderly; hormone suppression and immune effects long term
    • Opioid-induced hyperalgesia: pain becoming worse with escalating doses; the NHS notes that some people become more sensitive to pain and the dose is then usually lowered gradually
    • Irregular heartbeat, blurred or double vision, muscle stiffness, or feeling dizzy, tired and low in energy, which the NHS advises reporting to a doctor or NHS 111
    • Serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), which is rare

    Cautions and interactions

    • Controlled drug; start low, review early, plan the stop. US information warns that taking a different amount from the one prescribed can cause serious or life-threatening effects, and that liquid doses are measured with a proper measuring device
    • Reduce doses in kidney failure (morphine metabolites accumulate), liver disease and the elderly
    • Never crush or chew modified-release forms
    • Do not drive while drowsy or after dose changes
    • Alcohol is best avoided because it increases sleepiness and the risk of serious effects; recreational drugs raise the risk of breathing problems
    • Not stopped suddenly after regular use: withdrawal symptoms follow, and the dose is tapered on medical advice
    • Tolerance and dependence are expected with long-term use, so each prescription is a review of whether the benefit still outweighs the harm, especially in chronic non-cancer pain

    Conditions

    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Lung cancer A malignant tumour arising from the lining of the airways or alveoli, divided into non-small-cell (about 85%) and small-cell types.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Kidney stones Hard crystals, most commonly calcium oxalate, that form in the kidney and can pass into and block the ureter.
    • Pancreatitis Inflammation of the pancreas, acute when its own enzymes are activated inside the gland.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gallstones Hard deposits, usually of cholesterol, that form in the gallbladder when bile becomes supersaturated.

    Symptoms

    • Chest pain Pain or pressure anywhere between the neck and the upper abdomen.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.
    • Back pain Pain in the spine or the muscles beside it, most often the lower back.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Nausea The unpleasant sensation of being about to vomit.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Pulse oximetry and blood gases A clip on the fingertip shines red and infrared light through the tissue and reads the colour of pulsing blood to estimate how saturated haemoglobin…

    Procedures

    • Knee replacement The worn joint surfaces of the knee are replaced with metal components and a polyethylene insert to relieve the pain of severe arthritis when…
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.
    • Appendicectomy Surgical removal of the inflamed appendix, usually by keyhole surgery, is the standard treatment for acute appendicitis.

    Medications

    • Paracetamol The first-line painkiller and fever reducer for most people (acetaminophen in North America).
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Tramadol An opioid painkiller for moderate to severe pain that also boosts serotonin and noradrenaline signalling.
    • Oxycodone A strong opioid painkiller for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery.
    • Codeine and co-codamol Codeine is an opioid painkiller used for pain such as pain after an operation or injury.
    • Sertraline and SSRIs A selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) antidepressant used for depression, anxiety disorders, panic and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

    Drug classes

    • Opioids Strong painkillers that act on the body's own endorphin receptors: morphine, codeine, tramadol, oxycodone, fentanyl.

    Guides and news

    • Opioid painkillers: what to know Opioids such as morphine are prescription painkillers for severe pain; the NHS lists constipation and sleepiness as common side effects.
