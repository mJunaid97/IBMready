    Diazepam

    Also known as: Valium

    Diazepam is a benzodiazepine, a type of medicine used to treat anxiety, muscle spasms and seizures. The NHS says it should only be used for the short term, and that longer use can cause addiction and withdrawal symptoms. It is a controlled drug in the UK, and the NHS advises against driving, cycling or using machinery while it affects alertness.

    What it is used for

    • Treating anxiety.
    • Treating muscle spasms.
    • Treating seizures.

    How it works in the body

    NHS Inform describes benzodiazepines as depressant drugs that slow down the central nervous system. The sources used for this page do not describe the mechanism in more detail.

    Common side effects

    • The NHS lists feeling less alert, increased anxiety and depression, drowsiness, tiredness and low blood pressure as common side effects.
    • The NHS advises against alcohol and grapefruit juice while taking diazepam because they can increase the risk of side effects.
    • The NHS advises against driving, cycling or using tools or machinery while affected.

    Serious: seek help

    • Rare serious side effects listed by the NHS include a severe allergic reaction, slowed breathing and yellowing of the skin or eyes.
    • NHS Inform says stopping benzodiazepines suddenly can cause withdrawal effects that could be life-threatening.
    • In an emergency the route to care is 999 or A&E; for an urgent concern that is not an emergency it is NHS 111 or a same-day GP appointment, and for routine questions a GP appointment or a pharmacist.

    Cautions and interactions

    • The NHS says taking diazepam for longer than the short term can cause addiction and withdrawal symptoms.
    • NHS Inform says tolerance can develop quickly and there is a risk of psychological and physical dependence.
    • The MHRA says dependence can occur with therapeutic doses of benzodiazepines and can become established within weeks.
    • NHS Inform says benzodiazepines slow the central nervous system, which increases the risk of overdose, particularly when combined with alcohol, gabapentinoids or opioids.
    • The NHS lists long-term kidney, liver, heart or breathing problems, certain mental health conditions, a history of alcohol or recreational drug misuse and a past allergic reaction to diazepam or any other medicine as reasons diazepam may not be suitable.
    • The NHS says diazepam should not be taken in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist advises it. The manufacturer's patient leaflet says that if it is taken regularly during pregnancy, the baby may develop withdrawal symptoms.
    • The NHS says diazepam passes into breast milk in small amounts, and advises checking with a pharmacist or doctor before use while breastfeeding.

    What is diazepam used for?

    The NHS says diazepam is a benzodiazepine used to treat anxiety, muscle spasms and seizures. It is available only on prescription. NHS Inform describes benzodiazepines as depressant drugs that slow down the central nervous system.

    Dependence, withdrawal and stopping

    The NHS says diazepam should only be used for the short term, and that taking it for longer can cause addiction and withdrawal symptoms. NHS Inform says tolerance can develop quickly, with a risk of psychological and physical dependence. The MHRA says dependence can occur at therapeutic doses and can become established within weeks. If benzodiazepines are stopped suddenly, withdrawal effects can occur, and NHS Inform says these could be life-threatening. Because of this, reduction after longer use is gradual and planned with the prescriber; a GP appointment is the route, and NHS 111 or 999 if withdrawal symptoms are severe.

    Driving, alcohol and side effects

    The NHS lists feeling less alert, drowsiness, tiredness and low blood pressure among common side effects. The NHS advises against driving, cycling or using tools or machinery while affected. Alcohol and grapefruit juice can increase the risk of side effects, according to the NHS. Rare serious side effects include a severe allergic reaction, slowed breathing and yellowing of the skin or eyes.

    Controlled drug status and who needs extra care

    Diazepam is a Class C drug under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 and is in Schedule 4 Part 1 of the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. The NHS lists long-term kidney, liver, heart or breathing problems, certain mental health conditions and a history of alcohol or drug misuse among the reasons for care. It is not advised in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist recommends it, and the manufacturer's patient leaflet says that regular use during pregnancy may cause withdrawal symptoms in the baby. NHS Inform warns that combining benzodiazepines with alcohol, gabapentinoids or opioids raises the risk of overdose.

    Frequently asked questions

    How long can diazepam be taken?

    The NHS says it should only be used for the short term, because longer use can cause addiction and withdrawal symptoms.

    Is diazepam a controlled drug?

    Yes. It is Class C under the Misuse of Drugs Act 1971 and in Schedule 4 Part 1 of the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001.

    Can diazepam be drunk with alcohol?

    The NHS advises against alcohol with diazepam because it can increase the risk of side effects.
