    Propranolol

    Also known as: Inderal, Inderal LA, propranolol hydrochloride

    Propranolol is a beta blocker used for heart problems such as high blood pressure, to help with some symptoms of anxiety and to prevent migraines. It is available on prescription only. The NHS lists asthma, low blood pressure and a slow heart rate among reasons it may not be suitable, and describes extra effects from stopping suddenly after long-term use.

    What it is used for

    • High blood pressure and other heart conditions.
    • Helping with some symptoms of anxiety.
    • Preventing migraines.
    • Infantile haemangiomas (vascular birthmarks) in children, usually started by a specialist; this use is described as off-label.

    How it works in the body

    Propranolol is a beta blocker, a type of medicine that slows the heart rate.

    Common side effects

    • Feeling dizzy or tired, cold hands or feet, difficulty sleeping and nightmares are the most common; the NHS says these are usually mild and short-lived.
    • Dizziness is most likely when first starting or after an increase in dose, and the NHS says that anyone affected should not drive, ride a bike or use tools or machinery.
    • Hospital leaflets also list nausea and diarrhoea or constipation.

    Serious: seek help

    • The NHS describes unusual or easy bruising as a rare serious side effect and advises contacting a doctor or NHS 111 straight away.

    Cautions and interactions

    • The NHS lists a past allergic reaction to propranolol or another medicine, low blood pressure, a slow heart rate, worsening heart failure, and lung disease or asthma among reasons it may not be suitable.
    • UK patient leaflets say that people who have ever had asthma or wheezing should not take propranolol tablets.
    • The NHS lists diabetes among the conditions to mention to the doctor, and says propranolol may make it more difficult to recognise the warning signs of low blood sugar.
    • Leaflets say it may delay the response to treatment of low blood sugar.
    • The NHS says it can be taken in pregnancy but may sometimes affect a baby's growth, so extra growth scans are usually offered from around 32 weeks.
    • The NHS says breastfeeding is acceptable if the baby is healthy, because only tiny amounts pass into breast milk.
    • The LactMed database reports no adverse reactions clearly attributable to propranolol in breastfed infants, with isolated reports of low blood sugar and slow heart rate.
    • Some NHS trust migraine guidance, including from Cambridge University Hospitals, does not recommend it in pregnancy, when planning a pregnancy or during breastfeeding, so guidance differs and the clinician decides.
    • The NHS says other blood pressure medicines can sometimes lower blood pressure too much, causing dizziness or fainting.
    • Cold remedies, including pharmacy-bought ones, and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, diclofenac and naproxen are also listed, and the NHS says NSAIDs can raise blood pressure so are best kept to a minimum.
    • The NHS says alcohol can strengthen the blood-pressure-lowering effect, which may cause dizziness or light-headedness.
    • The NHS describes stopping suddenly after long-term use as able to cause extra effects such as an irregular heartbeat, sweating and shaking, and says the prescriber normally lowers the dose gradually.

    What is propranolol used for?

    Propranolol is a beta blocker. The NHS lists heart conditions, including high blood pressure, some symptoms of anxiety and the prevention of migraines among its uses. In children, NHS documents describe propranolol liquid being used for infantile haemangiomas, which are vascular birthmarks. A Dorset NHS document lists this use as off-label, meaning outside the manufacturer's licence, and restricts it to starting by a specialist. In the UK propranolol is available on prescription only, and is made as tablets, slow-release capsules and a liquid.

    How does propranolol work, and how quickly?

    Propranolol slows the heart rate. The NHS says it usually starts to work within a few hours. For heart conditions or high blood pressure the full effect can take 2 to 3 weeks. For migraine prevention it can take several weeks to make a real difference, so the benefit is not immediate.

    What are the side effects of propranolol?

    The NHS says the most common side effects are usually mild and do not last long. These are feeling dizzy or tired, cold hands or feet, difficulty sleeping and nightmares. Dizziness is most likely when first starting or after an increase in dose, and the NHS says anyone affected should not drive, ride a bike or use tools or machinery. Hospital leaflets also list nausea and diarrhoea or constipation.

    The NHS describes serious side effects as rare. It advises contacting a doctor or NHS 111 straight away for unusual or easy bruising.

    Who needs extra care with propranolol?

    The NHS lists several situations where propranolol may not be suitable, including a past allergic reaction to it or another medicine, low blood pressure, a slow heart rate, worsening heart failure, and lung disease or asthma. UK patient leaflets say that people who have ever had asthma or wheezing should not take propranolol tablets.

    The NHS lists diabetes among the conditions to mention to the doctor, and says propranolol may make it more difficult to recognise the warning signs of low blood sugar. Leaflets say it may delay the response to treatment of low blood sugar.

    On pregnancy, the NHS says propranolol can be taken but may sometimes affect a baby's growth, so extra growth scans are usually offered from around 32 weeks. The NHS says breastfeeding is acceptable if the baby is healthy, because only tiny amounts pass into breast milk. Some NHS trust migraine guidance, including from Cambridge University Hospitals, does not recommend it in pregnancy, when planning a pregnancy or during breastfeeding. The two sources therefore differ, and the clinician weighs this up for each person.

    Taking propranolol with other medicines, alcohol and stopping

    The NHS says that other blood pressure medicines can sometimes lower blood pressure too much, causing dizziness or fainting. Cold remedies, including pharmacy-bought ones, and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, diclofenac and naproxen are also listed, and the NHS says NSAIDs can raise blood pressure. The NHS says alcohol can strengthen the blood-pressure-lowering effect, which may cause dizziness or light-headedness.

    The NHS describes stopping suddenly after long-term use as able to cause extra effects such as an irregular heartbeat, sweating and shaking. The prescriber normally lowers the dose gradually, and any change is decided with them.

    The NHS says propranolol does not stop contraception working, but combined pills and the contraceptive patch are usually not recommended with it.

    Long-term use and anxiety

    The NHS says blood pressure is checked regularly for people taking propranolol long term for migraines or anxiety. For anxiety, the NHS says there do not seem to be lasting harmful effects from taking it for several months or years, and that it is best taken only for as long as needed.

    Some local NHS prescribing guidance, such as the Lancashire and South Cumbria formulary, advises against starting propranolol for generalised anxiety disorder or panic disorder and warns that it can be very toxic in overdose. Anyone in immediate danger can call 999, use NHS 111 and choose the mental health option, or call Samaritans free on 116 123 at any time.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is propranolol used for anxiety?

    The NHS says propranolol can help with some symptoms of anxiety. It is best taken only for as long as needed, according to the NHS. Some local NHS guidance is more cautious, so whether it suits a person is for their clinician to decide.

    Does propranolol affect contraception?

    The NHS says propranolol will not stop contraception working. However, the combined pill and contraceptive patch are not usually recommended with propranolol. Vomiting or prolonged diarrhoea can also make the pill less effective.

    Can people with diabetes have propranolol?

    The NHS lists diabetes among the conditions to tell the doctor about before starting propranolol. The medicine may make it more difficult to recognise the warning signs of low blood sugar.

    Can people drive while taking propranolol?

    Propranolol can make some people feel dizzy, especially when first starting or after an increase in dose. The NHS says anyone affected should not drive, ride a bike or use tools or machinery. If unsure, a doctor or pharmacist can advise.

    What happens if propranolol is stopped suddenly?

    The NHS describes extra effects after stopping suddenly following long-term use, such as an irregular heartbeat, sweating and shaking. The prescriber normally lowers the dose gradually, and decides how.

    Is Inderal the same as propranolol?

    Inderal is a UK brand name of propranolol hydrochloride tablets, made by Atnahs Pharma UK. Inderal LA is a modified-release capsule brand.
