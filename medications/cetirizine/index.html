    Cetirizine and antihistamines

    A non-drowsy second-generation antihistamine that blocks histamine H1 receptors to relieve hay fever, hives and other allergic symptoms.

    What it is used for

    • Hay fever and year-round allergic rhinitis: sneezing, runny nose, itchy eyes
    • Urticaria (hives) and itching
    • Mild allergic reactions to foods, insect bites and drugs (not a substitute for adrenaline in anaphylaxis)
    • Sedating first-generation antihistamines (chlorphenamine, promethazine) for short-term itch or nausea

    How it works in the body

    Blocks H1 histamine receptors on blood vessels, nerves and smooth muscle. When mast cells release histamine in response to an allergen, it normally makes capillaries leak (swelling, runny nose), stimulates itch nerves and constricts airways; with the receptors occupied, those effects are muted. Second-generation drugs cross the blood-brain barrier poorly, so they cause much less drowsiness than older ones.

    Common side effects

    • Mild drowsiness (about 1 in 10, more than loratadine or fexofenadine), dry mouth, headache, tiredness

    Serious: seek help

    • Marked sedation and impaired driving with first-generation agents; confusion and falls in the elderly, urinary retention in men with prostate enlargement
    • QT prolongation with some older non-sedating agents (withdrawn); rare hypersensitivity

    Cautions and interactions

    • Check driving response with any antihistamine; avoid alcohol
    • Dose reduced in kidney impairment for cetirizine
    • Not for children under 2 without advice; sedating antihistamines avoided in young children
    • Anaphylaxis needs adrenaline first: antihistamines are adjuncts only
