    Furosemide

    Also known as: frusemide

    A powerful loop diuretic that makes the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour, used to clear fluid overload in heart failure, kidney disease and liver disease. Less fluid in the tissues eases swelling and breathlessness and less fluid in the circulation lowers blood pressure, but the same action can dehydrate, deplete potassium and sodium and strain the kidneys, so blood tests and weight are followed.

    Usual preparations
    Tablets 20, 40, 500 mg; oral solution; intravenous injection or infusion (summary of the cited reference, not a prescription)
    Onset and duration
    Oral within an hour, lasting about 6 hours (hence the name Lasix: 'lasts six'); intravenous within minutes. The NHS notes that although each dose acts quickly, it may take a few weeks for the full effect on fluid and blood pressure.
    Terminology
    RxNorm RxCUI 4603 · ATC C03CA01 · FDA EPC Loop Diuretic [EPC]
    Monitoring
    Kidney function and electrolytes within a week of starting or changing dose, then regularly; weight and blood pressure. The NHS describes long-term treatment as needing regular blood and urine tests with a doctor or nurse, and people are told to report signs of dehydration or kidney trouble (very little or very dark urine, flank pain, fever) between checks.

    What it is used for

    • Fluid overload in heart failure: breathlessness, swollen legs, fluid on the lungs (intravenous in emergencies or when tablets cannot be taken)
    • Oedema from kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome) and liver cirrhosis (with spironolactone)
    • High blood pressure when kidney function is poor
    • Thiazide diuretics (bendroflumethiazide) and thiazide-like diuretics (indapamide) are the gentler relatives used for blood pressure

    How it works in the body

    Blocks the sodium-potassium-chloride co-transporter in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle, the segment that reabsorbs a quarter of all filtered sodium. Salt that stays in the tubule drags water with it, so urine output can rise several-fold within an hour. Potassium, magnesium and calcium are lost too, and the kidney compensates by activating the renin-angiotensin system. The NHS describes the result for the person: by acting on the loop of Henle furosemide increases the amount of salt and water passed in the urine; less fluid in the tissues reduces swelling, and less fluid in the blood helps lower blood pressure. A single dose works for about six hours, so it is usually taken once in the morning so that the extra trips to the toilet do not disturb sleep, and although it starts within an hour the full benefit on swelling and blood pressure can take some weeks to settle. It controls fluid overload and blood pressure without curing the underlying condition, so it is continued even when the person feels well, and stopping it abruptly lets blood pressure rise again.

    Common side effects

    • Passing lots of urine, urgency (take in the morning, and not late in the day, to avoid waking at night)
    • Dizziness on standing, dehydration, thirst with a dry mouth and lips, headaches, muscle twitches and cramps; getting up slowly helps, and driving is avoided while dizzy
    • Feeling sick
    • Low potassium, sodium and magnesium; raised uric acid (gout) and glucose

    Serious: seek help

    • Acute kidney injury from over-diuresis: pain in the lower back or side, feeling sick, a high temperature, passing little urine or blood in the urine
    • Severe dehydration: passing much less urine than usual, or dark, strong-smelling urine
    • Dangerous potassium or sodium disturbance and arrhythmia
    • Hearing loss (ototoxicity) with rapid high intravenous doses, especially with aminoglycosides
    • Collapse from low blood pressure in the frail
    • Severe skin reactions (Stevens-Johnson syndrome): blistering around the mouth, eyes, nose or genitals with flu-like symptoms; serious allergic reaction with a swollen throat or tongue or difficulty breathing

    Cautions and interactions

    • Daily weights are used in heart failure: a gain of about 2 kg over 3 days is the usual prompt to contact the heart failure team or GP, who adjust the diuretic dose
    • Pause on sick days with vomiting, severe diarrhoea, high temperature, sweating or shaking: the NHS says a doctor may advise stopping briefly until the illness settles, because fluid loss from both illness and diuretic together risks dehydration and kidney injury
    • Care with lithium, digoxin, NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen), aminoglycosides
    • Severe low potassium or sodium, anuria, dehydration: do not use until corrected
    • Not stopped without advice: the NHS warns that blood pressure can rise again, increasing the risk of heart attack and stroke

    Other medicines

    Furosemide + Ibuprofen

    ModerateMonitoring or dose adjustment advised

    NSAIDs reduce the effect of loop diuretics and, with them, increase the risk of acute kidney injury and fluid retention.

    Why it can occur
    nephrotoxicity · Renal prostaglandin inhibition reduces natriuresis and renal perfusion
    What official information says
    UK information for furosemide lists anti-inflammatory painkillers among medicines to tell a doctor about; the BNF advises monitoring renal function.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Official guidance: avoid regular use where possible; kidney function monitored if combined.
    What may be monitored
    Creatinine and eGFR · Fluid balance and weight

    Furosemide + Metformin

    Severity not gradedInteraction documented

    Loop diuretics such as furosemide can cause dehydration and reduce kidney function, increasing metformin accumulation and lactic acidosis risk, and can raise blood glucose.

    Why it can occur
    renal clearance
    What official information says
    UK information lists diuretics ('water tablets') among medicines to tell a doctor about when taking metformin.
    What to discuss with a clinician
    Documented; kidney function is monitored and metformin paused if dehydration develops.
    What may be monitored
    Creatinine and eGFR · Blood glucose · Blood glucose

    Conditions

    • Heart failure A syndrome in which the heart cannot pump enough blood to meet the body's needs, or can do so only at raised filling pressures.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Cirrhosis Scarring of the liver from long-term damage, in which nodules of regenerating cells are surrounded by fibrous bands that distort blood flow and…
    • High blood pressure (hypertension) Persistently raised arterial pressure, usually defined as 140/90 mmHg or more in the clinic (135/85 on home readings).
    • Heart attack (myocardial infarction) Death of heart muscle because a coronary artery is suddenly blocked, usually by a clot forming on a ruptured plaque.
    • Urinary incontinence The involuntary leakage of urine, from an occasional dribble on coughing to a complete loss of bladder control.

    Symptoms

    • Swelling (oedema) Excess fluid in the tissues.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Weakness Loss of muscle power, distinct from tiredness or heaviness.

    Medical tests

    • Kidney function tests Creatinine and urea are waste products the kidneys should clear; how much builds up in blood shows how well they are filtering.
    • Electrolytes The dissolved salts that set the water content of the body and the electrical behaviour of nerves, muscle and the heart: sodium, potassium, chloride.
    • Troponin test A blood test for cardiac troponin, a protein released when heart muscle cells are injured.
    • Blood pressure measurement The pressure of blood against the artery walls, recorded as two numbers: the systolic pressure when the heart contracts and the diastolic pressure…

    Biomarkers

    • Natriuretic peptides (BNP and NT-proBNP) Hormones released by the heart's ventricles when their walls are stretched by high pressure or volume.
    • Potassium The main positively charged ion inside cells; only about 2% is in the blood, but that small fraction sets the electrical excitability of nerves.
    • Sodium The main positively charged ion of the fluid outside cells and the chief determinant of blood osmolality.

    Procedures

    • Dialysis Dialysis takes over the kidneys' filtering work when they have failed.
    • Blood transfusion Donated red cells, platelets or plasma are given into a vein, matched to the recipient's blood group and checked at every step, to treat anaemia.
    • Coronary artery bypass grafting Open-heart surgery that bypasses blocked coronary arteries with the patient's own chest-wall artery and leg vein or arm artery.

    Medications

    • Ramipril An ACE inhibitor that relaxes blood vessels and eases the heart's workload, used for high blood pressure, heart failure.
    • Metoprolol and beta blockers A beta blocker that slows the heart and reduces its oxygen demand, used for angina, after heart attacks.
    • Amlodipine A long-acting calcium channel blocker that relaxes artery walls to lower blood pressure and prevent angina.
    • Allopurinol A xanthine oxidase inhibitor that lowers uric acid in the blood.
    • Lisinopril An ACE inhibitor that lowers blood pressure, protects the heart after a heart attack and in heart failure.
    • Spironolactone Spironolactone is a diuretic, a medicine that helps reduce fluid build-up in the body.

    Drug classes

    • Loop diuretics Powerful diuretics that make the kidneys excrete salt and water within an hour.
