    25-hydroxyvitamin D

    Also known as: vitamin D, 25-OH vitamin D, calcidiol

    The storage form of vitamin D that the liver makes, the best single measure of vitamin D status and the value the vitamin D test reports.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy
    Also known as
    vitamin D, 25-OH vitamin D, calcidiol
    Units
    nmol/L or ng/mL
    Measured by
    Vitamin D test

    What it is and what it does

    Vitamin D made in the skin by sunlight or taken in food is converted in the liver to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, which circulates for weeks; the kidney then activates a small fraction to calcitriol, the hormone that raises calcium absorption from the gut. The 25-hydroxy form is measured because it reflects the body's supply. The laboratory reports the total of the D2 and D3 forms of 25-hydroxyvitamin D, in the UK usually in nmol/L. The test is requested when deficiency or excess is suspected, when calcium, phosphate or parathyroid hormone results are abnormal, and in people at higher risk such as those aged 65 and over, people with little sun exposure and people with darker skin; routine testing of the general population is not recommended, and UK advice on supplements in autumn and winter does not depend on a blood test. In the UK most people make enough vitamin D in their skin from sunlight between about late March or early April and the end of September, but from October to early March the sunlight lacks the right wavelength, so the body relies on diet and stores; the NHS and the UK Health Security Agency advise everyone to consider a daily supplement through autumn and winter, and all year round for people who are rarely outdoors (frail, housebound or in care homes), who cover most of their skin outdoors or who have darker skin. Sunbeds are not recommended as a source. Measured levels are therefore higher in summer than in winter, and lower with darker skin, older age, higher body weight, consistent use of high-factor sunscreen, malabsorption, kidney or liver disease and some medicines. Results also depend on the method: automated immunoassays are the commonest, liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry is more precise, and pregnancy and kidney failure can interfere with immunoassays, so international standardisation programmes exist to make results comparable between laboratories.

    Why it may be higher

    • Excess supplements (rarely, causing high calcium)

    Why it may be lower

    • Little sun exposure, darker skin, covering clothing, winter at high latitude
    • Low intake and malabsorption
    • Obesity, older age, chronic kidney and liver disease
    • Some antiepileptics and other medicines
    • Consistent use of high-factor sunscreen, and sun exposure through glass, which blocks the UVB needed for synthesis
    • Phenytoin, phenobarbital and rifampicin

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Season and latitude
    • The assay method, which changes results between laboratories
    • Recent high-dose supplements
    • Pregnancy and kidney failure can interfere with immunoassay results
    • Immunoassays and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry can give different values; standardisation programmes aim to align them
    • Both D2 and D3 forms are counted in the total
    Reference ranges. Read against the categories printed on the report (typically deficient, insufficient or inadequate, and sufficient), not against a population reference interval. The boundaries differ between expert bodies and some guidelines no longer endorse fixed thresholds, so a result is interpreted with calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone, the season and the clinical setting, and the laboratory's method matters because immunoassays and mass-spectrometry methods do not always agree.

    Conditions

    • Osteoporosis Loss of bone mass and deterioration of bone architecture that makes bones fragile and prone to fracture after minimal trauma.
    • Bone fracture A break in the continuity of a bone, from a hairline crack to a shattered, displaced or open injury.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.

    Medical tests

    • Vitamin D test Measures 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the storage form the liver makes from vitamin D obtained from sunlight on skin and from food or supplements.

    Guides and news

    • Vitamins and supplements: who may need them The NHS says a varied and balanced diet should provide enough of several vitamins, but advises supplements for some people.
    • Vitamin D blood test: who needs one? NHS guidance does not recommend routine vitamin D tests for people without symptoms, and testing is kept for specific symptoms or conditions.
