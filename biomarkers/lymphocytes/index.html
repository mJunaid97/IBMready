    Lymphocytes

    Also known as: lymphocyte count, lymphs

    The white blood cells that carry adaptive immunity (B cells that make antibodies, T cells that direct the response and kill infected cells), together with natural killer cells, which act in innate immunity. The lymphocyte count rises in many viral infections and falls with steroids, HIV and some cancers.

    What it is and what it does

    Lymphocytes circulate between blood, lymph nodes, the spleen and the tissues, and most of them are outside the bloodstream at any moment. In children the normal count is much higher than in adults. A high count is common in viral infections such as glandular fever and in whooping cough; a persistently high count in an older adult raises the question of chronic lymphocytic leukaemia. A low count is seen with corticosteroids, acute illness, HIV infection, some autoimmune diseases and after chemotherapy or radiotherapy. The count comes from the white cell differential of a full blood count: the analyser (or a stained blood film) sorts white cells into five types and reports each both as a percentage of the total and as an absolute number. The absolute count is the one to interpret, because a surge in neutrophils during a bacterial infection lowers the lymphocyte percentage without changing the number of lymphocytes. Lymphocytes rise in many viral infections and, to a lesser extent, some bacterial ones, in whooping cough (often markedly), in people with an underactive or absent spleen, briefly after strenuous exercise, and in drug hypersensitivity reactions alongside fever, rash and enlarged lymph nodes; a sustained, unexplained rise raises the question of a lymphoproliferative disorder such as chronic lymphocytic leukaemia or lymphoma, and is followed up with a blood film and flow cytometry. They fall with corticosteroids and acute stress, which move lymphocytes out of the circulation into tissues, with an enlarged spleen that traps them, with HIV, influenza, viral hepatitis, COVID-19, tuberculosis and malaria, with lupus, Sjögren's syndrome and sarcoidosis, with chemotherapy, rituximab and radiotherapy, after major surgery or trauma, with heavy alcohol use and protein-poor diets, and in rare inherited immunodeficiencies. A low count on its own often causes no symptoms. A mildly low count in a well person is commonly just repeated, whereas a persistently or markedly abnormal count prompts a blood film and, where needed, flow cytometry to type the cells and look for infection, marrow disease or lymphoma.

    Why it may be higher

    • Viral infections (glandular fever, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis, many childhood viruses)
    • Whooping cough, tuberculosis, brucellosis
    • Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and lymphoma
    • Smoking; after removal of the spleen
    • Briefly after strenuous exercise, returning to baseline within about an hour
    • Drug hypersensitivity reactions, usually with fever, rash and enlarged lymph nodes
    • Underactive or absent spleen

    Why it may be lower

    • Corticosteroids and acute stress or illness
    • HIV infection
    • Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunosuppressants
    • Lupus, kidney failure, severe sepsis, malnutrition
    • Bone-marrow failure
    • Viral infections including influenza, viral hepatitis and COVID-19; tuberculosis and malaria
    • Sjögren's syndrome, sarcoidosis, Hodgkin lymphoma and aplastic anaemia; after bone-marrow or stem-cell transplantation
    • Rituximab and other antibody or antiviral therapies
    • Major surgery, trauma, heavy alcohol use and protein-poor diets
    • Older age, in which lower counts are common
    • Rare inherited immunodeficiencies (severe combined immunodeficiency, DiGeorge syndrome, Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome, ataxia telangiectasia, common variable immunodeficiency)

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Age: children run much higher counts
    • A recent viral illness can leave the count changed for weeks
    • Steroid dose timing
    • Whether the result is an absolute count or a percentage: a rise in neutrophils lowers the lymphocyte percentage without changing the number
    • Recent strenuous exercise or acute physical stress at the time of sampling
    Reference ranges. Reference intervals for this marker depend on the laboratory, the assay, the units, age, sex and pregnancy. Interpret a result against the interval printed on the laboratory report, not against a figure from the internet. Intervals are age-specific (children's counts run far higher than adults') and differ for absolute counts and percentages, so the two are not interchangeable. A mildly low count in someone who is well is usually monitored with a repeat full blood count rather than investigated; a persistently abnormal count is followed up with a blood film and, where indicated, flow cytometry.

    Conditions

    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • HIV test A blood test that looks for the human immunodeficiency virus.

    Biomarkers

    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.

    Guides and news

    • High white blood cell count: what it means A high white blood cell count is a non-specific sign, most often from infection, and doctors read it with the type of cell raised.
