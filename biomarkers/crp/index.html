    C-reactive protein (CRP)

    Also known as: CRP, C reactive protein, hs-CRP

    A protein the liver pours into blood within hours of any significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury. It rises and falls fast, so it tracks the course of an illness and the response to treatment; it does not say what the cause is.

    What it is and what it does

    CRP is an acute-phase protein: interleukin-6 released at a site of inflammation tells the liver to make it, levels begin to rise within about six hours, peak at around two days and fall with a half-life of roughly 19 hours once the trigger is removed. Bacterial infection, major surgery, trauma, heart attack, flares of inflammatory arthritis or bowel disease and some cancers all raise it, often into the hundreds of mg/L; many viral infections and some autoimmune diseases such as lupus raise it only modestly. A high-sensitivity assay (hs-CRP) measures the low-grade inflammation of obesity and atherosclerosis, used in some cardiovascular risk scores. CRP responds faster than the ESR, which is why it is preferred for monitoring acute illness. The protein takes its name from its reaction with the C-polysaccharide of the Streptococcus pneumoniae cell wall, the observation that led to its discovery in 1930; it circulates as a five-unit (pentameric) molecule that is largely anti-inflammatory, and at sites of inflammation dissociates into a monomeric form with pro-inflammatory effects on platelets and the vessel lining. Clinically it is a non-specific marker: the result says how much inflammation there is, not where it is or which organ is involved, so it is read with the history, examination and other tests. It is used to look for bacterial infection in someone with fever or chills, to watch for infection after surgery, to follow flares of rheumatoid arthritis, lupus or vasculitis, to judge whether an anti-inflammatory treatment is working, and as one input to decisions in suspected sepsis. Because its half-life is a matter of hours, a falling level is evidence that the process is resolving, and repeat measurement is more informative than a single value. The sample is an ordinary venous blood test with no preparation, though the laboratory may ask about supplements and anti-inflammatory drugs.

    Why it may be higher

    • Bacterial infection, sepsis, abscess, pneumonia, pyelonephritis
    • Surgery, trauma, burns, myocardial infarction
    • Inflammatory arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, vasculitis, gout flares
    • Cancer, particularly lymphoma and widespread disease
    • Mild elevation: obesity, smoking, diabetes, low-grade chronic inflammation
    • Low levels of physical activity and more sedentary time are associated with a higher baseline CRP
    • Late pregnancy, combined hormonal contraception and hormone replacement therapy (modest rises without disease)

    Why it may be lower

    • A low value is normal; it makes significant bacterial infection or major inflammation less likely but does not exclude early infection, viral illness or localised infection
    • Some autoimmune diseases, notably lupus and some rheumatoid arthritis, show little or no CRP rise despite active inflammation, so a low value does not exclude a flare

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Time since the illness began (a sample within the first hours can be normal)
    • Corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants blunt the rise
    • Obesity and smoking raise the baseline
    • Pregnancy and oestrogens raise it slightly
    • No fasting or other preparation is needed, but the laboratory may ask about supplements and about NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and aspirin
    • Which assay was used: a standard CRP and a high-sensitivity CRP measure different ranges and are reported against different reference points
    Reference ranges. Reference intervals for this marker depend on the laboratory, the assay, the units, age, sex and pregnancy. Interpret a result against the interval printed on the laboratory report, not against a figure from the internet. A standard CRP and a high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) are not interchangeable: hs-CRP detects very small rises used to estimate cardiovascular risk, and the risk bands quoted for it come from cardiology guideline bodies rather than from the laboratory's reference interval. Levels can be modestly raised in late pregnancy and with combined hormonal contraception or hormone replacement therapy without any disease, and in rheumatoid arthritis and lupus the CRP may stay low despite active disease.

    Conditions

    • Sepsis Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated body response to infection.
    • Pneumonia Infection of the alveoli, which fill with fluid and inflammatory cells so that part of the lung cannot exchange gas.
    • Rheumatoid arthritis A chronic autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks the synovial lining of joints.
    • Inflammatory bowel disease Chronic inflammation of the gut driven by an abnormal immune response: ulcerative colitis affects the colon's lining continuously from the rectum.
    • Appendicitis Acute inflammation of the appendix, usually because its opening into the caecum is blocked.
    • Gout An inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of uric acid crystals in joints, triggered when blood urate is persistently high.
    • Urinary tract infection Bacterial infection of the bladder (cystitis) or, when it ascends, the kidney (pyelonephritis), usually by gut bacteria such as Escherichia coli.

    Symptoms

    • Fever A body temperature of 38 °C (100.4 °F) or more, produced when the hypothalamus raises its set point in response to inflammatory signals.
    • Joint pain Pain in one or more joints.
    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Abdominal pain Pain between the ribs and the pelvis.

    Medical tests

    • CRP test A blood test for C-reactive protein, a protein the liver releases within hours of significant inflammation, infection or tissue injury.
    • ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate) An old but still useful test that measures how far red blood cells settle in a narrow tube in one hour.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Biomarkers

    • White blood cell count The total number of white blood cells in a volume of blood: the immune cells that fight infection and drive inflammation.
    • Neutrophils The most numerous white blood cell and the first responder to bacterial infection.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Albumin The most abundant protein in blood, made by the liver.

    Medications

    • Prednisolone and steroids A corticosteroid tablet that suppresses inflammation and the immune system, used in asthma flare-ups, arthritis.
    • Amoxicillin A widely used penicillin antibiotic that kills bacteria by breaking their cell walls, prescribed for chest, ear, sinus.
    • Azithromycin A macrolide antibiotic that stops bacteria making proteins, given as a short course (often three days) for chest, ear, sinus.

    Guides and news

    • High CRP: what it means and what happens next A high CRP means there is inflammation or infection somewhere in the body, but not where or why, so doctors read it with symptoms and other tests.
