    Haemoglobin

    Also known as: Hb, hemoglobin, haemoglobin concentration

    The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue. Its concentration is the standard measure of anaemia and the first number read on a full blood count.

    Open in the 3D explorerSpleen anatomy
    Also known as
    Hb, hemoglobin, haemoglobin concentration
    Units
    g/L in the UK, g/dL in the United States (1 g/dL = 10 g/L)
    Measured by
    Full blood count

    What it is and what it does

    Each red cell is packed with haemoglobin, a four-part protein whose iron atoms bind oxygen in the lung capillaries and release it where oxygen is low. The concentration in blood reflects the balance between production in the bone marrow (which needs iron, vitamin B12, folate and the kidney hormone erythropoietin) and loss or destruction of red cells. A low value is anaemia; the size of the red cells (MCV) then points to the cause.

    Why it may be higher

    • Living at altitude, smoking and chronic lung disease (the body makes more red cells when oxygen is scarce)
    • Dehydration, which concentrates the blood
    • Polycythaemia vera and other bone-marrow disorders
    • Testosterone and erythropoietin treatment

    Why it may be lower

    • Blood loss: heavy periods, bleeding from the gut, surgery or injury
    • Iron, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency
    • Chronic kidney disease (too little erythropoietin) and long-term inflammatory disease
    • Haemolysis, inherited haemoglobin disorders (thalassaemia, sickle cell disease), bone-marrow failure
    • Pregnancy, where plasma volume expands faster than red-cell mass

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Pregnancy and the menstrual cycle
    • Fluid balance at the time of sampling
    • Recent transfusion or blood donation
    • Sex, age and altitude of residence, which change the reference interval
    Reference ranges. Reference intervals for this marker depend on the laboratory, the assay, the units, age, sex and pregnancy. Interpret a result against the interval printed on the laboratory report, not against a figure from the internet.

    Conditions

    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Colorectal (bowel) cancer A malignant tumour of the colon or rectum, almost always arising from a benign polyp (adenoma) over years.
    • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) Progressive, largely irreversible airflow obstruction caused by damage to the airways (chronic bronchitis) and destruction of alveoli (emphysema).
    • Fibroids Benign (non-cancerous) growths of smooth muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the muscular wall of the uterus.
    • Ectopic pregnancy A pregnancy in which the fertilised egg implants and begins to grow outside the cavity of the uterus, most often in a fallopian tube.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.
    • Shortness of breath The sensation of not getting enough air.
    • Dizziness An umbrella word for several sensations: vertigo (the room spins, an inner-ear or brain problem), light-headedness (about to faint.
    • Heavy periods Menstrual bleeding that is heavier or lasts longer than usual and gets in the way of daily life.

    Medical tests

    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.
    • Iron studies A group of blood tests that together describe how much iron the body has stored.

    Biomarkers

    • Haematocrit The fraction of blood volume made up of red cells, reported as a percentage or a ratio.
    • Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) The average size of a red blood cell, calculated by the analyser during a full blood count.
    • Ferritin The protein that stores iron inside cells; the small amount in blood mirrors the body's iron reserve.
    • Bilirubin The yellow pigment made when old red cells are broken down, processed by the liver and excreted in bile.
    • HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin) Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it.
    • Platelets Small cell fragments that plug damaged vessel walls and start the clotting cascade.
    • Testosterone The principal androgen, made mainly by the testes and in smaller amounts by the ovaries and adrenal glands.

    Medications

    • Aspirin A low-dose antiplatelet that stops platelets clumping into clots, used long term after heart attacks and strokes.
    • Ibuprofen A non-steroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) that eases pain, inflammation and fever by blocking prostaglandin production.
    • Omeprazole A proton pump inhibitor that switches off acid production in the stomach.
