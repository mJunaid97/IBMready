    HbA1c (glycated haemoglobin)

    Also known as: glycated haemoglobin, A1c, haemoglobin A1c, glycosylated haemoglobin

    Haemoglobin that has glucose attached to it. Because red cells live about three months, the HbA1c fraction reports average blood glucose over the previous two to three months and is the marker used to diagnose and monitor type 2 diabetes.

    Open in the 3D explorerPancreas anatomy
    Also known as
    glycated haemoglobin, A1c, haemoglobin A1c, glycosylated haemoglobin
    Units
    mmol/mol (IFCC) or % (DCCT); 48 mmol/mol = 6.5%
    Measured by
    HbA1c, Blood glucose, Full blood count

    What it is and what it does

    Glucose in the blood attaches to haemoglobin slowly and irreversibly, so the higher the average glucose, the larger the share of haemoglobin that is glycated. Because the red cell population turns over every 120 days or so, HbA1c averages glucose exposure across that period, with the most recent month weighing most. It is reported in mmol/mol (the IFCC standard used in the UK) or as a percentage (the older DCCT units used in the United States). Unlike a single glucose reading it does not need fasting and is not disturbed by what was eaten that morning.

    Why it may be higher

    • Sustained high blood glucose: diabetes or prediabetes
    • Iron deficiency anaemia and other states with older red cells (falsely high)
    • Kidney failure, some haemoglobin variants (assay-dependent)

    Why it may be lower

    • Recent blood loss, haemolysis or transfusion (younger red cells: falsely low)
    • Pregnancy, erythropoietin treatment, some haemoglobin variants
    • Improving glucose control

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Any condition that shortens or lengthens red-cell survival changes the value independently of glucose
    • Haemoglobin variants can interfere with some assay methods
    • It cannot show day-to-day swings or hypoglycaemia
    Reference ranges. Reference intervals for this marker depend on the laboratory, the assay, the units, age, sex and pregnancy. Interpret a result against the interval printed on the laboratory report, not against a figure from the internet.

    Conditions

    • Type 2 diabetes A metabolic disease in which the body's cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance) and the pancreas gradually fails to compensate.
    • Type 1 diabetes An autoimmune disease in which the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas are destroyed.
    • Anaemia A haemoglobin concentration below normal (roughly under 130 g/L in men and 120 g/L in women), reducing the blood's capacity to carry oxygen.
    • Chronic kidney disease Persistent reduction in kidney function (a filtration rate below 60 ml/min) or evidence of kidney damage such as protein in the urine.
    • Polycystic ovary syndrome A common hormonal condition in which the ovaries make more androgens (hormones such as testosterone, which everyone makes.

    Symptoms

    • Fatigue Persistent tiredness that rest does not fix.

    Medical tests

    • HbA1c The fraction of haemoglobin that has glucose stuck to it.
    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose in blood, measured from a vein or a fingerprick.
    • Full blood count The most commonly ordered blood test: an automated count of the red cells, white cells and platelets in a small sample.

    Biomarkers

    • Blood glucose The concentration of glucose, the body's main circulating fuel, in blood at the moment of sampling.
    • Haemoglobin The iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to every tissue.

    Medications

    • Metformin The first-line tablet for type 2 diabetes: it reduces the glucose the liver releases and improves the body's response to insulin.
    • Insulin The hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells, given by injection or pump to everyone with type 1 diabetes and to many with type 2 when…
    • Atorvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking its production in the liver.
    • Rosuvastatin A high-intensity statin that lowers LDL cholesterol by more, milligram for milligram, than any other statin.

    Guides and news

    • HbA1c test: what it shows and why it matters The HbA1c test shows average blood glucose over the past two to three months and is used to diagnose and monitor diabetes.
