    Triglycerides

    Also known as: TG, triacylglycerol, serum triglycerides

    The main form in which fat is stored and transported: three fatty acids on a glycerol backbone, carried in blood by chylomicrons after meals and by VLDL from the liver. Raised levels add to cardiovascular risk and very high levels can trigger pancreatitis.

    Open in the 3D explorerLiver anatomy
    Also known as
    TG, triacylglycerol, serum triglycerides
    Units
    mmol/L (UK) or mg/dL (US); 1 mmol/L ≈ 88.5 mg/dL
    Measured by
    Lipid profile, Blood glucose

    What it is and what it does

    After a fatty meal the gut packages triglyceride into chylomicrons, which is why a non-fasting sample reads higher and why laboratories once insisted on fasting. Between meals the liver exports triglyceride in VLDL, particularly when it is over-supplied with calories, alcohol or sugar, or when insulin resistance lets fat stores release fatty acids freely. Moderately raised triglycerides usually travel with low HDL, central obesity and prediabetes; levels above about 10 mmol/L carry a real risk of acute pancreatitis. Because triglyceride-rich particles contribute to non-HDL cholesterol, the lipid profile as a whole rather than the single number guides treatment.

    Why it may be higher

    • A recent meal, alcohol, sugary and refined-carbohydrate-rich diets
    • Obesity, type 2 diabetes and insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome
    • Hypothyroidism, kidney disease, pregnancy
    • Medicines: corticosteroids, oestrogens, isotretinoin, some antipsychotics, thiazides, beta blockers
    • Inherited hypertriglyceridaemia (familial chylomicronaemia, familial combined hyperlipidaemia)

    Why it may be lower

    • Malnutrition, malabsorption, hyperthyroidism
    • Fibrates, omega-3 preparations, statins to a lesser degree, weight loss

    A high or low value can be associated with several different situations. The result is interpreted with other results, symptoms, history and clinical findings.

    What can affect the result

    • Fasting versus non-fasting sampling (up to a few hours' difference)
    • Alcohol in the preceding days
    • Acute illness and pregnancy
    Reference ranges. Reference intervals for this marker depend on the laboratory, the assay, the units, age, sex and pregnancy. Interpret a result against the interval printed on the laboratory report, not against a figure from the internet.
    Educational content. A biomarker page explains what a substance is and why its level can change. It cannot determine the cause of your result: interpret a result using the range supplied by the laboratory that performed the test, with the clinician who ordered it.
